10.1 Why a Portfolio? The Strategic Case for Multiple Patents

A single patent is a bet. A portfolio is a strategy. Ask any seasoned in-house counsel at a major technology company and they will tell you the same thing: the value of any individual patent is highly uncertain, but a well-constructed portfolio of fifty, five hundred, or fifty thousand patents creates durable competitive advantages that are nearly impossible to replicate quickly. This insight drives the patent arms race you see in the numbers below.

The Single Patent vs. Portfolio Analogy

Think of a single patent like a single stock pick. It might be brilliant — a foundational claim on a transformer architecture or a CRISPR gene-editing construct — but it is still one bet on one invention in one jurisdiction. The patent may be invalidated in inter partes review (IPR), designed around by a clever competitor, or simply become obsolete as technology evolves. Any of these outcomes leaves you with nothing.

A portfolio, by contrast, is more like a diversified fund. Individual patents fail; portfolios persist. A competitor who designs around your broad system claim runs into your narrower method claim. The company that invalidates your foundational patent discovers that ten continuation patents covering specific improvements remain fully valid. The country that refuses your broad software claim grants your narrower implementation claim. Redundancy and diversity are features, not inefficiencies.

★
The Three Functions of a Patent Portfolio

Defensive moat: A large portfolio deters competitors from suing you — they know you can countersue with your own patents, creating mutually assured destruction that keeps everyone at the negotiating table rather than in court. Revenue stream: Licensed portfolios generate royalty income. IBM's patent licensing program has historically generated over $1 billion per year. Competitive intelligence: Reading competitor patent filings gives you a 12-to-18-month preview of their R&D roadmap, before any product launches.

Defensive Moats

The concept of the patent defensive moat is central to big-technology IP strategy. When a company accumulates a large, technically diverse patent portfolio around its core products, it becomes both harder to attack (because any infringement suit triggers a counter-suit analysis) and more attractive as a licensing partner. This is why Google paid $12.5 billion for Motorola Mobility in 2012 — a deal that was widely understood at the time to be primarily a patent acquisition.

For AI companies, the defensive moat extends beyond direct competitors. A portfolio covering training methods, model architectures, inference optimization, and data preprocessing creates overlapping rings of protection. Even if a competitor successfully designs around your architecture patent, they still need to solve the training efficiency problem — and you have patents there too.

Licensing Revenue Streams

For companies whose primary business is not patent licensing, royalty income from a well-structured portfolio can be substantial. Qualcomm's entire business model rests on patent licensing. IBM, despite declining hardware revenues, has maintained a profitable patent licensing program for decades. In the AI space, companies like Interdigital and Acacia Research have built businesses around licensing foundational patents in connectivity and signal processing that AI systems depend upon.

For operating companies — companies that actually make products — patent licensing serves a different but complementary function: it becomes a tool for resolving competitor disputes without costly litigation. When two large technology companies are in dispute, a cross-license is often the outcome. The company with the larger, higher-quality portfolio negotiates from a position of strength.

The Portfolio Size vs. Innovation Quality Debate

Not everyone agrees that more patents is always better. Critics of the patent arms race point out that many large portfolio patents are low-quality — filed not because the invention is important but because the company's patent bonus program rewards quantity. Research by Lemley and Shapiro has shown that the average patent has a relatively low probability of being valid if litigated. The patents that actually get enforced tend to be the narrow slice of high-quality foundational claims at the top of each technology tree.

⚠
Quality vs. Quantity Trap

A thousand weak patents do not equal one strong one. Filing low-quality applications wastes prosecution budget, bloats maintenance fee obligations, and creates prosecution history estoppel that can actually narrow the scope of your better patents. The goal is a portfolio of meaningful claim scope, not a raw count of issued patents. Most patent counsel experienced in portfolio management will tell you: 20 high-quality patents are worth more than 200 mediocre ones.

The best-performing portfolios combine both breadth (many patents covering many aspects of the technology) and depth (foundational claims that competitors cannot easily design around). Building this kind of portfolio requires a deliberate strategy — not just filing applications on every invention as it appears, but thinking several years in advance about where the technology is going and what claim scope will matter when it gets there.

10.2 The Patent Family

A patent family is the collection of related applications that spring from a single original filing. Understanding how to build and manage a patent family is one of the most important skills in portfolio strategy. The different members of a family serve distinct strategic purposes, and the decision of when to file each type has significant consequences for claim scope, prosecution history, and competitive positioning.

Figure 10.1 — Illustrative patent family structure for an AI system patent, showing how a single parent application can branch into continuations, CIP, divisional, and international PCT filings.

Continuation Applications

A continuation application (CON) allows you to pursue additional claims based on the same specification as a parent application, as long as the parent is still pending. The continuation shares the parent's priority date and discloses the same invention — you cannot add new matter. What you can do is draft entirely different claims: broader, narrower, or focused on different aspects of the disclosed invention.

The strategic power of continuations is enormous. A skilled practitioner files the parent application with broad, system-level claims but also deliberately leaves material in the specification that can support narrower, more specific claims in continuations. When competitors design around the broad claims, those continuation claims — which are still pending and can be tailored to cover the competitor's specific implementation — spring into position. This is claim laddering in action, covered in detail in Section 10.3.

ℹ
Key Rule: Continuation Must Be Filed While Parent Is Pending

A continuation must be filed before the parent application issues as a patent or is abandoned. Once the parent issues, it is too late to file a continuation. This rule creates enormous urgency at the moment of allowance: before paying the issue fee, patent counsel should evaluate whether a continuation should be filed. Many companies maintain a standing policy of filing at least one continuation before every parent application issues.

Continuation-in-Part (CIP) Applications

A continuation-in-part (CIP) is a hybrid: it contains all the disclosure from the parent plus new matter. Claims that rely only on the parent disclosure get the parent's priority date. Claims that require the new matter get only the CIP's own filing date as their priority date.

CIPs are particularly useful in fast-moving fields like AI, where a research team may improve upon their initial invention while the original application is still pending. Rather than filing a completely new application (and losing the parent's priority date for the original concepts), they file a CIP that covers both the original and improved inventions in a single prosecution effort.

⚠
CIP Trap: Split Priority Dates

The split priority date in a CIP creates hidden vulnerability. Claims covering new matter have a later priority date, which means that anything published between the parent filing date and the CIP filing date is potentially prior art against those claims. In AI, where preprint servers publish daily, an 18-month gap between a parent filing and a CIP filing can expose new matter claims to substantial prior art. Always analyze the prior art landscape carefully before choosing CIP over a standalone new application.

Divisional Applications

When a restriction requirement issues — the USPTO's way of saying your application covers more than one distinct invention — you must elect one invention to prosecute in the original application. A divisional application allows you to pursue the non-elected inventions in separate applications, while retaining the parent's priority date.

Experienced patent counsel treat restriction requirements strategically. Rather than fighting the restriction (which rarely succeeds and wastes prosecution time), they use it as an opportunity to build out the patent family. Each restricted species becomes a separate divisional, creating a natural mechanism for portfolio expansion at no extra invention cost.

Foreign Counterparts and PCT Applications

A PCT application preserves your right to enter national phase in over 150 countries, with a single international filing. Within 30 months of the priority date (12 months from the PCT filing, in most cases), you choose which national phase entries to pursue. Chapter 11 covers PCT strategy in depth. For portfolio purposes, the key point is that foreign counterparts are essential members of a well-structured patent family, particularly for AI companies with global markets.

Prosecution History Considerations Across the Family

Every statement made during prosecution of any family member can be used against you in the others. This is the prosecution history estoppel principle extended across a patent family. If you narrow a claim in the parent application to distinguish a reference, that narrowing argument can constrain the scope of continuation claims even if the continuation uses broader language. Prosecutors must maintain consistent, coordinated claim strategies across the entire family.

10.3 Claim Laddering Strategy

Claim laddering is the art of building a progression of claims — from the broadest possible articulation of an invention down to extremely specific implementations — spread across multiple patents in a family. The analogy is a ladder: the broad claims at the top cover the widest possible scope; the narrow claims at the bottom are the hardest to design around because they track specific technical details that competitors are likely to implement.

Why spread claims across multiple patents rather than including all levels in a single application? Several reasons. First, independent claims in a single application are often argued against each other — if you assert both a broad claim and a narrow claim, the examiner may use the narrow claim as evidence of what "the invention" actually is, limiting the broad claim. Second, separate patents for different claim levels allow you to pursue prosecution strategies tailored to each level's prior art landscape. Third, separate patents create separate enforcement opportunities and licensing leverage.

The Transformer Architecture Ladder — A Worked Example

The following example demonstrates claim laddering using a fictional transformer-based AI system patent family with three continuation applications, each narrower than the previous but each creating a distinct enforcement position.

Parent Application — Broad Foundational Claims

US Patent 11,234,567 — "Neural Network System for Sequence-to-Sequence Processing"

The broadest system and method claims cover the general concept of attention-based processing across arbitrary sequence data. Resistant to design-around only if the prior art truly didn't disclose attention mechanisms at this level.

Representative Independent Claim (Broad)

1. A computer-implemented method for processing sequence data, the method comprising:

receiving an input sequence comprising a plurality of tokens; computing, for each token, an attention score based on relationships between said token and at least one other token in the input sequence; generating a weighted representation of the input sequence based on the computed attention scores; and producing an output sequence using the weighted representation.
Continuation 1 — Medium Specificity Claims

US Patent 11,456,789 — "Multi-Head Self-Attention for Natural Language Processing"

Narrows to multi-head attention specifically, with explicit language about parallel attention heads and learned projection matrices. Covers the core transformer architecture as implemented in practice by GPT-style and BERT-style systems.

Representative Independent Claim (Medium)

1. A system for processing natural language input, comprising:

a multi-head attention module configured to process an input token sequence using a plurality of attention heads operating in parallel, each attention head computing query, key, and value projections using learned weight matrices; a feedforward sublayer configured to apply position-wise transformations to the output of the multi-head attention module; and a position encoding module configured to inject positional information into the input token embeddings prior to attention processing.
Continuation 2 — Narrow Defensible Claims

US Patent 11,678,901 — "Scaled Dot-Product Attention with Temperature Scaling"

Narrows to the specific mathematical operations and the temperature scaling trick used in practice to stabilize training. Highly specific but nearly impossible to design around without degrading model quality — competitors who implement state-of-the-art transformers will almost certainly fall within this claim.

Representative Independent Claim (Narrow)

1. A method for computing scaled dot-product attention in a neural network, comprising:

computing attention logits as a dot product between a query matrix Q and a transposed key matrix K, scaled by the inverse square root of the key dimension dk; applying a softmax function to the scaled attention logits to produce an attention weight distribution; and computing an attended output as a weighted sum of a value matrix V using the attention weight distribution, wherein the scaling by 1/√dk reduces gradient magnitude variance during training.
📝
Strategic Insight: The Unavoidable Narrow Claim

The most powerful claim ladder positions narrow claims that cover implementation details that state-of-the-art practitioners will invariably adopt for performance reasons. In the transformer example above, the temperature scaling by 1/√dk is mathematically motivated — it keeps the dot products in a range where the softmax function has useful gradients. Any competitive implementation of multi-head attention will use this or a functionally equivalent scaling. A narrow claim that covers this specific, technically motivated detail is substantially harder to design around than a broad functional claim.

Protecting Against Design-Arounds

Every broad claim is vulnerable to a design-around. A sophisticated competitor will analyze your patent, identify the elements of your claims, and modify their implementation to avoid at least one element. The defense against design-arounds is having multiple claim levels in the ladder, such that the design-around of the broad claim falls squarely within the medium claim, and the design-around of the medium claim falls within the narrow claim.

For this to work, the specification — from the very first filing — must contain sufficient disclosure to support all levels of the claim ladder. This means that the original specification must be written with portfolio strategy in mind, disclosing not just the preferred embodiment but also alternative embodiments, specific mechanisms, and the technical reasons why particular implementations are preferred. That technical detail becomes the ammunition for future continuation claims.

"The specification is the portfolio's foundation. A thin specification supports only thin claims. Write the specification assuming you'll be prosecuting continuations for the next fifteen years — because if the invention is important, you will be." — Senior Patent Counsel, Major AI Company (anonymous)

10.4 The AI Patent Portfolio Blueprint

Building a comprehensive AI patent portfolio requires thinking across multiple technological layers simultaneously. A company whose portfolio covers only model architectures is exposed to competitors who improve the training process, the data pipeline, or the deployment infrastructure. A complete AI portfolio blueprint addresses all six layers of the AI technology stack.

🏝

Core Architecture Patents

Foundational model architecture inventions: novel attention mechanisms, network topologies, layer configurations, activation functions. These are the hardest to obtain (heavy prior art) and the most valuable if obtained. File broadly, prosecute aggressively, build continuations from day one.

📈

Training Method Patents

Novel training procedures: loss functions, regularization techniques, curriculum learning strategies, federated learning protocols, RLHF variants, preference optimization methods. Often easier to obtain than architecture patents and frequently required for competitive performance.

🔄

Data Pipeline Patents

Data collection, preprocessing, augmentation, and curation methods. Often undervalued by AI teams but critically important — companies that develop superior data pipelines achieve model quality advantages that competitors struggle to replicate even with the same architecture.

🎱

Application-Specific Patents

Domain-specific applications of AI methods: medical imaging diagnostics, natural language contract analysis, autonomous vehicle perception. These patents are often easier to get past eligibility challenges because they tie the AI method to a specific technical result in a specific domain.

👔

User Interface Patents

Novel UI/UX inventions for AI-powered products: prompt engineering interfaces, model output visualization, human-in-the-loop feedback systems. Often overlooked by technical teams but valuable for protecting the product experience that drives customer adoption.

⚙

System Integration Patents

Infrastructure for deploying AI at scale: model serving architectures, inference optimization, hardware-software co-design, multimodal system integration, API protocols for AI services. Protects the operational know-how that turns a research model into a production product.

Portfolio Balance Guideline

For an AI company with a significant product, a balanced portfolio typically allocates roughly 30–40% of patent budget to core architecture and training method patents (high value, harder to get), 30–40% to application-specific and system integration patents (easier to get, strong for enforcement), and 20–30% to maintaining continuation chains from foundational filings. Data pipeline and UI patents fill the remainder. Adjust this allocation based on where your specific competitive threats actually come from.

10.5 Patent Mining from Research

For most technology companies, the biggest bottleneck in portfolio building is not money or legal capacity — it is the identification of patentable inventions from the stream of research output that the engineering and science teams produce daily. Researchers are not trained to think in terms of patent claims; they think in terms of papers, experiments, and benchmarks. The patent mining process is the bridge between research culture and IP strategy.

How to Identify Patentable Inventions

Not every research result is a patentable invention. The filter is novelty, non-obviousness, and — for AI and biotech — patent eligibility. But within that filter, the candidates are wider than most researchers expect. The following categories of AI research output frequently give rise to patentable inventions:

  • Novel architectures: Any new neural network architecture, attention variant, or layer configuration that achieves better performance or efficiency than prior work.
  • Training innovations: New loss functions, optimizers, regularization techniques, learning rate schedules, or training curricula that improve performance or reduce training cost.
  • Data processing methods: Novel approaches to data collection, labeling, augmentation, or filtering that enable better model training.
  • Inference optimizations: Quantization methods, pruning techniques, distillation procedures, and speculative decoding approaches that reduce deployment cost.
  • Application-specific adaptations: Novel applications of general AI methods to specific domains, particularly where the adaptation requires non-obvious domain-specific insights.
  • Evaluation methods: Novel benchmarks or evaluation methodologies, particularly if they require specific technical implementations.

The Innovation Identification Workshop

The most effective mechanism for systematically identifying patentable inventions is the innovation identification workshop — a structured, regular meeting between patent counsel and research/engineering teams. The format typically involves researchers presenting recent work, with patent counsel actively probing for patentable elements that the researcher may have overlooked.

  1. Pre-Meeting Preparation

    Patent counsel reviews recent research papers, internal presentations, and code check-ins. Identifies candidate inventions before the meeting. Researchers complete a one-page invention disclosure form for each candidate.

  2. Technical Presentation

    Researcher presents the work informally — not as a legal document but as a technical explanation. Patent counsel listens for novel elements, unexpected results, and technical choices that were non-obvious.

  3. Probing Questions

    Patent counsel asks: "What would happen if you didn't do X?" "Did prior systems try Y?" "What was surprising about this result?" "Why this particular parameter choice?" These questions often reveal patentable elements the researcher didn't flag.

  4. Patent Eligibility Screen

    Quick first-pass § 101 analysis. Does the invention have a specific technical character? Is it tied to a particular machine? Does it improve the technology itself? If the answer to all three is "no," it may be a better candidate for defensive publication than patent filing.

  5. Filing Decision and Priority Assignment

    Each identified invention is scored on patentability, business value, and enforcement feasibility. High-priority inventions go directly to provisional or non-provisional filing. Lower-priority inventions may be defensively published.

When to File vs. When to Publish

The decision to file a patent application versus publish a research paper is one of the most consequential in AI IP strategy. Publication destroys your own patent rights in most jurisdictions outside the U.S. — and even in the U.S., post-AIA grace period rights are limited and subject to erosion. The fundamental rule: file before you publish.

The Publish-Then-File Strategy (12-Month Grace Period)

Under the AIA, inventors have a 12-month grace period after their own public disclosure to file a U.S. patent application. This grace period creates the publish-then-file strategy: publish the paper to establish prior art (blocking competitors from patenting the same idea), then file within 12 months to secure U.S. patent rights.

This strategy is common in academic and open-source AI research. The publication creates a defensive publication that blocks competitors, and the subsequent patent application captures the commercial value. The risk: the publication starts a 12-month countdown that requires careful calendar management, and the grace period is U.S.-only — publication typically destroys patent rights in Europe, Japan, China, and other major jurisdictions unless a patent was filed before the publication date.

⚠
Critical Limitation: Grace Period is U.S.-Only

The 12-month grace period under 35 U.S.C. § 102(b)(1) only applies in the United States. If you publish before filing, you permanently lose the ability to obtain patent protection in Europe (EPO), most of Asia (including China, Japan, Korea), and most other major jurisdictions. For AI companies with global markets, publishing without first filing a PCT application (or at least a U.S. provisional) is a serious strategic error.

Coordinating Research and IP Teams

The most effective AI patent programs embed IP awareness into the research culture itself. Researchers should understand, at a minimum: (1) what types of innovations are patentable; (2) why publication timing matters; (3) how to submit an invention disclosure; and (4) what happens after they submit one. Companies that treat IP education as a one-time orientation exercise rather than an ongoing cultural priority consistently underfile relative to their actual innovation output.

Best practices include: designating IP liaisons within each research team, incorporating invention disclosure deadlines into the paper submission process, creating financial incentives for researchers to identify patentable inventions (inventor compensation programs), and making the patent submission process as frictionless as possible — a researcher who has to fill out a 20-page form before a filing decision will find reasons not to.

10.6 Competitive Intelligence Using Patents

Patent filings are the best publicly available source of competitive intelligence in technology. A patent application filed today reveals the inventor's technical thinking from 12 to 18 months ago — and because applications are published 18 months after filing, you get a structured, documented view of what your competitors were working on before any product announcement. Building a systematic patent monitoring program is one of the highest-ROI activities in corporate IP strategy.

Monitoring Competitor Filings

The basic infrastructure for competitor patent monitoring involves setting up automated alerts for new filings by competitor companies (by assignee name), in relevant technology classifications (CPC codes for AI include G06N 3/00 series for neural networks), and by relevant inventor names (top researchers who move between companies often signal technology direction before any public announcement).

Reading Competitor Applications to Predict Product Roadmaps

A patent application tells you several things about its assignee's technical direction. The claims indicate what the company considers commercially valuable. The specification reveals the technical approach in detail. The inventor list shows who is working on the problem. The referenced prior art shows what the inventors consider the state of the art and how they position their contribution relative to it.

Sophisticated competitive intelligence analysts build technology maps by clustering related patent filings into technology themes, tracking the velocity of filings within each theme over time, and correlating patent activity with product announcements and research publications. A sudden surge in filings in a particular CPC subclass — say, G06N 3/08 (backpropagation methods) or G06N 20/00 (machine learning) — can signal a major development push before any public announcement.

Patent Landscaping for AI Domains

Patent landscaping is the systematic analysis of all patents in a given technology domain. A well-executed landscape answers: Who are the major patent holders? What technologies are most heavily patented? Where are the white spaces — areas of technical importance with relatively sparse patent coverage? What is the trajectory of filings over time, and what does it imply about where the technology is heading?

Freedom-to-Operate Implications

Competitive intelligence feeds directly into freedom-to-operate (FTO) analysis. Before launching a new AI product, a thorough FTO review must map the product's features against the patents identified in the competitive landscape. FTO opinions are not just for legal protection — they are strategic documents that inform product design decisions. If a particular feature is found to infringe a competitor's valid patent, the product team can redesign before launch rather than litigating after.

FTO analysis for AI products is particularly complex because a single AI system may invoke hundreds of patented techniques — architectures, training methods, tokenization approaches, inference optimizations. The standard approach is to decompose the product into its constituent technical components and run targeted searches for each component, prioritizing those where the company has largest patent exposure risk.

10.7 Defensive Publications

Not every invention that reaches the patent review committee should be patented. Sometimes, the best move is a defensive publication — a deliberate public disclosure of an invention designed to prevent competitors from patenting the same idea, without incurring the cost and complexity of a patent application.

When to Publish Instead of Patent

Defensive publication makes sense in several scenarios. First, when the invention is not patentable but is novel and useful — if you know it fails a § 101 analysis but a competitor might try to patent it anyway, publishing prevents that. Second, when the enforcement cost exceeds the licensing value — a narrow incremental improvement with limited commercial impact may not justify $20,000–$50,000 in prosecution costs plus ongoing maintenance fees. Third, when open dissemination supports business strategy — many AI companies compete on execution speed and data advantages, not IP, and publishing freely advances their ecosystem goals.

Fourth — and most strategically — when a patent application would result in a prosecution history that narrows the scope of related patents in the family, defensive publication may be preferable. If the claim scope you can obtain for an incremental improvement is narrower than what the competitor's design-around would already avoid, paying $30,000 in prosecution costs to get a patent with no enforcement value is purely wasteful.

The Structured Defensive Publication

A defensive publication is most effective when it is written to anticipate the claim language a competitor might use. This means including specific technical details, parameter ranges, method steps, and examples that would be recited in patent claims — not just a general description. A generic "we built a neural network that does X" publication will not block a competitor from patenting "a neural network that does X using the specific architecture Y with parameter range Z." Write your defensive publications with patent claim structure in mind.

10.8 The Patent Review Committee

A portfolio doesn't build itself. The organizational infrastructure that separates companies with strategic patent portfolios from those with ad hoc collections is the Patent Review Committee (PRC) — a standing cross-functional body that systematically evaluates every potential patent and makes informed filing, publication, and abandonment decisions.

Committee Composition

Chief Technology Officer (or VP Engineering)

  • Provides technical context and future roadmap perspective
  • Assesses commercial importance of technical inventions
  • Ensures alignment between IP strategy and R&D direction

Patent Counsel (Internal or External)

  • Assesses patentability and likely claim scope
  • Evaluates prosecution risk and cost estimates
  • Manages portfolio coordination and family strategy

Research/Engineering Leads

  • Presents technical disclosures
  • Identifies potential prior art from literature
  • Clarifies technical details for claim drafting

Product Management

  • Identifies product features most in need of protection
  • Flags competitive threats driving FTO concerns
  • Connects patent decisions to product roadmap

The Patent Disclosure Pipeline

The pipeline from invention identification to filing decision typically flows through five stages:

  1. Invention Disclosure Submission

    Inventor submits a one-to-three-page invention disclosure describing what is new, why it is different from prior work, what technical problem it solves, and how it is commercially relevant. This is not a legal document — it is a technical document.

  2. Initial Patentability Screening

    Patent counsel performs a quick (1–2 hour) prior art search to assess whether the invention is likely novel. Also checks against the company's existing portfolio to identify continuation opportunities versus new application opportunities.

  3. Patent Review Committee Meeting

    The committee meets monthly (weekly for high-velocity companies) to review screened disclosures. Each disclosure receives a patentability score, business value score, and enforcement feasibility score.

  4. Filing Decision

    Four outcomes: (A) File non-provisional immediately (high priority), (B) File provisional to preserve rights while gathering more information, (C) Defensively publish to block competitors, (D) Abandon — insufficient novelty or business value to justify any action.

  5. Prosecution and Portfolio Integration

    Once the filing decision is made, the application is prepared, filed, and integrated into the overall portfolio strategy. The patent is tracked through prosecution, with continuation decisions made at each key prosecution milestone.

Budget Allocation Framework

How much should a company spend on patents? There is no universal answer, but useful benchmarks exist. Large technology companies like Google and Microsoft spend roughly 1–2% of revenue on IP (including litigation). For a $10 billion revenue company, that is $100–$200 million annually. For early-stage startups, a common rule of thumb is $200,000–$500,000 per year for a meaningful foundational portfolio, covering 5–15 new filings per year plus prosecution costs on pending applications.

A simple budget allocation framework for a mid-size AI company (~$100M ARR):

  • New filings (provisionals + nonprovisionals): 40% of IP budget
  • Prosecution costs (responding to office actions, RCEs): 25%
  • Continuation strategy: 15%
  • Foreign prosecution (PCT, national phase): 15%
  • Maintenance fees + portfolio management: 5%

10.9 Portfolio Valuation and Analytics

Patent portfolios appear on balance sheets as intangible assets, but their actual value is frequently misunderstood — both understated and overstated. Building the analytical tools to assess portfolio value accurately is essential for strategic decision-making: which patents to maintain, which to license, which to abandon, and which merit litigation investment.

Individual patent quality is measured along several dimensions. No single metric tells the whole story, but a combination provides a useful signal.

DCF Valuation for Patent Portfolios

The theoretically correct method for valuing a patent portfolio is discounted cash flow (DCF) analysis. The model projects future royalty income or cost savings attributable to the patent portfolio, discounts those cash flows at a risk-adjusted rate (typically 15–25% for early-stage technology IP), and sums to a net present value.

In practice, DCF patent valuation is difficult because future royalty streams are highly uncertain, especially for early-stage portfolios that have not yet been licensed. Most patent transactions use comparable transactions analysis as a sanity check: what have similar portfolios sold or licensed for recently? For AI portfolios, per-patent prices in recent transactions have ranged from $500,000 to $5 million for high-quality foundational patents, with most commercial transactions in the $50,000–$500,000 per patent range for technology-specific portfolios.

Royalty Rates for AI Patents

When licensing AI patents, the royalty rate discussion begins with industry benchmarks. These benchmarks vary significantly by technology domain, application field, and license structure.

AI Technology Domain Typical Royalty Rate Common License Structure Notes
Foundation model architectures 1–5% of net sales Running royalty on inference revenue Few precedents; often settled as lump sum
NLP applications (enterprise) 2–8% of net sales Per-query or per-user revenue share Comparable to software patent benchmarks
Computer vision (consumer) 1–4% of unit price Per-unit device license Large volume; rates compressed
Medical AI diagnostics 3–10% of net sales Running royalty + upfront milestone Regulatory hurdles reduce comparables

⚠
Patent Royalty Stacking Risk in AI

A complex AI product may potentially infringe patents held by dozens of companies — semiconductor patents, training method patents, architecture patents, application patents, and UI patents all potentially apply to a single model deployment. If each patent holder demands a royalty rate based on the value of the entire product, the cumulative royalties quickly exceed the product's profit margin. This royalty stacking problem is particularly acute in AI and is a key argument in Georgia-Pacific factor 9 (the benefit of the patented invention to licensees) in any reasonable royalty analysis.

10.10 Building a Startup Patent Portfolio

The strategic considerations for a well-funded Big Tech patent program are not the same as those for a startup operating on a Series A budget with a six-month runway. Startups face specific constraints — limited budget, urgent competitive threats, and the need to demonstrate IP value to investors — that require a tailored approach to portfolio building.

The "Patent Pending" Value for Fundraising

For pre-revenue startups, the primary near-term value of a patent portfolio is not licensing revenue but fundraising leverage. Investors — particularly at Series A and B — view patent filings as signals of technology differentiation and long-term defensibility. A startup that can say "we have three issued patents and eight pending applications covering our core technology" tells a different story than one that says "we have no IP protection yet." The former suggests founders who think strategically about competitive moats; the latter raises questions about defensibility.

The most efficient use of limited startup IP budget is to file provisional applications on every significant invention — even before a full non-provisional is financially feasible — to establish priority dates and generate "patent pending" status. A provisional application costs $2,000–$8,000 in attorney fees (compared to $15,000–$35,000 for a non-provisional) and buys 12 months to decide whether to convert to a full application.

Provisional Applications as Budget Strategy

The provisional strategy is: file provisionals broadly and cheaply to lock in priority dates; convert only the most commercially valuable inventions to full non-provisionals within 12 months. Provisionals need not be as precisely drafted as non-provisionals (they do not require formal claims), but they must contain sufficient disclosure to support the claims you plan to file later. A poorly drafted provisional that fails to disclose the key technical details of the final invention provides little priority date benefit.

★
Provisional Application Best Practice

Draft your provisional application as if it were a non-provisional without claims. Include: a detailed description of the problem being solved, the specific technical approach (with enough detail to enable a skilled person to reproduce it), multiple embodiments and variants, examples with experimental data if available, and a section describing what distinguishes this from prior approaches. When you convert to non-provisional, this specification becomes the foundation for claim drafting. A well-drafted provisional protects not just your priority date but your ability to draft broad continuation claims years later.

Patent Pledge Programs

Several patent pledge frameworks offer startups a way to build goodwill with the developer community while still maintaining defensive IP rights. Understanding these programs helps startups decide whether to participate — and whether potential investors or partners have made commitments that affect the company's IP strategy.

Open Invention Network (OIN)

The world's largest patent non-aggression community, specifically protecting the Linux ecosystem. Members commit not to assert patents against Linux or core open-source software. Membership is free and grants access to a substantial defensive portfolio. Over 3,700 members including Google, IBM, Microsoft, and most major AI hardware companies.

LOT Network

License on Transfer (LOT) agreement: members grant each other a patent license that activates only if a patent is transferred to a patent assertion entity (PAE/NPE). Protects against PAE litigation. Free to join; particularly useful for startups worried about portfolio transactions that put formerly-friendly patents in hostile hands.

Defensive Patent License (DPL)

A reciprocal pledge framework for open-source and open-technology communities. DPL members grant each other royalty-free licenses to all patents, conditioned on the licensee also pledging their portfolio to DPL. Smaller community than OIN but aligned with open-source values.

Tesla's Patent Pledge

Tesla committed in 2014 not to initiate patent suits against parties who use Tesla's patents in good faith. A unilateral non-aggression pledge used as a PR and ecosystem-building tool. Several AI companies have made similar pledges for specific technology domains to signal open collaboration.

Suggested 3-Year IP Roadmap for a Startup

Year 1 — Months 1–6: Foundation

Establish Priority Dates for Core Inventions

File provisional applications on all significant inventions before any public disclosure, investor demo, or conference presentation. Budget: $15,000–$30,000. Target: 3–6 provisional applications covering the core technical approach, key novel features, and at least one application-specific implementation.

Year 1 — Months 7–12: First Non-Provisionals

Convert Top-Priority Provisionals

Convert the 1–2 most important provisionals to non-provisional applications. Consider PCT filing to preserve international rights if the product has a global market. Budget: $30,000–$60,000. File continuation on the first provisional before it expires to keep the family alive while the non-provisional is examined.

Year 2 — Months 13–18: Portfolio Expansion

File Continuation Applications and New Inventions

File continuation applications on issued/pending non-provisionals, targeting medium-specificity claims that cover specific product features. File new provisionals and non-provisionals on inventions from the second wave of R&D. Implement innovation identification workshop quarterly. Budget: $40,000–$80,000.

Year 2 — Months 19–24: International Expansion

National Phase Entry for Top Patents

Enter national phase in the 3–5 most commercially important jurisdictions (EP, CN, JP at minimum for global AI products; add KR and CA depending on markets). Budget: $50,000–$100,000 for 2–3 patents in 3 jurisdictions. Prioritize patents most likely to be enforced against competitors with operations in those countries.

Year 3 — Months 25–36: Strategic Depth

Narrow Claims, Application Patents, and First Issuances

File narrower continuation applications targeting design-around scenarios identified through competitive intelligence. File application-specific patents on the top 2–3 product use cases. Expect first patents to issue from the Year 1 provisionals (USPTO examination typically takes 2–4 years). Celebrate the first issuance — it is a milestone for future fundraising. Budget: $60,000–$100,000.

Startup IP Budget Summary (3-Year Plan)

Total estimated investment over three years: $195,000–$370,000, producing approximately 8–15 pending/issued U.S. patent applications and 4–8 international counterparts in key jurisdictions. This portfolio, if the underlying technology proves valuable, provides meaningful defensive and fundraising value. It will not equal the portfolios of Google or IBM, but it is sufficient to (1) deter many potential copycat competitors, (2) support Series B+ fundraising narratives, and (3) create leverage in potential M&A discussions.

10.11 Portfolio Pruning and Maintenance

A patent portfolio is not a one-way accumulation machine. Patents have ongoing maintenance costs, and as technology evolves, patents that were once commercially relevant become worthless. Systematic portfolio pruning — regularly evaluating which patents to maintain, which to let lapse, and which to license or sell — is essential to maintaining an efficient, cost-effective portfolio.

Maintenance Fee Schedule

U.S. utility patents require maintenance fees at three intervals after issuance. Failure to pay any maintenance fee results in automatic lapse of the patent. The USPTO provides a 6-month grace period after each due date, but late fees apply.

First Maintenance 3.5 years (3 years + 6 months window) $800 $2,000 +$160 / +$400
Second Maintenance 7.5 years (7 years + 6 months window) $1,800 $3,760 +$160 / +$400
Third Maintenance 11.5 years (11 years + 6 months window) $3,700 $7,700 +$160 / +$400

ℹ
Cumulative Maintenance Cost Over Patent Life

A large-entity patent held to expiration (20 years from filing) incurs roughly $13,460 in maintenance fees alone, not counting prosecution costs ($15,000–$50,000 to obtain), foreign filing costs, or attorney oversight fees for each maintenance period. For a portfolio of 100 active patents, that is $1.35 million in maintenance fees over the life of those patents. This is why pruning matters: a patent that generates no licensing revenue and creates no defensive value should be allowed to lapse.

The Portfolio Audit Process

1

Inventory & Classify

Catalog all active patents by technology area, claim type, and product relevance. Update the classification annually as products evolve.

2

Quality Score

Apply the quality metrics from Section 10.9: forward citations, claim breadth, family size, remaining term, and current commercial relevance.

3

Commercial Review

For each patent, ask: Is it practiced by the company's current products? Is it practiced by known competitors? Has it generated any licensing revenue?

4

Cost-Benefit Decision

Compare ongoing cost (maintenance fees + attorney oversight) to estimated value. Patents with no commercial relevance and declining citation rates are prime abandonment candidates.

5

Action

Options: maintain, actively license, sell to patent broker, donate to defensive pledge program, or allow to lapse at next maintenance fee date.

When to Abandon

The decision to abandon (stop paying maintenance fees and let a patent lapse) should be made deliberately, not by default. Before abandoning, consider: (1) Can the patent be sold? A patent with no value to your company may have significant value to a competitor or a licensing entity. Patent brokers like IPValue and Acacia can provide quick market assessments. (2) Can the patent be donated to a defensive pledge program? The Open Invention Network and LOT Network accept patent contributions that can offset licensing costs elsewhere in your portfolio. (3) Is the technology actually obsolete, or has the product simply changed? AI patents covering training methods for architectures that are no longer in production may still cover the methods used in deployed models that continue to operate.

Licensing as an Alternative to Abandonment

Before abandoning any patent with remaining term, evaluate whether a licensing campaign would recover more than the ongoing maintenance cost. Even a modest one-time license fee of $50,000 from a single licensee substantially exceeds the maintenance fee for an individual patent. Patent licensing requires finding willing licensees — which requires understanding who is actually practicing the patented technology — but a focused effort on a small number of high-quality patents that are being widely practiced can generate substantial returns with relatively modest investment.

10.12 Portfolio Strategy Checklist

The following checklist covers the key strategic decisions every AI company should address when building and managing a patent portfolio. Use this as an annual audit tool, not just an initial planning guide — portfolio strategy must evolve as the technology and competitive landscape evolve.

A. Foundation Strategy

  • Have we identified and filed patents on all foundational technology innovations (architecture, training methods, data pipeline)?
  • Does every significant invention have a priority date established before any public disclosure (conference, paper, demo, open-source release)?
  • Have we written specifications broad enough to support continuation claim laddering for the next 10–15 years?
  • Have we established a formal invention disclosure process that researchers actually use?
  • Do we hold at least quarterly innovation identification workshops with technical teams?
  • Are key researchers trained on what is patentable and why publication timing matters?

B. Family and Continuation Strategy

  • Have we filed at least one continuation before each parent application issued?
  • Do our continuation applications cover different claim types (system, method, medium) from the parent?
  • Have we analyzed our top 10 issued patents for continuation opportunities to cover narrower, design-around-resistant claims?
  • Have we coordinated prosecution history across family members to avoid creating prosecution history estoppel that limits scope in related patents?
  • Have we evaluated CIP opportunities for inventions that are improvements on existing patented technologies?
  • Are all divisional election decisions being treated strategically (not just acquiescing to restriction requirements without continuing)?

C. International Strategy

  • Have we filed PCT applications on our most commercially important inventions within 12 months of the U.S. filing date?
  • Have we entered national phase in the key jurisdictions where our competitors operate or manufacture (EP, CN, JP at minimum)?
  • Do we have a clear policy on which inventions merit international protection, based on market and enforcement analysis?
  • Are all PCT applications drafted to satisfy EPO written description standards as well as USPTO standards?

D. Competitive Intelligence

  • Have we set up automated alerts to monitor patent filings by our top 5 competitors?
  • Have we conducted a patent landscape analysis for our core technology domain within the past 18 months?
  • Have we completed a freedom-to-operate analysis for every major product launch?
  • Do we have a protocol for reviewing competitor patent publications and extracting competitive intelligence?
  • Are we tracking the patent activity of top researchers who might be recruited by competitors?

E. Defensive and Collaborative Programs

  • Have we established a defensive publication program for inventions that do not meet the filing threshold?
  • Have we evaluated membership in OIN, LOT Network, or other defensive pledge programs?
  • Do we have a policy on how to handle open-source releases to minimize loss of patent rights?
  • Have we considered making strategic defensive publications to block competitors in technology areas we are not patenting?

F. Portfolio Management and Valuation

  • Do we have a Patent Review Committee with clear decision criteria and quarterly meetings?
  • Have we scored our entire portfolio on quality metrics (forward citations, claim breadth, family size, commercial relevance) within the past year?
  • Have we completed a maintenance fee audit to identify patents that should be abandoned?
  • Do we have current DCF or comparable transaction estimates for our top 20 patents?
  • Have we explored licensing or sale of patents that are no longer core to our product strategy?
  • Does our annual IP budget reflect the actual strategic priorities of the business (not just historical spending patterns)?
  • Are we prepared to present a coherent IP narrative to Series B+ investors or M&A acquirers?
★
The Most Common Startup IP Mistake

In ten years of advising AI startups at Fish & Richardson, the single most common and costly mistake I see is founders who say: "We'll do the IP work once we have product-market fit." By the time product-market fit is achieved, the company has typically published papers, presented at conferences, and open-sourced code — destroying patent rights in every jurisdiction that doesn't have a grace period (i.e., most of the world). The cost to retroactively build a portfolio from disclosures that are already public is orders of magnitude higher — and the scope you can obtain is orders of magnitude narrower — than if you had filed provisionals before the first disclosure. File first. Always.
