1. The Road from Invention to Patent

Obtaining a United States patent is a multi-year administrative and legal process involving inventors, patent counsel, and the United States Patent and Trademark Office (USPTO). For technology companies — and especially for AI and machine learning innovators — understanding this pipeline is not merely academic. Filing decisions made in the first weeks after an invention is conceived can determine whether a company ultimately secures meaningful patent protection or finds itself empty-handed years later.

This chapter traces the complete journey from initial invention disclosure through patent grant. We examine each stage in detail: what happens, who does what, how long it takes, and where the most common and costly mistakes occur. We pay particular attention to Art Unit 2100 series — the USPTO's technology centers responsible for computer architecture, software, and information security — where AI and ML patent applications are concentrated and where pendency times and first-action allowance rates present distinctive challenges.

The Complete Timeline from Invention to Grant

The following timeline shows all major stages a U.S. patent application passes through. Average durations are based on USPTO FY 2024 statistics. Individual applications may vary significantly — Track One prioritized examination can compress the examination phase to 6–12 months, while complex AI applications in crowded art units may experience significantly longer waits.

Day 0

Invention Disclosure

The inventor(s) document the invention in a company invention disclosure form (IDF) or lab notebook. This triggers internal IP review and starts the clock on any internal disclosure bar. For AI inventions, this includes recording training data sources, model architecture, hyperparameters, and benchmark results.

Days 14 – 60

IP Committee Review & Prior Art Search

The company's IP committee or designated patent counsel reviews the disclosure, conducts a preliminary prior art search, and makes a filing recommendation. For startups, this step may be faster but is no less important. Patentability opinions are typically drafted at this stage.

Days 30 – 90

Provisional Patent Application Filed

The provisional establishes a priority date and gives the company 12 months to file a full application. Government filing fee: $1,800 (large entity) / $900 (small) / $450 (micro). The provisional must provide written description support for every claim you intend to pursue. Average preparation time: 2–6 weeks with competent counsel.

Month 3 – 12

Non-Provisional Application Prepared

Full prosecution-ready application is drafted: detailed specification, claims, abstract, and drawings. The claims strategy is developed with an eye toward both AI/ML functional claim coverage and hardware-implementation claims to navigate § 101 eligibility. This is typically the most time-intensive phase for patent counsel.

Month 12

Non-Provisional Filed (or PCT Application)

The non-provisional must be filed within 12 months of the provisional. Alternatively, a PCT application may be filed to preserve international options. USPTO receives the application, assigns a serial number, and begins pre-examination processing. Large entity basic filing fee: $1,760 (online).

Month 18

Application Published

Under 35 U.S.C. § 122(b), the application is published at 18 months from earliest claimed priority date. The published application becomes prior art against later-filed applications. Publication can be avoided only by filing a non-publication request at filing, coupled with a commitment not to file foreign applications.

Months 18 – 24

Pre-Examination Processing & Art Unit Assignment

The application is classified, assigned to a Technology Center and Art Unit, and queued for examination. For AI/ML applications, this typically lands in AU 2100–2196 (Computer Architecture and Software) or AU 3600 (Transportation, Electronic Commerce, Business Methods) depending on the primary classification.

Months 24 – 36

First Office Action (Non-Final Rejection or Allowance)

The assigned examiner conducts a prior art search and issues either a first action allowance or, more commonly, a non-final office action with rejections. In AI art units, the overwhelming majority of first actions include § 101 (subject matter eligibility) and § 103 (obviousness) rejections. Average time to first action in AU 2100: approximately 26–30 months.

Months 33 – 42

Applicant Response & Interview

Applicant has 3 months (extendable to 6) to respond to the office action. Responses may include claim amendments, arguments traversing rejections, and/or a request for an examiner interview. Interviews can dramatically accelerate prosecution by clarifying the examiner's concerns and identifying claim language that achieves allowance.

Months 40 – 54

Final Office Action or Notice of Allowance

Following applicant response, the examiner issues either a notice of allowance or a final office action. If final, the applicant may file a Request for Continued Examination (RCE), an after-final amendment, or an appeal to the Patent Trial and Appeal Board (PTAB). Each path adds time and cost.

Months 50 – 60+

Notice of Allowance & Issue Fee

When allowed, the USPTO issues a Notice of Allowance. The applicant must pay the issue fee within 3 months (currently $1,200 for large entities, $480 for small, $240 for micro). Failure to timely pay results in abandonment, though petition for revival is available.

Months 54 – 66

Patent Granted

The patent issues on a Tuesday, bearing the date of grant. The patent term is 20 years from the earliest effective U.S. filing date of the non-provisional application, subject to Patent Term Adjustment (PTA) for USPTO delays. Maintenance fees are due at 3.5, 7.5, and 11.5 years post-grant.

💡
Track One: Compressing the Timeline

The USPTO's Track One Prioritized Examination program, available for an additional fee (~$4,200 for large entities, ~$2,100 for small, ~$1,050 for micro), can reduce total pendency to 6–12 months from filing to final disposition. Track One is capped at 10,000 petitions per fiscal year and applications must not include more than 4 independent and 30 total claims. For companies with imminent product launches, licensing negotiations, or litigation needs, Track One is often the most cost-effective acceleration strategy available.

2. Invention Disclosure and Notebooks

The invention disclosure is the foundational document of the patent process. A well-prepared invention disclosure form (IDF) enables patent counsel to assess patentability, identify claim scope, and draft a strong application. A poorly documented disclosure leads to missed claim opportunities, weak specifications, and — in the worst case — loss of patent rights due to inadequate corroboration of conception and reduction to practice.

The Invention Disclosure Form (IDF)

Most technology companies use a standardized IDF that captures the essential information patent counsel needs. Key sections of a comprehensive IDF include:

  • Invention title and one-sentence summary — A plain-language description of what the invention does.
  • Problem solved — What problem or inefficiency does this invention address? What was the state of the art before this invention?
  • Technical description of the invention — How does it work? Include system architecture, process flows, algorithms, and formulas. Diagrams are strongly encouraged.
  • Novel features — What is new compared to existing approaches? Inventors should describe what they believe distinguishes their invention from prior art.
  • All inventors — Full legal names, employee IDs, and signatures. Inventorship is a legal determination — err on the side of inclusion and let counsel determine who is a joint inventor under 35 U.S.C. § 116.
  • Conception date — When was the inventive concept first conceived? Contemporaneous records are critical for establishing priority.
  • Reduction to practice date — When was the invention first built, tested, or demonstrated to work?
  • Known prior art — Publications, products, patents, or systems the inventors are aware of that are related to the invention.
  • Public disclosures — Has the invention been disclosed publicly (conference presentations, papers, demos, code repositories, blog posts)? If so, dates are critical for determining whether the AIA § 102(b)(1) one-year grace period applies.
  • Commercial embodiments — Is this invention being incorporated into a product? Identifying products practicing the claims is important for patent marking compliance (35 U.S.C. § 287).

Electronic Lab Notebooks and Corroboration

Under the America Invents Act (AIA), the United States is now a first-inventor-to-file system (35 U.S.C. § 102). The race to file has replaced the old first-to-invent system that rewarded meticulous lab notebooks. However, laboratory notebooks and electronic equivalents remain critically important for:

  • Derivation proceedings — Under 35 U.S.C. § 135, a first-filed applicant can challenge whether the other party derived the invention from them. Documented evidence of independent conception is essential.
  • Inequitable conduct defense — Detailed records help demonstrate that material prior art was not withheld from the USPTO.
  • Inventorship disputes — When co-inventors disagree about who contributed to the claimed invention, contemporaneous records often resolve the dispute.
  • Trade secret preservation — For aspects of the technology that will not be patented, records establish trade secret ownership and the timeline of creation.
  • Government contract obligations — Federal funding agreements (Bayh-Dole) may require documentation of inventive activity and timely disclosure to funding agencies.
⚠️
Corroboration in the AIA Era

While first-to-file has reduced the emphasis on corroboration for priority disputes, undated or uncorroborated disclosure forms create serious problems in derivation proceedings and post-grant review. Every IDF should be signed and dated by the inventor(s) on the day of submission. Electronic systems (such as Anaqua, CPA Global, or IP.com) automatically timestamp submissions and provide tamper-evident audit trails. Paper notebooks should be witnessed and dated by a non-inventor who can testify to having read and understood the disclosure.

Special Considerations for AI and Machine Learning Inventions

AI and ML inventions present distinctive documentation challenges that do not arise with traditional software or hardware inventions. Standard IDF forms rarely capture the full technical picture of an AI invention. Patent counsel working with AI companies should request the following additional documentation:

  • Training data description — What data was used to train the model? Was it proprietary, licensed, or publicly available? How was it preprocessed or curated? The specification may need to describe the training data in enough detail to satisfy the written description and enablement requirements of 35 U.S.C. § 112(a).
  • Model architecture — Layer structure, attention mechanisms, activation functions, embedding dimensions. For transformer-based models: number of heads, depth, context window size.
  • Training procedure — Optimizer, learning rate schedule, batch size, compute resources, number of training steps. This information is relevant to both enablement and to establishing the inventive contribution over prior architectures.
  • Model checkpoints — Save and preserve model weights at key stages of training, especially at the point when novel performance was first observed. These can serve as evidence of reduction to practice.
  • Evaluation benchmarks and performance data — What benchmarks were used? What was the baseline performance of prior-art models on those benchmarks? What improvement does the invention demonstrate? Quantitative data is invaluable for arguing non-obviousness.
  • Ablation studies — Results of experiments removing components of the invention. These directly support arguments that the specific claimed combination produces unexpected results.
  • Failure modes and limitations — Honest documentation of where the model fails is important for enablement analysis and for scoping claims that accurately describe what the invention actually does.

3. Provisional Patent Applications

A provisional patent application is a simplified, lower-cost filing that establishes a priority date without initiating formal examination. Governed by 35 U.S.C. § 111(b) and 37 C.F.R. § 1.53(c), a provisional is not examined, never directly issues as a patent, and automatically abandons 12 months after filing. Its primary value is to lock in a priority date while giving the applicant time — and flexibility — to develop the invention, assess commercial potential, and prepare a more polished non-provisional application.

Why File a Provisional?

Benefits

  • Lower cost — Government filing fee is $1,800 (large entity), $900 (small), or $450 (micro) — substantially less than a full non-provisional.
  • Establishes priority date immediately — Under AIA § 102, prior art is evaluated as of the earliest effective priority date. Every day of delay is a day of risk.
  • 12-month window — Time to assess commercial viability, secure funding, and identify the optimal claim strategy before committing to full prosecution costs.
  • "Patent Pending" status — Legally permissible upon provisional filing, providing commercial deterrence and licensing leverage.
  • Informal requirements — No formal claims are required. The specification does not need to meet the full formality requirements of a non-provisional.
  • Multiple provisionals can be combined — A single non-provisional may claim priority to multiple provisionals filed within the 12-month window, allowing incremental disclosure as the invention develops.
  • PCT 12-month starting point — For international filers, the PCT application is typically due 12 months from the provisional, starting the 30-month national phase clock.

Limitations and Risks

  • Never examined or granted — A provisional never becomes a patent. It must be converted to or replaced by a non-provisional within 12 months, or all rights are lost.
  • Priority only for what is disclosed — The provisional only supports priority for claim elements that are fully described in the provisional itself. Claim scope added in the non-provisional will not receive the provisional's priority date.
  • No automatic conversion — The USPTO does not automatically convert provisionals. The applicant must separately file a non-provisional and include a specific priority claim referencing the provisional.
  • Confidentiality exposure — A provisional filed with insufficient detail may need to be supplemented or re-filed, potentially creating prosecution history estoppel risks.
  • Thin provisionals are dangerous — A provisional that fails to describe key claim elements creates a false sense of security. Competitors filing after your provisional but before your non-provisional may secure earlier priority dates for claim elements not in your provisional.
  • Clock starts running immediately — The 12-month deadline is hard; it cannot be extended. Missing it results in permanent loss of the provisional's priority benefit.
🚫
The "Thin Provisional" Problem

One of the most common and costly mistakes in patent practice is filing a provisional application that lacks the technical detail needed to support the claims that will ultimately matter commercially. Many companies, under time pressure, file a provisional that consists of a slide deck, a product brief, or an early-stage technical memo. When patent counsel later tries to draft claims covering the full invention, they discover that key elements were never described in the provisional. Those claim elements then receive only the non-provisional's filing date — sometimes months or years later — as their priority date. In competitive technology markets, that gap can be fatal. A provisional application should be written with the same technical depth as a non-provisional specification, even if claims are omitted.

What a Provisional Must Contain

While formal requirements are relaxed, a provisional must satisfy the written description and enablement requirements of 35 U.S.C. § 112(a) as to every element of any claim you intend to pursue in the later non-provisional. Specifically, the provisional must include:

  • A detailed technical description of the invention sufficient to enable a person of ordinary skill in the art (POSITA) to make and use it without undue experimentation.
  • Descriptions of all embodiments you intend to claim, including alternative implementations and preferred modes.
  • Any necessary drawings, diagrams, or flowcharts that illuminate the technical description.
  • For software and AI inventions: pseudocode, architectural diagrams, data flow charts, and descriptions of key algorithms.
  • For AI/ML inventions specifically: training methodology, model architecture, input/output specifications, and evaluation criteria.
  • A clear identification of the novel aspects of the invention — what distinguishes it from the prior art.

Provisional Filing Checklist

  • Cover sheet (Form PTO/SB/16) or equivalent information (inventor names, correspondence address)
  • Specification describing the invention in detail — at least as detailed as you want your claims to be
  • Drawings, flowcharts, and architecture diagrams referenced in the specification
  • Filing fee paid (online via Patent Center)
  • Correct entity status established (large/small/micro) — see Section 4 for definitions
  • All inventors listed (even if inventorship will be confirmed later)
  • All known prior art identified for later IDS submission
  • Description of all alternative embodiments you may want to claim
  • For AI/ML: training data description, model architecture, evaluation benchmarks
  • Docket the 12-month non-provisional deadline immediately upon filing
  • Confirm receipt of USPS Express Mail or Electronic Acknowledgement Receipt from USPTO
✅
Best Practice: File the Provisional with Draft Claims

Although claims are not required in a provisional application, including a set of draft claims — even rough ones — has significant practical value. Draft claims force the inventors and counsel to articulate what is actually novel, they serve as a roadmap for the specification, and they ensure that the specification contains disclosure specifically directed at the elements that will ultimately be claimed. Many experienced practitioners write draft claims first, then draft the specification around them, regardless of whether the filing is provisional or non-provisional.

4. Non-Provisional Patent Applications

The non-provisional patent application — filed under 35 U.S.C. § 111(a) — is the formal patent application that will be examined by the USPTO and, if allowed, will mature into a patent. Unlike the provisional, a non-provisional must include at least one claim, must comply with all formal requirements of 37 C.F.R. Part 1, and initiates the examination process upon filing.

Required Sections of a Non-Provisional Application

  1. Specification (37 C.F.R. § 1.71)

    The full written description of the invention. Must include: (1) Title of the Invention; (2) Cross-References to Related Applications (if any); (3) Statement Regarding Federally Sponsored Research (if applicable); (4) Field of the Invention; (5) Background of the Invention; (6) Summary of the Invention; (7) Brief Description of the Drawings; (8) Detailed Description of the Preferred Embodiment(s); (9) Claims; (10) Abstract. The Detailed Description is the heart of the specification and must enable the full scope of the claims.

  2. Claims (35 U.S.C. § 112; 37 C.F.R. § 1.75)

    The claims define the legal scope of the patent right. A well-drafted set of claims includes: (1) one or more independent method claims covering the process; (2) one or more independent system/apparatus claims covering the hardware/software implementation; (3) a Beauregard claim (computer-readable medium) for software-based inventions; (4) dependent claims narrowing each independent claim to specific embodiments. For AI applications: claims should address both the trained model and the training process.

  3. Abstract (37 C.F.R. § 1.72(b))

    A concise summary of the invention, not exceeding 150 words. The abstract should describe the most important technical features and utility of the invention. It appears on the patent's front page and in USPTO search results. Critically: do not recite claim language verbatim in the abstract — courts and examiners may use the abstract to construe claims, and overreaching abstracts can create prosecution history estoppel problems.

  4. Drawings (37 C.F.R. § 1.81–1.88)

    Required whenever drawings are necessary for understanding the invention. For software and AI applications, this includes flowcharts, system architecture diagrams, neural network diagrams, data flow charts, and illustrative examples of the algorithm's operation. See Chapter 7 for detailed drawing requirements. Each figure must be referenced at least once in the detailed description.

  5. Application Data Sheet (ADS) (37 C.F.R. § 1.76)

    The ADS provides bibliographic information: inventors' names, citizenship, and residence; correspondence address; entity status; priority claims; and assignment information. For applications claiming priority to a provisional, the ADS must include the provisional's application number, filing date, and a specific benefit claim under 35 U.S.C. § 119(e). Errors in the ADS can be corrected by filing a corrected ADS, but some errors (e.g., missing priority claims) may have time-sensitive correction procedures.

  6. Information Disclosure Statement (IDS) (37 C.F.R. § 1.97–1.98)

    The IDS is the applicant's disclosure to the USPTO of all known material prior art. All U.S. patents, U.S. patent publications, foreign patents, and non-patent literature (NPL) known to the inventors or prosecution team that are material to patentability must be submitted. Failure to disclose known material prior art can result in the patent being unenforceable for inequitable conduct. The IDS should be filed with the application or as early as practicable thereafter.

  7. Declaration or Oath (35 U.S.C. § 115; 37 C.F.R. § 1.63)

    Each inventor must sign a declaration (or oath) stating that they believe they are the original inventor of the claimed invention and that they have reviewed and understand the application. Under the AIA, a declaration may be replaced by a substitute statement in limited circumstances (e.g., deceased inventor). Declarations can be filed after initial filing but prior to payment of the issue fee.

Entity Status: Fees and Qualifications

USPTO fees are scaled by entity size. Claiming an incorrect entity status is a serious matter — paying insufficient fees (underpayment) can result in abandonment, while claiming small or micro entity status when not entitled results in prosecution and potential enforceability problems. Verify entity status carefully before each fee payment.

Total Cost of U.S. Patent Prosecution

Government fees are only a fraction of total prosecution costs. Attorney fees for drafting, prosecution, and responding to office actions typically range from $15,000 to $35,000 for an AI/ML application through allowance, depending on complexity and the number of office action rounds. Applications requiring an appeal to PTAB can cost significantly more. Companies building patent portfolios should budget $20,000–$50,000 per patent asset, inclusive of USPTO fees, attorney fees, and response costs over the full prosecution lifecycle.

5. Filing Through Patent Center

The USPTO's Patent Center (patentcenter.uspto.gov) is the agency's unified electronic filing and application management system, which replaced the older EFS-Web platform in 2022. Patent Center is the exclusive portal for electronically filing new applications, submitting responses, paying fees, and retrieving prosecution documents. All registered practitioners must use Patent Center for electronic filing; unrepresented inventors may also file through the system.

Step-by-Step Filing Process

  1. Create or Log In to USPTO Account

    Both registered patent practitioners and unrepresented inventors must have a USPTO.gov account. Registered practitioners use their USPTO registration number credentials. Customers must have a Customer Number associated with their account to file on behalf of applicants and receive correspondence from the USPTO.

  2. Select Filing Category

    In Patent Center, select "File an Application" and choose the appropriate filing type: Utility (non-provisional), Design, Plant, Provisional, or PCT National Stage. For continuation, divisional, and continuation-in-part applications, select the appropriate continuation type and enter the parent application number.

  3. Upload Application Documents

    All documents must be submitted in PDF/A format (or Word format for the specification, which Patent Center will convert). Required uploads: (1) Application Data Sheet (ADS); (2) Specification with claims and abstract; (3) Drawings (if applicable, in PDF format meeting USPTO drawing standards per MPEP § 608.02); (4) Executed inventor declaration(s) or a statement that declaration will be submitted later; (5) IDS (Form PTO/SB/08) with any listed references; (6) Power of Attorney (Form PTO/AIA/82), if applicable.

  4. Complete the Application Data Sheet

    The ADS is the single most legally consequential document in the filing package after the claims. Verify: (1) all inventor names are spelled correctly and match signatures; (2) priority claims under § 119(e) reference the exact provisional application number and filing date; (3) entity status is correctly selected; (4) correspondence address is the practitioner's Customer Number address; (5) title of invention matches the specification.

  5. Calculate and Pay Fees

    Patent Center includes an automated fee calculator. Verify the fee calculation manually: count independent claims (excess over 3), total claims (excess over 20), and confirm entity status. Pay via electronic funds transfer (EFT, lowest transaction cost), credit card, or existing USPTO deposit account. EFT is the preferred payment method — no surcharge and immediate confirmation.

  6. Review and Submit

    Patent Center displays a complete review screen before final submission. Review all documents, fee calculations, and data for accuracy. Once submitted, changes to the ADS (particularly priority claims) may require formal petition and may not be grantable if the statutory period has expired.

  7. Retrieve and Record Acknowledgement Receipt

    Patent Center immediately generates an Acknowledgement Receipt confirming filing date, serial number, and documents received. Download and archive this receipt immediately. The filing date shown on the Acknowledgement Receipt is the application's official filing date for priority purposes. Record the serial number in your docketing system and docket the critical deadlines: 18-month publication, PCT deadline (if applicable), and examination milestones.

Assignment Recording

Patent rights vest initially in the inventor(s) under 35 U.S.C. § 262. To transfer ownership to the employing company (the standard situation in technology), a written assignment must be executed by each inventor and recorded with the USPTO's Assignment Division. Assignments can be recorded at or after filing through Patent Center or via the USPTO's Assignment Center portal. The assignment should be recorded before the application publishes (at 18 months) to ensure the correct assignee appears on the published application. Under the AIA, a registered assignee or obligated assignee may also apply for a patent in the company's name from the outset, with inventor declarations filed separately.

Power of Attorney

A Power of Attorney (POA) authorizes a registered patent practitioner to act before the USPTO on behalf of the applicant. The POA must be signed by the applicant (assignee or inventors, depending on who is the applicant of record). Without a POA, the practitioner can only file correspondence as a "customer" of the applicant and may face limitations on certain actions. Best practice: execute and file the POA at the time of application filing to ensure seamless communication with the examiner throughout prosecution.

⚠️
Patent Center Outages and Filing Deadlines

Patent Center experiences periodic planned and unplanned outages. Under 37 C.F.R. § 1.7(a), if the USPTO is unavailable on a filing deadline date, the deadline is extended to the next business day when the office is open. However, this protection does not apply to statutory deadlines (such as the 12-month provisional conversion deadline or PCT national phase entry deadlines). For any deadline-critical filing, never wait until the last day. Experienced practitioners typically docket a "must-file" date 3 business days ahead of the actual deadline and prepare filing packages well in advance. Keep a backup filing option (registered mail with certificate of mailing under 37 C.F.R. § 1.8) as a contingency.

6. What Happens After Filing

Once a patent application is submitted to the USPTO, it enters a multi-stage administrative pipeline before an examiner ever reads a word of the specification. Understanding this pipeline helps practitioners manage client expectations, identify when delays are normal versus problematic, and know when to take action to accelerate or monitor prosecution.

Stage-by-Stage Details

Docketing and Serial Number Assignment

Within hours to days of electronic filing, Patent Center assigns an application serial number in the format XX/XXX,XXX (e.g., 18/437,291 for applications filed in fiscal year 2024 in the utility track). This serial number is the application's permanent identifier throughout prosecution and is used for all correspondence with the USPTO. A Filing Receipt is generated and sent to the correspondence address of record, confirming the serial number, filing date, inventors, and title.

Office of Patent Application Processing (OPAP)

The OPAP conducts a formalities review of the application to confirm: (1) all required documents are present; (2) the specification is readable and not missing pages; (3) drawings are in acceptable format; (4) the correct fees have been paid; (5) inventor signatures (or statements that declarations will be submitted) are present. OPAP may issue "Notice to File Corrected Application Papers" (NTFCAP) or "Notice of Incomplete Application" if deficiencies are identified. These must be addressed within the specified time period — typically 2 months.

Technology Center and Art Unit Assignment

After formalities clearance, the application is classified under the Cooperative Patent Classification (CPC) system and assigned to a Technology Center (TC) and Art Unit. For AI and machine learning applications, the most common TC/AU assignments are:

  • TC 2100 (Computer Architecture, Software, & Information Security): AU 2100–2196 — machine learning algorithms, neural networks, natural language processing, computer vision, reinforcement learning
  • TC 3600 (Transportation, Electronic Commerce, & Business Methods): AU 3620–3690 — AI applied to business processes, e-commerce recommendations, financial AI, healthcare AI applications
  • TC 1600 (Biotechnology & Organic Chemistry): AU 1630–1670 — AI/ML applied to drug discovery, genomics, protein structure prediction
  • TC 2400 (Networking, Multiplexing, Cable, & Security): AU 2460–2490 — AI for network optimization, cybersecurity AI, anomaly detection

The art unit assignment is critical because different art units apply § 101 eligibility requirements differently, have different examiner workloads, and different first-action allowance rates. If an application is misclassified, a petition for art unit reassignment is available but rarely granted without strong justification.

18-Month Publication

Under 35 U.S.C. § 122(b), applications are published as U.S. Patent Application Publications (USPA) at 18 months from the earliest claimed priority date, unless a non-publication request was timely filed. The published application receives a Publication Number in the format US 20XX/XXXXXXX. From the publication date, the applicant gains "provisional rights" under § 154(d) — the right to recover reasonable royalty from an infringer who had actual notice of the published application, subject to the claims as ultimately issued being substantially identical to the published claims.

7. The Examination Process

Patent examination is a quasi-adversarial administrative proceeding in which a USPTO examiner evaluates the applicant's claimed invention against statutory requirements and prior art. The examiner acts as both advocate for the patent system's integrity (rejecting claims that do not deserve patent protection) and as a neutral evaluator of the law. Understanding how examiners work — their incentives, their databases, their interpretive frameworks — is essential to effective prosecution strategy.

The Examiner's Role and Incentives

USPTO patent examiners are federal employees, most with degrees in engineering, computer science, or physical sciences, and most working toward eventual registration as patent agents. Examiners are evaluated on "production" — a metric that measures the number of "counts" (roughly, actions taken) per biweek. This production incentive system has important practical implications:

  • Examiners earn a "count" for issuing a first office action and another for disposing of the application (allowance, abandonment, or RCE). This creates some incentive to issue office actions rather than allow on first review, since an allowance earns only one count while a rejection followed by response followed by allowance earns two.
  • The complexity of AI and ML applications — often with many claims, complex technical descriptions, and § 101 eligibility issues — means that examiner production counts do not fully compensate for the time required to examine these applications. This can lead to delayed examination and shortcuts in prior art searching.
  • Examiners in busy art units (AU 2100 in particular) carry heavy dockets. Backlogs in TC 2100 have historically been among the worst at the USPTO. As of FY 2024, the average examiner docket in AU 2100 contains over 90 pending applications.

Examiner Interview Practice

One of the most powerful and underutilized tools in patent prosecution is the examiner interview. Under 37 C.F.R. § 1.133, the applicant (or their representative) may request a telephone, video, or in-person interview with the examiner at any time during prosecution. Interviews are particularly valuable in AI and ML prosecution for several reasons.

Why Interviews Work

  • Direct dialogue clarifies the examiner's specific concerns about § 101 eligibility, claim scope, and prior art — information that is often obscured in formal written rejections.
  • Examiners can informally indicate whether proposed claim amendments would overcome rejections, saving one full prosecution round and months of delay.
  • Technical demonstrations can illustrate the AI system's operation in ways that static drawings and text cannot — particularly valuable for complex neural architectures.
  • Personal rapport reduces adversarial friction and creates goodwill that can lead to more reasonable claim constructions and thoughtful engagement with arguments.
  • Statistics show that applications with interviews have higher allowance rates and shorter prosecution histories than those without.

Interview Risks and Limitations

  • Anything said during an interview that is not reflected in the Interview Summary (Form PTO-474) may be used against the applicant in later claim construction, creating informal prosecution history estoppel.
  • An interview before the examiner has fully studied the application can be premature — the examiner may not have enough context to engage meaningfully.
  • Some examiners use interviews to extract claim concessions that they could not compel through formal action — practitioners should prepare specific alternative claim language in advance, not open-ended invitations to amend.
  • In complex rejections with multiple grounds, interviews can sometimes only address some issues, leaving others unresolved and potentially hardening the examiner's position on those issues.

Strategic Implication: Claim Drafting for AI Art Units

The § 101 rejection rates shown above are not immutable facts of nature — they are a function of how claims are drafted. Applications with claims that recite specific technical improvements to computer functionality (reduced computational complexity, improved memory utilization, faster inference, novel hardware architectures) have substantially lower § 101 rejection rates than applications whose claims are drafted at a high level of abstraction. The data supports a clear drafting strategy: anchor independent claims in the technical implementation, use dependent claims to cover the higher-level functional result. For AI/ML applications, this typically means including claims that describe the specific training methodology, architectural innovations, and measurable technical benefits alongside broader functional claims.

8. The Decision: Allowance or Office Action

After completing the prior art search and reviewing the application, the examiner reaches a decision on each claim. The overwhelming majority of applications — particularly in AI art units — receive at least one office action before allowance. Understanding the procedural mechanics of both paths, and the statistics behind them, enables practitioners to set realistic client expectations and craft efficient prosecution strategies.

Path 1: Notice of Allowance

If the examiner finds all pending claims allowable — meaning they satisfy § 101 (subject matter eligibility), § 102 (novelty), § 103 (non-obviousness), and § 112 (written description, enablement, definiteness) — the USPTO issues a Notice of Allowance (NOA) on Form PTOL-85. The NOA specifies:

  • The application serial number, title, and allowed claim numbers
  • The issue fee amount due and the 3-month deadline for payment
  • Any examiner's amendments made during allowance (which may require examiner interview if applicant disagrees)
  • Any reasons for allowance — a valuable prosecution history document that can support future infringement analysis

Upon timely payment of the issue fee, the patent is forwarded to the USPTO's Issue and Publication Division, where it is assigned an issue date (always a Tuesday) and a patent number (in the format US X,XXX,XXX B2 for post-AIA patents). The patent is published in the Official Gazette on the issue date and becomes enforceable from that date.

Path 2: Office Action

An office action is a written communication from the examiner setting forth rejections, objections, and/or requirements. There are three main types of office actions in utility prosecution:

  • Non-Final Office Action (NFOA): The first rejection on the merits. The applicant has a full right to amend claims, submit arguments, and request an interview. A complete response to a NFOA must be filed within 3 months of the mailing date to avoid extension fees, and within 6 months (maximum) to avoid abandonment.
  • Final Office Action (FOA): Issued when the examiner believes prosecution is complete. After a FOA, the applicant's options are more limited: an after-final amendment under 37 C.F.R. § 1.116 (examiner has discretion to enter or reject), an RCE, or an appeal. A FOA does not mean the end of prosecution — it means the examiner has taken their best shot at articulating why the claims should not be allowed.
  • Restriction Requirement: If the application contains claims directed to more than one independent and distinct invention, the examiner may require the applicant to elect one group of claims for examination, with the other groups divisional out. Restriction requirements are common in AI applications with both training method claims and inference system claims.

After Allowance: Continuing Application Strategy

Patent grant is not the end of the strategic picture. Many companies file continuation applications — which claim priority to the parent application but present different or narrowed claims — to pursue additional coverage as the technology develops and competitive landscape becomes clearer. Three continuation types are available:

  • Continuation (CON): Same disclosure as the parent; different claims. Used to pursue broader, narrower, or differently drafted claims that were not allowed in the parent prosecution.
  • Continuation-in-Part (CIP): Adds new subject matter to the parent disclosure. New matter receives only the CIP filing date; original disclosure receives parent priority. Common in rapidly developing AI fields where the product evolves during prosecution.
  • Divisional (DIV): Pursues claims to an invention that was restricted out of the parent. Receives the parent's priority date and constitutes a distinct patent family member.

9. PCT Applications

The Patent Cooperation Treaty (PCT), administered by the World Intellectual Property Organization (WIPO), provides a streamlined mechanism for seeking patent protection in over 150 countries through a single international application. For technology companies with global ambitions — which describes virtually every AI company of significance — the PCT is the international patent prosecution framework of choice.

A PCT application does not itself result in an "international patent" — there is no such thing. Rather, the PCT establishes a filing date and provides a centralized international phase during which an International Searching Authority (ISA) conducts a prior art search, before the applicant must commit to individual national or regional patent filings. This international phase buys time and information, allowing companies to make informed national phase entry decisions based on actual prior art search results and commercial traction.

PCT Chapter I vs. Chapter II

Chapter I: International Application and Search (Mandatory)

Every PCT application proceeds through Chapter I. The applicant files an international application with a Receiving Office (for U.S. applicants, typically the USPTO or IB). The ISA — which for U.S.-origin applications is typically the USPTO, EPO, or Korean KIPO — conducts a search and issues a Written Opinion (WO-ISA) and International Search Report (ISR). The ISR lists prior art documents considered by the ISA. The Written Opinion assesses whether the claimed invention appears to meet the requirements of novelty, inventive step, and industrial applicability. At the end of Chapter I (approximately 18 months from filing), the international application is published as a WIPO publication with number WO XXXX/XXXXXX.

Chapter II: International Preliminary Examination (Optional)

Before national phase entry, applicants may optionally request International Preliminary Examination (IPEA) under Chapter II. This triggers a demand for examination before an International Preliminary Examining Authority (IPEA), which issues an International Preliminary Report on Patentability (IPRP — Chapter II). The IPRP provides a more detailed patentability opinion than the Chapter I Written Opinion and is based on an amended set of claims. Many applicants use Chapter II to refine their claims and obtain a favorable IPRP before national phase entry, which can ease prosecution in individual national offices. The demand for Chapter II must be filed no later than 22 months from the priority date.

Key PCT Strategic Considerations for AI Companies

  • ISA selection matters. The choice of ISA significantly affects the prior art search quality and the Written Opinion's patentability assessment. The EPO as ISA typically conducts the most thorough search and the most rigorous patentability analysis — useful if you want to know the worst-case scenario before national phase entry. The USPTO as ISA applies U.S. law standards, which may differ from EPO practice (particularly on § 101 / Article 52(2) exclusions for software).
  • The ISR is not binding. National patent offices conduct their own examinations independent of the ISR and Written Opinion. A negative Chapter I opinion does not prevent national phase entry or allowance in individual countries.
  • Translation costs are the biggest barrier. For most countries, national phase entry requires a certified translation of the application into the local language. Japanese, Korean, and Chinese translations of a complex AI application can cost $15,000–$30,000 each. Companies should prioritize national phase entries based on commercial markets and competitive threats.
  • Strategically delay national phase entry. The PCT buys 30 months from priority date before committing to individual country filings. Use this time to assess: Has the product launched? Is there infringement to enforce against? Are there competitors in specific markets who need to be blocked?

AI Patent Filing in Key International Markets

AI patent strategy increasingly requires attention to Chinese filings. China has surpassed the United States in AI patent application volume — CNIPA received over 200,000 AI-related patent applications in 2023. Chinese courts have become increasingly willing to enforce AI patents, and the CNIPA has shown favorable disposition to AI inventions that demonstrate specific technical effects. For any AI company with Chinese market exposure, CNIPA national phase entry through PCT is a non-optional component of a comprehensive portfolio strategy. The EPO presents its own challenges — Article 52(2) of the European Patent Convention explicitly excludes "mathematical methods" and "programs for computers" as such, requiring claim drafting that emphasizes the technical character of the invention. However, EPO practice is nuanced, and well-drafted AI claims regularly obtain European patents.

Micro Entity Status: Verify Before Every Payment

Micro entity status must be recertified each time a fee subject to the micro entity discount is paid. Monitor inventor compensation annually and verify eligibility before each USPTO fee payment.

11. Strategic Timing

Patent filing timing is one of the most strategically consequential decisions in IP management, yet it is one that many technology companies handle reactively rather than proactively. Filing too early — before the invention is fully developed — can result in a thin specification that fails to support commercially important claims. Filing too late — after a public disclosure or a competitor's filing — can cost the company its priority date or, in the worst case, its patent rights entirely.

The AIA § 102 Grace Period and the Publication Race

Under the AIA's first-inventor-to-file system, prior art is defined under § 102(a)(1) to include everything "patented, described in a printed publication, or in public use, on sale, or otherwise available to the public before the effective filing date of the claimed invention." This means that every public disclosure — including the inventor's own disclosures — constitutes prior art against the application.

The saving grace is § 102(b)(1), which creates a one-year grace period for disclosures "made by the inventor or joint inventor or by others who obtained the subject matter disclosed directly or indirectly from the inventor." In plain terms: if an inventor publishes a paper about their invention and files a patent application within one year, the paper does not count as prior art. However, third parties who independently publish similar work during that one-year window do create prior art against the application — § 102(b)(1) does not protect against independent third-party disclosures.

🚫
The AI Publication Trap: arXiv and Conference Deadlines

The AI research community's publication culture creates a distinctive patent risk that does not exist in most other technology fields. The norm of posting preprints to arXiv simultaneously with or before conference submission means that an AI company's breakthrough research can become public prior art against its own patent application literally overnight — and before a patent application is even conceived. Companies should implement a clear policy: no arXiv posting, conference submission, or product demo disclosure without prior IP review and either: (a) a provisional application on file, or (b) an explicit decision that the technology will not be patented. This review process can and should be fast — 24–72 hours for straightforward inventions — but it cannot be skipped.

Coordinating with Product Launches

For AI companies launching products that embody patented technology, the sequence of filing, publication, and product release requires careful coordination:

  1. File before any public disclosure — Press releases, product pages, investor decks, and beta user agreements can all constitute "public use" or "on sale" activity triggering the § 102(a)(1) prior art bar. File before any of these occur.
  2. Mark products appropriately — After filing, products may be marked "Patent Pending." After grant, marking with the patent number satisfies 35 U.S.C. § 287 and is required to recover pre-suit damages for infringement. Virtual marking (a URL to a page listing patent numbers) satisfies the marking requirement for software and web services.
  3. Track the 18-month publication date — Once your application publishes, your claims are publicly visible to competitors. File continuation applications before publication if you have additional claim coverage to pursue that you do not want to telegraph to competitors.
  4. Coordinate international filing deadlines with market entry — If you are entering the Chinese or European market within 2–3 years, ensure PCT national phase entry is calendared and funded well in advance of the 30-month deadline.

Race-to-File Considerations in AI

In competitive AI subsectors — large language model training, diffusion model architectures, reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF), neural network hardware accelerators — the race to file is real and unforgiving. Several practical points:

  • Multiple independent research groups routinely arrive at similar architectures within weeks or days of each other. In a first-to-file system, the winner is the first filer, not the first discoverer.
  • Patent filings are a leading indicator of where competitors are investing. Monitoring competitor patent applications (which publish at 18 months) is a cost-effective competitive intelligence strategy.
  • Provisional applications are effective race-to-file tools, but only if they contain enough disclosure to actually support the claims you need. A provisional that describes a concept without the implementation details is not worth the filing fee.
  • Consider filing multiple provisionals as the invention evolves — filing a second provisional at month 6 to capture improvements, then a non-provisional at month 12 claiming priority to both. This staggered approach can maximize claim coverage while keeping costs manageable during the development phase.

12. Application Filing Checklist

Use this comprehensive checklist to verify that a non-provisional utility patent application is complete and ready for filing. Each item represents either a legal requirement, a best practice grounded in prosecution experience, or a deadline management obligation. A single missing item can result in office actions, missed deadlines, or loss of patent rights.

A. Invention Disclosure and Pre-Filing

  • Signed Invention Disclosure Form received from all named inventors
  • Conception date established and documented with corroborating evidence
  • Reduction to practice date documented (actual or constructive)
  • All public disclosures identified with exact dates (papers, talks, demos, GitHub repos, preprints)
  • § 102(b)(1) grace period analysis completed — confirm application filed within 12 months of any inventor disclosures
  • Prior art search completed by counsel or professional search firm
  • Patentability opinion or assessment prepared and reviewed with client
  • All known prior art collected for IDS submission
  • Inventorship determination finalized — all and only persons who contributed to conception of at least one claim element are named
  • Entity status confirmed — large, small, or micro entity status verified as of filing date

B. Specification Drafting

  • Title of invention included and matches ADS
  • Cross-reference to related applications (if any) included with correct application numbers and filing dates
  • Federally sponsored research statement included if invention was made with government support
  • Background of the Invention describes the state of the art and problem addressed — written to support non-obviousness arguments
  • Summary of the Invention provides a concise overview of the claimed invention consistent with claims
  • Brief Description of the Drawings lists and briefly describes each figure by figure number
  • Detailed Description provides full written description support for every claim element of every pending claim
  • At least one preferred embodiment is described in sufficient detail to enable a POSITA to make and use the invention
  • Alternative embodiments described to provide claim scope support and design-around resistance
  • For AI/ML applications: training methodology, model architecture, input/output specifications, and evaluation criteria are described with sufficient detail to satisfy § 112(a) enablement
  • Every claim element finds express support in the specification — claim-by-claim written description mapping completed by drafting attorney
  • Abstract drafted — does not exceed 150 words; does not use trademark names; describes the invention broadly
  • Specification reviewed for unintentional claim disclaimers — no language limiting claims to specific embodiments unless intentional

C. Claims Drafting and Review

  • At least one independent claim present
  • Independent method claim(s) drafted covering the process
  • Independent system/apparatus claim(s) drafted covering the hardware and/or software implementation
  • Beauregard claim (computer-readable medium) included for software-based inventions
  • Dependent claims in appropriate number and scope — typically 20–30 total claims for a comprehensive filing
  • Claims reviewed for § 101 subject matter eligibility — independent claims include concrete, technical improvement language; abstract idea is applied to a specific technical problem
  • Claims reviewed for § 112(b) definiteness — no indefinite terms (e.g., "approximately," "substantially") without specification support defining their meaning
  • Independent claim count: [ ] — note any excess over 3 for fee calculation
  • Total claim count: [ ] — note any excess over 20 for fee calculation
  • Means-plus-function claims (§ 112(f)) identified; corresponding structure identified in specification for each MPF element
  • Claim numbering is sequential, starting with claim 1, with all dependencies correctly stated
  • Claims reviewed by second attorney or senior partner before filing

D. Drawings

  • All figures identified in the Brief Description of Drawings section are present
  • Drawings comply with USPTO format requirements: black and white, on 8.5" × 11" paper equivalent, with appropriate margins
  • Each drawing element is identified with a reference numeral used consistently throughout the specification and drawings
  • Reference numerals in the drawings all appear and are defined in the Detailed Description
  • Flowcharts clearly show decision points, process steps, and flow direction
  • Architecture diagrams show all components referenced in the claims
  • For AI/ML applications: neural network diagrams, training pipelines, and data flow diagrams are included as necessary
  • Drawings reviewed in PDF format as they will be submitted to USPTO — confirm legibility, resolution, and character size

E. Administrative Documents

  • Application Data Sheet (ADS) completed — inventors, correspondence address, entity status, title
  • Priority claim in ADS correctly references provisional application number and filing date (if applicable)
  • Inventor declaration(s) (Form PTO/AIA/01) executed by all inventors, or statement filed that declarations will be submitted later
  • Assignment agreement executed by all inventors and ready for recordation
  • Power of Attorney (Form PTO/AIA/82) executed and ready for filing
  • Information Disclosure Statement (Form PTO/SB/08) listing all known prior art, with copies of non-patent literature references
  • Transmittal letter or cover sheet prepared
  • All documents converted to PDF/A format for Patent Center upload
  • Document filenames follow Patent Center naming conventions

F. Fees and Filing

  • Fee calculation completed and verified: basic filing + search + examination + excess claims (if applicable) + size fee (if applicable)
  • Entity status re-verified as of filing date — not assumed from prior filings
  • Payment method confirmed — deposit account number, credit card, or EFT ready
  • Track One petition prepared (if prioritized examination desired) and fees calculated
  • Filing checklist reviewed and signed off by responsible attorney
  • Patent Center filing completed and Acknowledgement Receipt downloaded and archived
  • Filing receipt reviewed — serial number, filing date, and documents received confirmed

G. Post-Filing Docketing

  • Serial number entered into docketing system immediately
  • 12-month deadline docketed if provisional was filed (deadline to file non-provisional claiming priority)
  • 18-month publication date docketed (for monitoring and continuation strategy purposes)
  • PCT filing deadline (12 months from priority) docketed if international filing is contemplated
  • Client notified of filing date, serial number, and "Patent Pending" status
  • Assignment recorded with USPTO Assignment Division within 3 months of filing
  • IDS supplementation procedure in place — team members know to flag new prior art to prosecution counsel for IDS submission
  • Examiner assignment monitored — set Patent Center alert for first office action
✅
The 15-Minute Rule

Experienced patent counsel apply a simple rule to every filing: set aside 15 uninterrupted minutes before final submission to read the entire ADS from top to bottom and cross-check every priority claim, inventor name, and entity status declaration against the underlying documents. ADS errors are the single most common source of correctible but painful prosecution history problems — wrong provisional application number, misspelled inventor name, incorrect entity status. These errors can usually be corrected but require petitions, additional fees, and consume examiner and attorney time that could be better spent. Fifteen minutes of careful review at the point of filing prevents hours of remediation downstream.
