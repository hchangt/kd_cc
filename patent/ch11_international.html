1. The International Patent Landscape

When an AI company builds a breakthrough model — a novel transformer variant, a new training methodology, an efficient inference engine — the competitive stakes extend far beyond domestic borders. Competitors operate in Shenzhen, Seoul, Tokyo, and Munich as readily as in San Francisco. Manufacturers who might infringe a process patent are scattered across supply chains that span a dozen countries. Licensees who might pay royalties are often large multinationals headquartered in Europe or Japan. And the fastest-growing AI markets in the world — China, South Korea, and India — represent enormous revenue opportunities that domestic patents cannot touch.

Yet one of the most persistent misconceptions in technology innovation is the idea that a single "world patent" exists. It does not. A U.S. patent grants rights only within the territory of the United States. A Chinese patent grants rights only within China. An issued European patent, once validated in individual member states, grants rights only in those states where validation fees were paid and translations were filed. International patent protection is, at bottom, a collection of national and regional grants — each obtained through separate prosecution, each independently maintained, and each enforced in local courts applying local rules.

Why International Protection Matters for AI/ML Companies

For an AI or machine learning company, international patent strategy is rarely optional. Consider the following structural facts about the AI industry:

  • Manufacturing is global. If an AI chip, training appliance, or inference accelerator embodies your patented architecture, and that hardware is manufactured in Taiwan or South Korea, a U.S. patent alone cannot block importation unless you have also secured rights in those manufacturing jurisdictions — or rely on U.S. ITC remedies.
  • Cloud services cross borders. A competitor running your patented AI inference method on servers in the EU or Japan does not infringe U.S. patent rights. You need European and Japanese patents to pursue that conduct.
  • China is the world's largest AI filer. Chinese companies filed more AI-related patents than any other country since 2019. If your technology enters the Chinese market — whether through products, services, or licensing — Chinese patent rights are the only meaningful lever you have.
  • Acquisitions and due diligence. Strategic acquirers, particularly large technology companies and sovereign wealth funds, assign substantial value to international patent families. A purely domestic portfolio signals limited geographic ambition and reduces enterprise valuation in M&A.
  • Cross-licensing leverage. International coverage creates cross-licensing parity with global technology companies. Without European and Japanese patents, a U.S. AI startup negotiating a cross-license with a Japanese industrial conglomerate has minimal leverage.

The Paris Convention and National Treatment

The foundational instrument of international patent law is the Paris Convention for the Protection of Industrial Property, adopted in 1883 and now administered by the World Intellectual Property Organization (WIPO) with 180 member states. The Paris Convention establishes two principles that every international filer relies upon:

National treatment requires each member state to grant nationals of other member states the same rights as its own nationals in matters of intellectual property. A Chinese company filing a patent application at the USPTO must be treated identically to a U.S. company filing the same application. National treatment is the bedrock principle that makes a multilateral patent system coherent.

Convention priority — codified in Article 4 of the Paris Convention — gives an applicant who files a patent application in one member country the right to file in any other member country within twelve months and claim the original filing date as the "priority date" for prior art purposes. This twelve-month priority window is the cornerstone of international filing strategy: it gives applicants time to assess commercial viability in foreign markets before committing to the substantial expense of international filing.

ⓘ
The Priority Date Is Everything

Under the AIA's first-inventor-to-file system, and under most international patent systems, the priority date determines which prior art counts against your application. A publication by a competitor that appears between your priority date and your foreign filing date does not invalidate your claims in most jurisdictions — as long as you file before the priority year expires. Protecting your priority date is the single most important deadline in international patent practice.

Foreign Filing License Required Before Filing Abroad

Under 35 U.S.C. § 184, applicants may not file a patent application in a foreign country for an invention made in the United States without first obtaining a foreign filing license from the USPTO — or waiting six months from the U.S. filing date. Violations can result in abandonment of the U.S. application and criminal penalties. Always check foreign filing license requirements with counsel before filing internationally, particularly for inventions with national security implications (defense AI, cryptography, satellite navigation).

2. The Patent Cooperation Treaty (PCT)

The Patent Cooperation Treaty (PCT), administered by WIPO in Geneva, is the single most important procedural instrument in international patent practice. Concluded in 1970 and entered into force in 1978, the PCT now has 153 contracting states — encompassing virtually every commercially significant patent jurisdiction in the world.

The PCT does not create a "world patent." What it does is establish a unified procedure that allows an applicant to file a single international application in one language, pay one set of international fees, and effectively designate all 153 member states simultaneously. The critical benefit is temporal: the PCT buys time. By filing one PCT application within twelve months of the priority filing, an applicant can defer the national phase entries — and the substantial national filing fees, translation costs, and attorney fees that come with them — for up to thirty months from the priority date.

The Two Phases of PCT Prosecution

Phase 1 — International Phase (Months 0–30)

The international phase begins when the PCT application is filed and encompasses all activity under WIPO's framework before national phase entry. Key events:

  • Filing: Submit the international application (PCT/RO/101) to a receiving office (e.g., USPTO, EPO, or WIPO directly) with the international filing fee, search fee, and transmittal fee.
  • International Search (ISA): An International Searching Authority (ISA) — typically the USPTO, EPO, JPO, or KIPO — searches for relevant prior art and produces an International Search Report (ISR) within 16 months of priority.
  • Written Opinion (WO/ISA): The ISA issues a non-binding Written Opinion on novelty, inventive step, and industrial applicability — effectively a preliminary assessment of patentability.
  • International Publication: WIPO publishes the application at 18 months from priority date. After publication, provisional patent protection may be available in many countries.
  • Chapter II Demand (optional): If the Written Opinion is unfavorable, applicants may file a Demand for International Preliminary Examination (IPEA) — effectively an optional second look at patentability before national phase entry.
Phase 2 — National Phase (30 months from priority)

At 30 months from priority, the applicant must enter the national phase in each desired country or regional patent office. This is when costs escalate significantly:

  • Pay national filing fees in each jurisdiction
  • Retain local patent attorneys or patent agents in each country
  • File certified translations (where required)
  • Respond to national office actions under local examination standards
  • Each jurisdiction then examines the application independently under its own substantive law
☀
The PCT's Core Value Proposition: Buy Time, Reduce Risk

For an AI startup assessing whether to pursue international protection, the PCT provides a disciplined decision framework. At month 12, you file the PCT application and pay a manageable set of international fees (typically $3,000–$5,000 total for a U.S.-origin filing). You then have until month 30 — an additional 18 months — to watch the market, assess commercial traction, see whether a competitor enters your space, and determine which specific countries are worth the cost of national phase entry. If the technology stalls or the market shifts, you can let the PCT application lapse at month 30 without having spent $50,000–$100,000 on national filings that will never be commercialized.

The International Search Report and Written Opinion

The International Search Report (ISR) is produced by the ISA and lists the prior art documents (Category A: anticipatory, Category X: novelty-defeating, Category Y: obviousness-relevant, Category E: prior documents, Category D: documents cited in the application) that the ISA searcher identified. The ISR is not binding on national phase examiners, but it serves as a roadmap for prosecution: it tells you what art you will face and gives you an opportunity to amend claims before national phase entry.

The Written Opinion (WO/ISA) accompanies the ISR and applies PCT Article 33 criteria — novelty, inventive step, and industrial applicability. A favorable Written Opinion is an asset: it creates prosecution history you can leverage in national phases, and some national offices give it substantial weight. An unfavorable Written Opinion signals the need for strategic claim amendment before national phase entry.

4. European Patent Protection

The European patent system offers some of the most commercially valuable protection available outside the United States — and some of the most nuanced doctrinal hurdles for AI and software inventions. Understanding the system requires grasping both the regional EPO layer and the national validation layer that follows.

The European Patent Convention Framework

The European Patent Convention (EPC), currently in its 2000 revision, governs the European patent system. The EPO examines applications under the EPC and, upon grant, issues a "bundle patent" — a single granted patent that must then be validated separately in each EPC member state by paying a national validation fee and, in most states, filing a certified translation of the claims into the national language. There are 39 EPC contracting states as of 2025, encompassing the major European economies.

The Unitary Patent: A Structural Shift (2023)

On June 1, 2023, the Unitary Patent system entered into force, representing the most significant structural reform of European patent law in decades. A patentee whose European patent is granted by the EPO may now request "unitary effect" within one month of grant, which creates a single patent with uniform protection across all participating EU member states — currently 17, with more expected to join.

The Unitary Patent dramatically reduces validation costs. Instead of paying validation fees in each member state (which could easily total €15,000–€30,000 for comprehensive European coverage), a single renewal fee is paid to the EPO. The Unified Patent Court (UPC) has exclusive jurisdiction over Unitary Patents and also has jurisdiction over classic European patents not opted out of the UPC system.

ⓘ
Brexit and the UK Patent Situation

The United Kingdom left the EU and therefore cannot participate in the Unitary Patent system or the Unified Patent Court. Post-Brexit, UK patent coverage requires either (a) a separate UK national patent application, (b) validation of a granted European patent at the UK Intellectual Property Office (UKIPO) with translation of claims into English (free, since English is accepted), or (c) entry through the national phase of a PCT application at the UKIPO. Companies with significant UK market presence — common in fintech, life sciences, and enterprise AI — must account for UK filing separately.

Software Patents in Europe: The "Technical Character" Requirement

Article 52(2) EPC explicitly excludes from patentability, among other things, mathematical methods, mental acts, and "programs for computers" — as such. The critical phrase is "as such." The EPO's Technical Boards of Appeal have held, consistently since Vicom (T 208/84) and refined through IBM/Computer program product (T 1173/97) and subsequent cases, that software claims are eligible if they produce a "further technical effect" beyond the normal physical interactions between software and the hardware on which it runs.

For AI inventions at the EPO, the relevant question is whether the AI method produces a technical effect that serves a technical purpose. This is assessed using the technical problem-solution approach: the claim must define a technical problem and a technical solution to that problem. An AI model that improves image compression efficiency, reduces noise in a signal, or optimizes network routing presents a technical problem with a technical solution. An AI model that predicts financial market movements, generates creative text, or classifies business entities is more likely to be found non-technical — even if it runs on hardware.

Practical implication for AI patent drafting at the EPO: Claims should be structured to emphasize the technical character of the invention. Method claims should specify the technical effect achieved (e.g., "reducing computational complexity," "improving signal-to-noise ratio," "enabling real-time processing with bounded memory"). The description should frame the technical problem solved — not just the business or commercial problem — and the claims should be tied to specific technical implementation details rather than functional outcomes.

Comparison: EPO Article 52 vs. U.S. Alice Doctrine

🇺🇸 USPTO / Alice

  • Two-step Mayo/Alice framework
  • Step 1: Is the claim directed to an abstract idea, law of nature, or natural phenomenon?
  • Step 2A: Does the claim integrate the exception into a practical application?
  • Step 2B (if Step 2A fails): Does the claim add "significantly more"?
  • Outcome: Highly uncertain — fact-specific, subjective "abstract idea" analysis
  • AI impact: Substantial rejection rate; functional claiming under fire

🇪🇺 EPO / Article 52

  • Exclusions are listed in Art. 52(2) but apply only "as such"
  • Technical character test: Does the claim have a technical character?
  • Technical contribution test: Does the invention make a technical contribution to the art?
  • Problem-solution approach: Is the inventive step assessed over technical prior art?
  • Outcome: More predictable — "technical" vs. "non-technical" is cleaner than "abstract idea"
  • AI impact: AI tied to hardware or physical processes generally passes; pure statistical methods fail

EPO Opposition Proceedings

One of the most powerful (and most used) post-grant tools in European patent practice is the opposition proceeding. Within nine months of the date of publication of the grant of a European patent, any third party may file an opposition at the EPO on grounds of lack of novelty, lack of inventive step, added matter, insufficiency of disclosure, or subject matter excluded under Article 52. Opposition is cheap relative to litigation — the EPO opposition filing fee is approximately €815 — and highly effective: roughly one-third of opposed European patents are revoked outright, and over half are maintained in amended form.

For AI companies, the opposition proceeding is both a risk and an opportunity. Your competitors can use opposition to challenge your European AI patents at modest cost with significant effect. Conversely, you can use opposition to challenge competitor patents before they become enforcement tools. Given the growing number of AI patents being granted by the EPO, opposition filings in the AI technology sector have increased sharply since 2021.

5. China Patent Strategy

China's rise to become the world's largest AI patent filer is not accidental — it reflects deliberate national policy. The State Council's "New Generation Artificial Intelligence Development Plan" (2017) set explicit targets for AI patent output as a metric of technological leadership. CNIPA (the China National Intellectual Property Administration, which replaced SIPO in 2018) has responded with updated AI examination guidelines, fast-track examination programs, and a surge in patent grant rates for AI-related inventions.

The Chinese Patent System Overview

China offers three types of patent protection:

  • Invention patents (发明专利): Full substantive examination; 20-year term; equivalent to utility patents in the U.S. and EP. AI methods and systems are filed as invention patents.
  • Utility model patents (实用新型专利): No substantive examination; 10-year term; limited to structural features of a product (not methods). Useful for hardware implementations but not for software or AI methods.
  • Design patents (外观设计专利): UI/UX designs; 15-year term since 2021 patent law revision.

For AI companies, invention patents are the relevant category. The key eligibility concept under Chinese patent law is the "technical solution" requirement: under Article 2 of the Patent Law, an invention must constitute a "technical solution utilizing natural laws to solve a technical problem." Methods of mental activity and abstract mathematical rules are excluded — but an AI method that operates on technical data (sensor inputs, hardware parameters, network traffic) to solve a technical problem is generally eligible.

💡
CNIPA 2021 Draft AI Examination Guidelines — Key Provisions

CNIPA's 2021 draft guidelines on computer program-related inventions (issued for public comment) addressed AI patents specifically. Key provisions relevant to AI patent drafting:

  • Neural network model claims: Acceptable if the neural network architecture and training method are defined with sufficient technical specificity to be distinguished from the prior art.
  • Training data claims: Claims to datasets or training data compilations may be eligible as computer-implemented inventions if tied to a specific technical use.
  • AI application claims: Claims should recite the specific technical field of application (medical imaging, industrial inspection, autonomous driving) to anchor the "technical solution" requirement.
  • Functional language: Permitted, but must be supported by the specification with concrete implementation details.

Prosecution Tips for AI Patents in China

  1. Use method + system + computer-readable medium ("tri-claim") structure. Chinese examiners generally allow all three claim types in a single application, providing broader coverage than some other jurisdictions.
  2. Emphasize the technical problem and technical solution. Unlike the U.S., where "practical application" is the touchstone, Chinese eligibility analysis centers on the "technical solution" to a "technical problem." Explicitly frame the invention this way in both the claims and the specification.
  3. File both Chinese-language and English-language descriptions. While Chinese is required, filing an English description concurrently (as an annex or in parallel) helps ensure the Chinese translation is accurate and complete.
  4. Use CNIPA's fast-track examination programs. CNIPA offers prioritized examination (优先审查) for key technical areas including AI, reducing prosecution time from ~22 months to ~6 months for qualifying applications.
  5. Consider filing utility model alongside invention patent. Where the AI system has a structural hardware implementation (dedicated inference chip, training appliance), filing a utility model provides fast protection (no substantive examination; grant in ~6 months) while the invention patent is examined.
  6. Monitor continuation-equivalent practice. China does not have continuation applications in the U.S. sense, but divisional applications and voluntary amendment practice can be used to create related applications covering improvements.

Joint Ventures and IP Protection Concerns

Foreign companies operating in China — particularly through joint ventures (JVs) or technology licensing arrangements — face distinct IP risks that patent strategy must address. These include contractual assignment of improvements to the JV, technology transfer obligations under regulatory approval conditions, and the risk that locally-created improvements or derivative works may be owned by the Chinese JV partner under joint ownership rules.

Best practices for AI companies entering the Chinese market include: (1) filing Chinese national patent applications before entering JV negotiations; (2) ensuring AI model weights, training data, and core algorithms are maintained as trade secrets separate from any technology transferred; (3) using contractual provisions specifying ownership of AI-generated improvements; and (4) registering patents with CNIPA before entering China operations to establish documented prior ownership.

6. Japan Patent Strategy

Japan is consistently among the top three patent jurisdictions globally, and the Japanese market for AI — spanning robotics, automotive, manufacturing, healthcare, and consumer electronics — is among the most commercially significant in the world. The JPO has invested substantially in developing AI-specific examination guidelines and has positioned Japan as a leading jurisdiction for AI patent protection.

JPO AI Examination Guidelines: "Creations Utilizing AI"

In January 2019, the JPO issued landmark examination guidelines specifically addressing AI-related inventions. These guidelines introduced the category of "creations utilizing AI" and addressed patentability from both the eligibility and inventive step perspectives. The guidelines were updated in 2024 to address generative AI and large language models.

Under Japanese patent law, computer software patents are eligible when "information processing by software is concretely realized using hardware resources." The JPO applies this test under Article 2(1) of the Patent Act: an "creation of technical ideas utilizing natural laws." An AI method that implements specific computational steps on identified hardware — even if the hardware is general-purpose — will generally meet this threshold.

For AI-specific inventions, the JPO guidelines specify that the following claim types are acceptable:

  • Method claims for machine learning training procedures (with specified training data types, loss functions, and hardware configurations)
  • System claims for AI inference systems (with defined model architecture, input/output specifications)
  • Computer program claims (stored on computer-readable media)
  • Data structure claims (for novel training datasets or structured AI outputs with defined technical applications)

Fast Examination Programs at the JPO

The JPO offers several acceleration programs particularly valuable for AI companies:

  • Super Accelerated Examination: For applicants who have not filed a foreign application (or who will not do so). Target examination within 3 months of request.
  • Accelerated Examination: For applicants filing in Japan first, or where foreign application has been filed. Target examination within 6 months.
  • PPH (Patent Prosecution Highway): Where a corresponding application has been allowed at a partner office (USPTO, EPO, KIPO, CNIPA, etc.), JPO examination can be accelerated using the foreign office's work product. Average grant time under PPH: approximately 12 months.
  • Early publication: Japan defaults to 18-month publication, with an option to request earlier publication — useful for applicants wishing to establish prior art dates or stimulate licensing inquiries.
☀
Japan as a High-Value AI Market: Strategic Filing Priority

Japan should be near the top of any "Global Five" international filing strategy for AI companies in sectors including: industrial robotics and automation, automotive AI systems (Toyota, Honda, Nissan), semiconductor and chip design (Renesas, Kioxia), precision manufacturing, and medical device AI. Japanese corporations are also significant licensees — having strong Japanese patent coverage creates cross-licensing leverage with companies that generate substantial royalty income from U.S. operations. The JPO's relatively fast prosecution timeline and reasonable costs make it one of the most cost-efficient major jurisdictions for AI patent protection.

7. Korea, India, and Emerging Markets

South Korea (KIPO)

🇰🇷 Korea Intellectual Property Office (KIPO)

South Korea is the fourth-largest AI patent filer globally, driven primarily by Samsung Electronics, LG Electronics, SK Hynix, and a growing number of AI-native startups. KIPO issued AI-specific examination guidelines in 2021 that take a relatively permissive approach to software and AI patent eligibility.

Key KIPO AI patent features:

  • Computer program claims accepted when the program causes specific technical effects on hardware
  • AI model training method claims generally eligible without significant eligibility hurdles (similar to JPO approach)
  • Claim-by-claim examination approach allows mixed eligible/ineligible claim sets
  • Priority Examination program: grants within 3–4 months for qualifying applications
  • KIPO-USPTO PPH: substantial prosecution acceleration for applications with USPTO allowances
  • Annual maintenance fees are among the lowest of the major five offices

Strategic note: Korea is particularly important for companies in the semiconductor, memory, display, and consumer electronics AI sectors. The Korean domestic market is large, concentrated, and dominated by players who are themselves major patent holders — cross-licensing is common.

India (IPO/CGPDTM)

🇮🇳 Indian Patent Office (IPO)

India presents a distinct patent landscape for AI companies. Section 3(k) of the Indian Patents Act explicitly excludes from patentability "a mathematical or business method or a computer programme per se or algorithms." The phrase "per se" creates ambiguity — and the Indian Patent Office has historically applied Section 3(k) broadly, rejecting many software and AI method claims.

Prosecution strategy for AI patents in India:

  • Claim AI methods as implemented on hardware (system claims perform better than method claims in India)
  • Emphasize technical application and physical effect — claims tied to specific technical domains (medical imaging, industrial sensing) fare better
  • Computer-implemented inventions with a "novel hardware configuration" are more likely to be allowed
  • India's prosecution timeline is historically slow: 5–7 years is not unusual, though reforms since 2020 have improved throughput
  • India does not participate in the Unitary Patent and has a distinct national examination framework

Strategic note: India is a rapidly growing AI market, home to major technology outsourcing operations for U.S. and EU companies. For AI companies with significant India revenue or operations, patent protection — despite the challenges — is strategically important. Given India's growing role as an AI services hub, trade secret protection is often more practically enforceable than patents in the near term.

Strategic Jurisdiction Selection: Decision Framework

No AI company has unlimited IP budget. Selecting which jurisdictions to pursue requires a disciplined decision framework that weighs commercial value against prosecution cost and risk. The following factors should drive jurisdiction selection:

  1. Revenue and market presence: File where you sell, license, or derive revenue. If 40% of your revenue comes from Europe, the EPO is non-negotiable.
  2. Manufacturing and supply chain: File where your product or the infringing product is manufactured. Process patents are infringed where the process is practiced.
  3. Competitor presence: File where your primary competitors are headquartered. Domestic patents create licensing leverage in cross-license negotiations.
  4. Technical field: Semiconductor and electronics AI → Korea and Japan. Industrial AI → Germany (via EPO). Pharmaceutical AI → EU, Japan, Australia. Consumer AI → US, China, EU.
  5. Enforcement availability: Patents are only as good as the courts that enforce them. The U.S. ITC, German regional courts, and the new Unified Patent Court offer strong and relatively fast enforcement. Some jurisdictions offer nominal rights but weak enforcement infrastructure.

8. The PCT Process Step by Step

The following process steps walk through a complete international filing from initial priority application through national phase grant, with practical guidance at each stage for AI and ML patent applicants.

  1. File the Priority Application in the Home Country

    File a provisional (U.S.) or utility patent application — or equivalent — in your home jurisdiction. This establishes the priority date under Article 4 of the Paris Convention. For U.S.-origin AI inventions, a well-drafted provisional application with complete technical disclosure (architecture diagrams, training methods, benchmark data) is strongly preferred over a bare-bones placeholder. The priority application's disclosure limits what you can claim internationally — new matter cannot be added. Key deadline: This filing starts the 12-month Paris Convention clock.

  2. File the PCT Application Within 12 Months

    Before the one-year anniversary of the priority filing, file the PCT application (PCT/RO/101) at a receiving office. Typically this is the USPTO (if a U.S. applicant), EPO, or WIPO directly. The application must include: the description, claims (in the filing language), abstract, drawings, inventor information, and designation of states. Select your International Searching Authority (ISA) at this stage — your choice of ISA determines which examiner searches your claims and which national law standard is applied in the Written Opinion. For AI inventions, EPO as ISA typically provides the most thorough prior art search; USPTO as ISA typically produces faster results. Key deadline: PCT filing must be within 12 months of priority date.

  3. International Search Phase — ISR and Written Opinion

    The ISA searches global patent and non-patent literature (including arXiv, conference proceedings, and technical standards — highly relevant for AI) and produces the International Search Report (ISR) and Written Opinion (WO/ISA). Upon receiving the ISR, carefully review the cited prior art. If the Written Opinion indicates §101/Art. 52 issues or significant prior art, prepare a responsive amendment or "Article 19 amendment" to the claims before publication. Article 19 amendments are filed within two months of ISR receipt and are published alongside the application. Key deadline: Article 19 amendments due within 2 months of ISR issuance.

  4. Chapter II Demand — Optional Preliminary Examination

    If the Written Opinion is unfavorable, consider filing a Chapter II Demand for International Preliminary Examination. This triggers a second examination cycle before an International Preliminary Examining Authority (IPEA) — usually the same body as the ISA. You can file written arguments and "Article 34 amendments" in response to a preliminary examination report (IPER/A). A positive IPEA report (IPER) stating "YES" to novelty, inventive step, and industrial applicability carries weight in national phases, particularly in Japan, Korea, and Australia. Chapter II is optional and adds fees (~$600 for a U.S. applicant, large entity) but can be valuable for borderline cases. Key deadline: Demand must be filed before 22 months from priority.

  5. National Phase Entry at 30 Months

    By the 30-month deadline (counted from the earliest priority date), you must enter the national phase in each jurisdiction where you seek protection. National phase entry requires: (1) payment of the national filing fee, (2) appointment of local patent counsel, (3) filing of certified translations (in non-English jurisdictions), and (4) any specific national formality documents. Some countries allow early national phase entry (e.g., if you need an earlier grant for licensing or enforcement) — this can be requested before the 30-month deadline. Key deadline: 30 months from priority date (31 months in some jurisdictions — verify country-by-country).

  6. Individual Country Examination

    Each national or regional patent office examines the application independently under its own substantive law. The ISR and WO/ISA are not binding, but most examiners review them. Prosecution strategy differs by jurisdiction: U.S. prosecution requires Alice/§101 vigilance; EPO prosecution requires technical character arguments; Chinese prosecution should emphasize the technical solution framework. In each jurisdiction, respond to office actions with claim amendments and arguments tailored to local examination standards. This phase typically takes 1–4 years per jurisdiction. Use PPH wherever available to accelerate examination (see Section 10).

  7. Grant and Maintenance

    Upon grant, each patent requires ongoing maintenance fee payments at regular intervals (annually in most jurisdictions; every 3.5 years at USPTO) to keep the patent in force. A patent that lapses for non-payment of maintenance fees becomes permanently abandoned and enters the public domain. For a global AI patent family covering the "Big Five" jurisdictions (US + EP + CN + JP + KR), total maintenance costs over the 20-year patent term can reach $80,000–$120,000. Patent portfolio management software (Anaqua, Dennemeyer, CPA Global) is essential for tracking maintenance deadlines across large international portfolios.

An abandoned patent reduces budget drag; a patent allowed to lapse accidentally due to missed deadlines is an unrecoverable loss.

10. Patent Prosecution Highway (PPH)

The Patent Prosecution Highway (PPH) is one of the most powerful cost-reduction and time-acceleration tools available to international patent applicants. The program allows an applicant whose claims have been found allowable (or patentable, in PCT context) by one participating patent office — the "Office of Earlier Examination" (OEE) — to request accelerated examination in a partner office — the "Office of Later Examination" (OLE) — based on the work product from the first office.

The logic is simple and effective: if the USPTO has examined a set of claims and found them allowable over the prior art, the JPO need not duplicate that search from scratch. Under PPH, the JPO examiner can leverage the USPTO's search and examination work, examine only the claims that correspond to the allowed U.S. claims, and issue a first office action — or in many cases a notice of allowance — in weeks rather than years.

How to Use PPH Strategically

The most effective PPH strategy for AI companies follows a sequence of deliberate steps:

  1. Optimize claims for allowance in the "lead office." Choose one jurisdiction — typically the USPTO or EPO, depending on where the technology is most clearly patentable — as the lead office and focus prosecution resources on securing a clean allowance quickly. Use Track One (USPTO) or PACE (EPO) to accelerate the first allowance.
  2. Align foreign claims to allowed claims. Upon allowance in the lead office, amend the claims in each foreign national phase to correspond to (or narrow to) the allowed claims. This is the prerequisite for PPH: the foreign claims must "sufficiently correspond" to the allowed lead-office claims.
  3. File PPH requests at national phase entry. When entering each national phase, simultaneously file the PPH request with a copy of the lead-office allowance, the allowed claims, and a claim correspondence table. Many offices accept PPH requests filed concurrently with national phase entry.
  4. Use PCT-PPH where available. A positive International Preliminary Examination Report (IPER/Chapter II) with a "YES" opinion on all claims enables PCT-PPH at all participating offices — making the PCT Chapter II investment highly cost-effective if PPH savings are factored in.

The London Agreement: Reducing European Translation Burdens

The London Agreement on the Application of Article 65 EPC, in force since 2008, allows EPC member states to partially waive the translation requirement for European patents. States participating in the London Agreement (including Germany, France, the UK, Switzerland, the Netherlands, and others) agree to either: (a) waive translation entirely if the patent is granted in one of the EPO's official languages, or (b) require only a translation of the claims (not the full specification) into their national language.

This substantially reduces European validation costs for English-language applicants. For a European patent granted in English and validated in London Agreement states, only claims translations (rather than full specification translations) are required in states that have the national language as an official EPO language. Non-London Agreement states — including Italy, Spain, Portugal, Greece, and several Eastern European states — still require full specification translations.

Machine Translation vs. Certified Human Translation

The quality and acceptability of machine-translated patent text has improved dramatically with neural machine translation (NMT) models, and the irony is not lost that the same AI technologies being patented are now materially improving the translation process. However, the following distinctions remain important:

  • Official filing translations must be human-certified. All major patent offices require translations to be certified as accurate by a qualified translator. An uncertified machine translation output does not meet this requirement and will be rejected.
  • AI-assisted translation (human-in-the-loop) is standard practice. Most professional patent translation firms now use DeepL, WIPO's PatentScope machine translation engine, or specialized patent NMT tools as a first-pass draft, which is then reviewed, corrected, and certified by a qualified human translator. This hybrid approach reduces cost and turnaround time without sacrificing the human certification required by patent offices.
  • WIPO's Patent Scope translation tool is available free of charge and supports translations across 10 language pairs relevant to patent prosecution. It is useful for internal review and prior art research, but the output is not suitable for official filing without professional review.
  • Critical claims language requires specialist review. The exact language of patent claims — particularly for AI inventions with nuanced technical terminology — requires a translator with both linguistic proficiency and technical domain expertise. A mistranslation in a claim can narrow scope, create prosecution history estoppel, or render the claim unenforceable. Do not use generic translation services for claims.
☀
Budget Tip: Stage Your Translations

For PCT applicants, translation costs can be staged to align with commercial milestones. The PCT system allows filing in English with deferred translations in Japan (2-month English provisional) and Korea (14-month delayed Korean translation). This means you can enter the national phase at month 30 in Japan and Korea with an English provisional, then commit to the translation cost only after confirming commercial traction in those markets — effectively buying an additional 2–14 months of optionality on translation spend.

12. International Filing Checklist

The following checklist covers the key planning and execution steps for a comprehensive international patent filing strategy. Items are organized by phase: pre-filing strategy, PCT filing, international phase management, and national phase execution.

Pre-Filing Strategy

  • Identify all target jurisdictions based on revenue, manufacturing, competitor presence, and market strategy
  • Classify the invention by technology area to determine jurisdiction-specific eligibility risks (especially software/AI under Alice, Art. 52 EPC, Section 3(k) India)
  • Confirm foreign filing license from USPTO (35 U.S.C. § 184) or confirm 6-month waiting period has elapsed
  • Identify all inventors by country of residence — ensure proper inventorship in each jurisdiction (inventorship rules differ: U.S. requires all joint inventors; some jurisdictions permit employer as named applicant)
  • File provisional (U.S.) or utility application with complete technical disclosure — do not skimp on specification detail, as it limits what can be claimed internationally
  • Confirm written assignments from all inventors to the corporate applicant (chain of title must be clean before national phase entry in most jurisdictions)
  • Choose target ISA for PCT filing (EPO = thorough search; USPTO = fast; JPO = strong electronics/AI)
  • Build a 12-month budget model covering PCT filing fees, translation estimates, and national phase entry cost projections by jurisdiction

PCT Filing (Month 12 from Priority)

  • File PCT application before 12-month Paris Convention anniversary — verify exact date and build in a safety buffer of at least 2 weeks
  • Verify all formal requirements of the receiving office (signature, power of attorney, inventor declarations)
  • File well-structured claims covering independent method, system, and CRM (computer-readable medium) claim types
  • Include claims of varying scope (broad, medium, and narrow) to preserve prosecution options internationally
  • Designate all contracting states (default under current PCT rules, but confirm designation)
  • Confirm receipt of filing receipt and international application number (PCT/XX/YYYY/NNNNNN format)

International Phase Management (Months 12–30)

  • Docket and track the 16-month ISR deadline — create calendar alerts at the ISA
  • Upon receipt of ISR: analyze cited prior art, identify potential §103/Art. 56 vulnerabilities, assess Written Opinion
  • Evaluate Article 19 amendment (claims only) within 2 months of ISR to improve WO/ISA before national phase
  • Assess whether Chapter II Demand is warranted — file by month 22 if proceeding
  • Begin national phase preparation at month 24 — do not wait until month 30 to engage local counsel in each jurisdiction
  • Confirm translation deadlines for Japan (2-month provisional English) and Korea (14-month delayed Korean) to stage costs
  • Track WIPO publication at month 18 — verify publication is accurate and complete
  • Assess whether any publication-triggering events (commercialization, press releases) are imminent in target jurisdictions — confirm provisional protection status

National Phase Execution (Month 30 onward)

  • Enter national phase at each selected jurisdiction by month 30 (or 31 where available)
  • File PPH requests simultaneously with national phase entry where USPTO or EPO allowance is in hand
  • File certified translations into required national languages (Chinese, Japanese, Korean) — use AI-assisted + human review workflow
  • Engage local patent agents with AI/ML subject-matter expertise in each jurisdiction — generic IP firms without technical staff will produce weaker prosecution
  • Conduct jurisdiction-specific claim amendments for EPO (technical character), CNIPA (technical solution), and India (non-software per se) before first office action to front-load prosecution efficiency
  • Set up patent docketing system (or service) to track all office action response deadlines, annuity due dates, and grant publication dates across all jurisdictions
  • Implement portfolio review process: annually assess whether each in-progress or granted foreign case aligns with current commercial strategy — abandon non-strategic cases before the next annuity payment is due
  • Upon grant in any jurisdiction: evaluate whether Unitary Patent request is appropriate (EPO-granted patents, within 1 month of grant)
  • Upon first grant (typically Korea or China): assess PPH eligibility in remaining jurisdictions and file PPH requests immediately
  • Track opposition deadlines for EPO grants (9 months from publication of grant) — assess whether competitors are filing oppositions; consider filing opposition against competitor AI patents within their 9-month window
⚠
Missed PCT Deadlines Are Non-Recoverable

The 12-month Paris Convention priority deadline and the 30-month PCT national phase entry deadline are absolute. Unlike some USPTO deadlines that can be revived with a petition and showing of unintentional delay, a missed priority year results in the permanent loss of priority rights internationally — meaning any publications between your priority date and the late filing date can be used as prior art against you. A missed national phase entry deadline results in the permanent abandonment of your application in that jurisdiction — the application cannot be revived except in very limited circumstances. Build multi-calendar systems, legal docketing software, and redundant attorney reminders around these two deadlines. No exception.

Ongoing Portfolio Management

  • Maintain a master patent family spreadsheet tracking status in each jurisdiction, next deadline, last action, and annuity due dates
  • Review and pay annuity (maintenance) fees in each jurisdiction — automate through a patent renewals service (Dennemeyer, CPA Global, Anaqua) for portfolios with 20+ patents
  • Conduct biennial "portfolio pruning" to abandon patents that no longer map to products, revenue, or licensing targets — each abandoned patent saves ongoing maintenance spend
  • Monitor foreign competitor patent grants in your technology space — consider opposition (EPO) or inter partes review equivalents (Japan, Korea) against patents that threaten FTO
  • Track national law changes — AI patent examination guidelines are evolving rapidly in China, Japan, Korea, and India; a change in examination standards may affect pending applications

Chapter Summary

International patent protection for AI and ML companies is not optional — it is a structural requirement for any company operating in global markets. The absence of a "world patent" means that protection must be assembled jurisdiction by jurisdiction, each with its own substantive law, procedural requirements, and cost structure. The PCT provides the essential tool for managing this complexity: a single international application that preserves optionality across 153 countries while deferring the major cost commitments to the 30-month national phase entry.

The "Global Five" strategy — USPTO, EPO, CNIPA, JPO, KIPO — covers the jurisdictions where the vast majority of commercially significant AI activity occurs, where leading AI companies are headquartered, and where patent enforcement infrastructure is most robust. Total lifecycle cost for a Global Five family is $150,000–$275,000 over 20 years — a substantial but manageable commitment when weighed against the commercial value of the underlying AI technology.

The Patent Prosecution Highway dramatically reduces both time and cost when used strategically: secure one allowance in a lead jurisdiction, align claims in foreign offices, and file PPH requests to collapse a 2–4-year prosecution timeline into months. Combined with thoughtful translation staging and active portfolio pruning, these tools give AI companies the ability to build genuinely global patent protection on budgets that would have been impossible two decades ago.

File where your customers are, where your competitors operate, and where courts will actually enforce your rights. Everything else is overhead." — Prof. Peter K.
