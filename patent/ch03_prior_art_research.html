You have spent three years building a transformer-based drug-target binding predictor. You believe it is novel. Your team is ready to file. Before you do, there is a question you absolutely must answer: has anyone done this before?

Prior art research is the unglamorous foundation of all patent strategy. It determines whether your application will survive examination, informs how broadly you can claim your invention, and — critically — tells you whether shipping your product will expose you to infringement liability. This chapter teaches you to do it right: systematically, efficiently, and with the rigor that courts and examiners demand.

ⓘ
Chapter Scope

This chapter covers both patentability searches (can I get a patent?) and freedom-to-operate searches (can I ship without infringing?). These are related but distinct inquiries requiring different methodologies. We cover both, with AI/ML and biotech examples throughout.

1. Why Prior Art Matters

Prior art is the legal universe of everything that was publicly known before your invention's critical date. Two fundamental patent requirements flow directly from the prior art landscape:

  • Novelty (35 U.S.C. § 102): Your invention must not have been disclosed anywhere — even in one prior-art reference — before the critical date. A single reference that describes every element of your claimed invention destroys novelty outright. This is called anticipation.
  • Non-obviousness (35 U.S.C. § 103): Even if no single reference discloses your entire invention, the patent office can combine multiple references to argue that your invention would have been obvious to a person having ordinary skill in the art (PHOSITA) at the time of filing. This is the most common rejection AI/ML patents face.

The Dual Role of Prior Art Research

Prior art research serves two distinct strategic purposes that technologists often conflate:

Patentability Search

Question: Can I obtain a patent on my invention?

Scope: Worldwide prior art in all languages

Goal: Find the closest art so you can draft claims around it

Timing: Before drafting the application

Who uses it: Inventor + patent attorney/agent

Freedom-to-Operate (FTO) Search

Question: Can I make/use/sell my product without infringing?

Scope: In-force patents in jurisdictions where you operate

Goal: Find patents whose claims read on your product

Timing: Before commercial launch

Who uses it: Business + IP counsel

The AIA Timeline: What Counts as Prior Art

The America Invents Act (AIA), effective March 16, 2013, fundamentally changed U.S. prior art rules. Whether AIA or pre-AIA law applies depends on whether any claim in the application has an effective filing date on or after March 16, 2013.

⚠
The Inventor's Own Work as Prior Art

Under AIA, if you publish your research (e.g., post to arXiv or publish at NeurIPS) and then wait more than 12 months before filing, your own publication becomes prior art against your own patent application. The clock starts from the first public disclosure — including a conference presentation, a blog post, or even a public GitHub commit. File before you publish, or file within 12 months of your first disclosure.

Why AI/ML Patents Face Heightened Prior Art Scrutiny

The AI/ML field presents unique prior art challenges. The academic publication cycle is extraordinarily fast — arXiv alone publishes hundreds of machine learning papers per week. A technique that seems novel in January may have been described in a preprint in December. Additionally, open-source code repositories (GitHub, HuggingFace) constitute prior art once publicly accessible, even without a corresponding academic paper.

🚫
The Helsinn Trap: Secret Commercial Sales Still Bar Patents

In Helsinn Healthcare v. Teva Pharmaceuticals (2019), the Supreme Court held that the on-sale bar under AIA applies even when the sale is confidential and the details of the invention are not disclosed publicly. This means that if your company entered into a commercial arrangement (e.g., an API licensing agreement) for an unpatented AI/ML system before filing, that secret commercial activity can still bar patentability. Document the timeline of your commercial activities meticulously.

4. Classification Systems

Patent classification systems organize the entire patent literature into a hierarchical taxonomy. Mastering the relevant classification codes for your technology area transforms you from a keyword searcher into a systematic prior art professional. Keyword searches miss patents that use different terminology; classification searches catch everything in the technical area regardless of words used.

CPC vs. IPC: The Two Major Systems

The Cooperative Patent Classification (CPC) is a joint system developed by the USPTO and EPO. It is more granular than the International Patent Classification (IPC) and is the primary system used for AI/ML patent searches. The International Patent Classification (IPC) is administered by WIPO and used by over 100 patent offices; CPC codes are typically aligned with IPC but with additional subdivisions.

ⓘ
CPC Code Structure

CPC codes follow a hierarchical structure: Section → Class → Subclass → Group → Subgroup. For example, G06N 3/04 breaks down as: G = Physics, 06 = Computing/Calculating, N = Computing by non-arithmetic methods, 3 = Neural networks, /04 = Architectures. The deeper you go, the more specific the art. Always search at multiple hierarchy levels.

How to Find the Right CPC Code

If you are not sure which CPC codes apply to your invention, use these three techniques:

  1. Look at the closest known patent: Find any patent you know is in your space (even an imperfect one) and check its CPC codes in the front page data. These codes are your starting point.
  2. Use the CPC scheme browser: The USPTO hosts the full CPC scheme at https://www.uspto.gov/web/patents/classification/cpc/html/cpc.html. Browse the G06N subclass definitions to find matching codes.
  3. Run a keyword search first, extract codes: Do a preliminary keyword search in Google Patents or Espacenet, export the top 20 results, and run frequency analysis on their CPC codes. The most common codes in your result set are usually the right ones.

5. Search Strategy for AI/ML Patents

A systematic search strategy prevents two failure modes: the too-narrow search that misses the closest prior art, and the too-broad search that generates thousands of irrelevant results. The following six-step process is the one we use at Fish & Richardson for pre-filing patentability opinions in the AI/ML space.

  1. Define Key Technical Concepts

    Before touching any database, write a precise technical description of your invention in 3–5 sentences. Identify: (a) the problem being solved, (b) the technical approach — architecture, algorithm, training method, data structure, (c) the key distinguishing features that differ from the standard/prior approach, and (d) the result or improvement achieved. For each concept, brainstorm synonyms — "attention mechanism," "self-attention," "transformer attention," "query-key-value attention" all describe the same thing and may appear differently in prior art.

  2. Identify Relevant CPC Classification Codes

    Using the technique in Section 4, identify 3–8 CPC codes that cover your invention. For a drug-target binding predictor using a graph neural network: G06N 3/04 (neural networks), G06N 20/00 (ML general), G06N 3/08 (learning methods), C12N 15/00 (molecular biology), and G16H 70/40 (drug repurposing). Record these codes in your search log. Verify by browsing each code in Espacenet to confirm documents in the code match your technology area.

  3. Build Structured Keyword Queries

    Construct Boolean queries using the technical vocabulary you mapped in Step 1. Structure queries from broad to narrow. Start with the most specific query (all key concepts), review results, then broaden by removing terms one at a time. Use proximity operators (NEAR, ADJ) available in USPTO and Espacenet to find terms appearing near each other. Record every query you run, the database used, and the result count in your search log.

  4. Search Forward and Backward Citations

    Once you find relevant patents, follow the citation graph in both directions. Backward citations (references cited in the patent) lead to earlier foundational art. Forward citations (patents that cite this patent) show subsequent developments that may include your invention. Google Patents and Lens.org display both citation directions visually. Key rule: never analyze a patent in isolation — always trace at least one generation of citations in both directions.

  5. Screen and Cluster Results

    From your combined search results, apply a two-pass screening process. First pass: scan titles and abstracts; discard clearly irrelevant documents. Second pass: read the claims and abstract of surviving candidates; identify "hot" references (documents disclosing 3+ elements of your invention). Cluster hot references by technical approach. This clustering reveals the prior art landscape and identifies where the gaps — and your patentable distinctions — may lie.

  6. Map Claims to Your Invention

    For each hot reference, perform a formal claim mapping exercise (see Section 6). Create a matrix: rows are the elements of your proposed claims, columns are the cited references. For each cell, note whether the reference discloses that element. References that map to every element of a proposed claim are anticipatory — your claim must be distinguished over them. References that map to some elements can be combined under a §103 obviousness analysis. Use this mapping to refine your claim scope before drafting.

6. Reading and Analyzing Patent Claims

Claims are the legal heart of a patent — the only part that defines the exclusive right. Everything else (specification, drawings, abstract) exists to support, explain, and interpret the claims. As a prior art researcher, you must read claims precisely and map them against your own technology with surgical accuracy.

Independent vs. Dependent Claims

Every patent has a hierarchy of claims:

  • Independent claims stand alone. They define the broadest version of the invention. If your product does not infringe the independent claim, it cannot infringe any dependent claim that depends from it.
  • Dependent claims incorporate all limitations of the claim(s) they depend from, then add more. They are narrower, but they provide fallback scope if the independent claim is invalidated.
  • Multiple-dependent claims depend from more than one prior claim. Common in European-origin applications; rarer in U.S. practice due to additional fees.
Annotated Claim Example — Hypothetical AI Drug Discovery Patent

Claim 1. [INDEPENDENT] A computer-implemented method for predicting protein-ligand binding affinity, the method comprising:

[Preamble] (sets context; "comprising" means open-ended — additional elements OK)

receiving, by at least one processor, a molecular graph representation of a candidate ligand, wherein the molecular graph comprises a plurality of nodes representing atoms and a plurality of edges representing chemical bonds;

[Element 1] (defines the input data structure — must match in both literal infringement and FTO analysis)

applying a graph neural network comprising at least three message-passing layers to the molecular graph to generate a ligand embedding vector;

[Element 2] (defines the model architecture — "at least three" is a quantitative limitation; two layers would not infringe)

concatenating the ligand embedding vector with a protein sequence embedding derived from a pre-trained protein language model; and

[Element 3] (defines the fusion step — the protein embedding source matters for FTO)

outputting, by a prediction head, a binding affinity score representing the predicted binding between the candidate ligand and the protein.

[Element 4] (defines the output — "binding affinity score" is a specific claim term requiring precise construction)

Claim 2. [DEPENDENT] The method of claim 1, wherein the graph neural network is an attentive fingerprint network.

[Adds limitation] — narrows to a specific GNN architecture; if claim 1 is invalid, claim 2 may survive as a distinct invention

Claim 8. [INDEPENDENT — System] A system for predicting protein-ligand binding affinity, the system comprising: at least one processor; and memory storing instructions that, when executed by the at least one processor, perform the method of any one of claims 1–7.

[System claim] — parallel protection for the hardware/software combination; same scope as method claims

Literal Infringement vs. Doctrine of Equivalents

Patent infringement is found in two ways:

  • Literal infringement: Every element of at least one claim is present in the accused product exactly as written. This is the easiest analysis — read the claim, look at the product. A perfect word-for-word match.
  • Doctrine of equivalents (DoE): Even if your product does not literally infringe, it may infringe under DoE if the accused element performs substantially the same function, in substantially the same way, to achieve substantially the same result as the claimed element. This is the function-way-result test from Graver Tank v. Linde Air Products (1950).
⚠
Prosecution History Estoppel Limits DoE

If a patent applicant narrowed a claim during prosecution to overcome a prior art rejection, they are estopped from arguing DoE to recapture the surrendered scope (Festo Corp. v. Shoketsu Kinzoku Kogyo Kabushiki Co., 2002). When analyzing FTO for a third-party patent, always review the prosecution history (file wrapper) to identify narrowing amendments. The USPTO PAIR system provides public access to prosecution histories for granted patents.

7. Non-Patent Literature (NPL) Search

For technology-intensive fields like AI/ML and biotech, non-patent literature (NPL) is often the most damaging category of prior art. The academic community publishes cutting-edge ML techniques months to years before any patent covering the same technique is filed. Missing NPL prior art is one of the most common errors in AI/ML patent searching.

8. Freedom-to-Operate Analysis

Freedom-to-operate (FTO) analysis is the most legally consequential prior art activity a company can undertake. Getting it wrong does not just affect your patent application — it exposes your product to injunctions, treble damages, and forced redesigns. This section walks through the FTO process with sufficient rigor to help you have an intelligent conversation with your IP counsel about the risks your product faces.

🚫
FTO Opinion = Attorney Work Product

While this section teaches you how FTO analysis works, a formal FTO opinion must be written by a licensed patent attorney. The opinion can serve as evidence of good-faith belief in non-infringement to rebut claims of willful infringement (which can trigger treble damages). An internal engineer's analysis, however thorough, does not provide this legal protection. Once you identify a potential issue from your own search, bring in counsel immediately.

FTO vs. Patentability: Understanding the Difference

These two inquiries are often confused but are fundamentally different in scope and purpose:

  • Patentability search: Searches all prior art (published patents, applications, NPL, public disclosures). Goal: find art that would anticipate or render obvious your claimed invention. Scope: worldwide; expired patents matter.
  • FTO search: Searches only in-force patent claims in specific jurisdictions. Goal: find claims that could be asserted against your product. Expired patents do not create infringement risk. The scope is narrower but the legal consequence of missing something is more severe.

The FTO Process

Phase 1 — Product Mapping

Define the Product Features to Be Analyzed

Prepare a detailed technical description of your product, including every technical feature that could potentially read on a patent claim. For AI/ML products, this includes: model architecture, training data and methodology, inference pipeline, input/output format, hardware requirements, and the use case. Break the product into discrete functional modules — each module may need a separate FTO inquiry.

Phase 2 — Search

Identify Potentially Relevant In-Force Patents

Execute the same search strategy described in Section 5, but with two critical differences: (1) exclude expired patents (utility patents expire 20 years from filing; design patents 15 years from grant); (2) focus on in-force patents in the jurisdictions where you make, use, sell, offer for sale, or import the product. Use Google Patents' "legal status" filter or Espacenet's legal status tool to identify in-force patents.

Phase 3 — Claim Analysis

Read Claims of Identified Patents Against Your Product

For each potentially relevant patent, perform the claim mapping exercise from Section 6. Work from the independent claims first — if your product does not infringe any independent claim, you do not infringe any dependent claim from that patent. Construct arguments of non-infringement for each element you believe is not met. Note ambiguous elements that require claim construction analysis.

Phase 4 — Risk Stratification

Classify Patents by Risk Level

Assign each relevant patent to a risk tier: High Risk (all elements of at least one claim appear to read on your product — requires immediate action), Medium Risk (most elements read; DoE analysis needed; prosecution history review required), Low Risk (one or more elements do not appear to read; well-reasoned distinction available), No Risk (no elements read; factual basis clearly documented). High-risk patents require formal attorney opinion.

Phase 5 — Response Strategies

Design Arounds, Licensing, or Invalidity Challenges

For patents in the High or Medium risk category, four responses exist: (1) Design-around: modify your product to avoid at least one claim element — this is the preferred technical solution; (2) License: negotiate a license from the patent owner; (3) Challenge validity: file an IPR (inter partes review) or ex parte reexamination if you have prior art that could invalidate the patent; (4) Proceed with risk: rarely recommended without counsel; consider the patentee's enforcement history and financial capacity.

Phase 6 — Opinion

Obtain Formal Clearance Opinion from Patent Counsel

A formal written FTO opinion from qualified patent counsel accomplishes two things: (1) it documents the company's good-faith analysis for purposes of rebutting willful infringement; (2) it provides an attorney-client privilege-protected record of the risk assessment. The opinion should be updated whenever the product changes materially or when new patent publications in the relevant space are identified. Set up patent watch alerts for this purpose.

When to Get an FTO Opinion

Timing matters. The following events should trigger an FTO inquiry:

  • Before product launch in any new market or jurisdiction
  • Before significant investment in a new technology direction (capital efficiency)
  • Before acquiring a company or technology — the target's FTO position becomes your liability
  • When a competitor files a patent in your exact technology area — monitor competitor prosecution
  • After a cease-and-desist letter is received — requires immediate counsel engagement
  • Before licensing negotiations — understanding the patent landscape strengthens your position

9. Common Search Mistakes

The following are the most common errors made by technologists — and surprisingly many attorneys — when conducting prior art and FTO searches. Each one has resulted in invalidated patents or costly infringement actions that could have been avoided.

Mistake 1: Ignoring Foreign Patent Applications (Especially Chinese Patents)

China surpassed the U.S. as the world's largest patent filer in 2011 and has maintained that position ever since. CNIPA published over 1.5 million patent applications in 2023 alone. For AI/ML and biotech, Chinese academic institutions and companies (Baidu, Tencent, Alibaba, Huawei, Tsinghua, Peking University) are prolific filers.

The error: many technologists run a search on USPTO and Google Patents, see U.S.-centric results, and declare the space clear. A Chinese publication or application published before your filing date is valid prior art under AIA §102, even if it never enters the U.S. national phase.

Fix: Always include CNIPA (China), JPO (Japan), KIPO (South Korea), and EPO (Europe) in every comprehensive search. Use PatSnap or Google Patents' multilingual translation to access Chinese-language art. Espacenet's "worldwide" database is a good starting point for non-English prior art.

Mistake 2: Ignoring Non-Patent Literature (NPL)

In the AI/ML space, NPL prior art is frequently more relevant and more damaging than patent prior art, because the academic community publishes techniques faster than the patent system processes applications. A technologist who searches only patent databases for an attention mechanism invention will miss the Vaswani et al. (2017) arXiv paper — and face a guaranteed prior art rejection.

USPTO examiners are specifically trained to search arXiv, IEEE Xplore, and ACM DL for AI/ML applications. If they find it and you did not disclose it, you face a rejection plus questions about your duty of disclosure (37 C.F.R. §1.56).

Fix: Every AI/ML patent search must include at minimum: arXiv.org (cs.LG, cs.AI, cs.CV, cs.CL), Google Scholar, and IEEE Xplore. For biotech: PubMed and relevant journal databases. The NPL search is not optional — it is the most important part of the search for fast-moving technology fields.

Mistake 3: Searching Too Narrowly (Terminology Tunnel Vision)

AI/ML terminology has evolved rapidly. The same concept may be described by entirely different terms depending on the decade, community, or application domain. A search for "graph neural network" will miss patents that use the terms "graph convolutional network," "spectral graph convolution," "message-passing neural network," or "relational graph network" — all of which cover closely related architectures. Similarly, "fine-tuning" might appear as "transfer learning," "domain adaptation," "feature extraction," "task-specific training," or "low-rank adaptation."

The error is using only the current buzzword for your concept and ignoring the historical and domain-specific vocabulary.

Fix: Before searching, build a synonym/concept map. Use CPC classification codes alongside keywords — the classification system is vocabulary-independent. Use semantic search tools (Google Patents, Semantic Scholar) that match conceptual similarity rather than keyword matching. Review the first 10 search results for additional terminology used in their claims and abstracts, and add those terms to your query.

Mistake 4: Missing Continuation and Divisional Patent Families

Large tech companies like Google, IBM, Microsoft, and Meta maintain enormous patent families with hundreds of continuation applications covering the same invention. A patent family can remain active and generate new claims for 20 years from the original priority date. Finding the parent patent and concluding the family is fully characterized is a serious mistake — a newly issued continuation with fresh claims may read directly on your product even though the parent was filed a decade ago.

Example: Google has filed dozens of continuation applications from the original Word2Vec patent family. A search that finds only the parent misses later continuations with potentially broader or more specifically targeted claims.

Fix: For any potentially relevant patent, always pull the full patent family. In USPTO Patent Public Search, search by AN/ (assignee) + priority date range. In Espacenet, use "INPADOC patent family" to see all family members. In Google Patents, the "Similar" and "Family" tabs show related applications. Flag any pending applications in the family — pending applications can still issue with claim amendments.

Mistake 5: Confusing Date of Invention with Date of Filing

Under AIA (post-March 16, 2013), the critical date for prior art is the effective filing date — not the date you actually conceived or reduced to practice the invention. Pre-AIA law allowed inventors to "swear behind" prior art by proving an earlier date of invention. Under AIA, this is not available for first-to-file applications.

The practical error: an inventor knows they conceived the invention on January 1, 2023, but filed the application on March 1, 2024. A competitor publishes a paper on September 15, 2023 (after conception but before filing). Under AIA, that paper is prior art — the inventor cannot swear behind it by proving earlier conception.

Fix: File a provisional application immediately upon identifying a potentially patentable invention. A provisional application locks in the priority date at low cost (<$1,000 for small entities) and gives you 12 months to finalize the non-provisional. Maintain dated invention disclosure records (signed, witnessed, timestamped) for all inventions, but understand these establish internal records, not AIA priority dates.

Mistake 6: Forgetting About Design-Arounds in FTO Analysis

When engineers find a potentially blocking patent in an FTO search, the instinctive reaction is often "we need to license this." But patents are defined by their claims — narrow your implementation until it does not read on any claim element, and you have achieved a design-around. This is almost always cheaper than licensing and leaves the product fully competitive.

The mistake is failing to ask: what is the minimum change to our product that eliminates infringement of this claim? For many AI/ML patents, the answer is surprisingly minor — changing a hyperparameter range, using a different activation function, or changing the input representation can remove a claimed element entirely.

Fix: In every FTO analysis, include a design-around analysis section. For each High/Medium risk patent, have your engineering team propose three alternative implementations. Have counsel evaluate whether each alternative avoids the claim. Document this analysis — it also demonstrates good faith if litigation occurs. Design-around analysis is an underused and highly cost-effective IP risk management tool.

10. Documenting Your Search

Documentation transforms a prior art search from a one-time activity into a defensible legal record. Proper documentation serves three purposes: (1) it satisfies your duty of candor to the USPTO under 37 C.F.R. §1.56 (you must disclose known material prior art); (2) it creates an evidence trail for willfulness defenses in litigation; and (3) it enables other team members to build on your work rather than repeat it.

The Duty of Disclosure

Every individual "associated with the filing or prosecution" of a patent application (inventors, attorneys, assignees) has a duty to disclose information they are aware of that is material to patentability. This duty is discharged by filing an Information Disclosure Statement (IDS) with the USPTO listing all known material prior art. Failure to disclose material prior art with an intent to deceive constitutes inequitable conduct, which can render the entire patent unenforceable — a catastrophic outcome.

💡
When in Doubt, Disclose

The IDS threshold is low: disclose anything "material to patentability," which courts have interpreted broadly. It costs almost nothing to include an extra reference in your IDS. The downside of non-disclosure — inequitable conduct, patent unenforceability — is existential. Develop a personal practice: every time you read a paper or patent that touches your invention area, drop it in a running IDS folder. Disclose it all.

Automated Patent Search: Python Code Examples

The following code demonstrates how to automate patent search and monitoring using freely available APIs. This is useful for setting up continuous monitoring of a technology area or bulk-processing search results.

"""
USPTO PatentsView API — Automated Patent Search
Requirements: pip install requests pandas

PatentsView API Docs: https://patentsview.org/apis/purpose
"""

import requests
import json
from datetime import datetime, timedelta

PATENTSVIEW_URL = "https://search.patentsview.org/api/v1/patent/"

def search_patents_patentsview(
    keywords: list[str],
    cpc_codes: list[str],
    date_start: str,
    date_end: str,
    max_results: int = 100
) -> list[dict]:
    """
    Search USPTO patents via PatentsView API.

    Args:
        keywords: List of terms to search in claims/abstract
        cpc_codes: List of CPC codes (e.g., ["G06N3/04", "G06N20/00"])
        date_start: ISO date string "YYYY-MM-DD"
        date_end:   ISO date string "YYYY-MM-DD"
        max_results: Maximum number of results

    Returns:
        List of patent dicts with title, id, date, assignee, abstract
    """
    # Build query — keyword OR search across title and abstract
    keyword_query = {"_or": [
        {"_text_any": {"patent_title": " ".join(keywords)}},
        {"_text_any": {"patent_abstract": " ".join(keywords)}}
    ]}

    # CPC filter
    cpc_filters = {"_or": [
        {"cpc_subgroup_id": code} for code in cpc_codes
    ]}

    # Date range
    date_filter = {
        "_gte": {"patent_date": date_start},
        "_lte": {"patent_date": date_end}
    }

    # Combine all filters
    query = {"_and": [keyword_query, cpc_filters, date_filter]}

    payload = {
        "q": query,
        "f": [
            "patent_id",
            "patent_title",
            "patent_date",
            "patent_abstract",
            "assignee_organization",
            "cpc_subgroup_id",
            "patent_num_claims"
        ],
        "o": {"per_page": min(max_results, 1000), "page": 1},
        "s": [{"patent_date": "desc"}]
    }

    response = requests.post(
        PATENTSVIEW_URL,
        headers={"Content-Type": "application/json"},
        data=json.dumps(payload),
        timeout=30
    )
    response.raise_for_status()
    return response.json().get("patents", [])

"""
arXiv API — Automated NPL Prior Art Search
Requirements: pip install arxiv

arXiv API docs: https://info.arxiv.org/help/api/index.html
"""

import arxiv
from datetime import datetime

def search_arxiv_prior_art(
    query: str,
    categories: list[str],
    max_results: int = 50,
    start_date: datetime | None = None,
    end_date: datetime | None = None
) -> list[dict]:
    """
    Search arXiv for potential NPL prior art.

    Args:
        query: Search string (supports AND, OR, field prefixes)
        categories: arXiv categories e.g. ["cs.LG", "cs.AI", "q-bio.QM"]
        max_results: Max papers to return
        start_date: Filter papers submitted after this date
        end_date:   Filter papers submitted before this date

    Returns:
        List of paper dicts sorted by submission date descending
    """
    # Build category filter
    cat_filter = " OR ".join(f"cat:{c}" for c in categories)
    full_query = f"({query}) AND ({cat_filter})"

    client = arxiv.Client(page_size=100, delay_seconds=3, num_retries=3)

    search = arxiv.Search(
        query=full_query,
        max_results=max_results,
        sort_by=arxiv.SortCriterion.Relevance,
    )

    results = []
    for paper in client.results(search):
        submitted = paper.published.replace(tzinfo=None)

        # Apply date filters
        if start_date and submitted < start_date:
            continue
        if end_date and submitted > end_date:
            continue

        results.append({
            "arxiv_id":   paper.entry_id,
            "title":      paper.title,
            "authors":    [a.name for a in paper.authors],
            "submitted":  submitted.strftime("%Y-%m-%d"),
            "categories": paper.categories,
            "abstract":   paper.summary[:400] + "...",
            "url":        paper.pdf_url
        })

    return sorted(results, key=lambda x: x["submitted"], reverse=True)

"""
EPO Open Patent Services (OPS) API — International Patent Search
Requirements: pip install requests
Register for free OPS key at: https://www.epo.org/searching-for-patents/data/web-services/ops.html
"""

import requests
from base64 import b64encode

EPO_OPS_BASE = "https://ops.epo.org/3.2/rest-services"

def get_ops_token(consumer_key: str, consumer_secret: str) -> str:
    """Obtain OAuth2 token for EPO OPS API."""
    credentials = b64encode(
        f"{consumer_key}:{consumer_secret}".encode()
    ).decode()

    response = requests.post(
        "https://ops.epo.org/3.2/auth/accesstoken",
        headers={
            "Authorization": f"Basic {credentials}",
            "Content-Type": "application/x-www-form-urlencoded"
        },
        data="grant_type=client_credentials"
    )
    return response.json()["access_token"]


def search_epo_cpc(
    token: str,
    cpc_code: str,
    keywords: str,
    date_range: tuple[str, str]
) -> dict:
    """
    Search Espacenet via OPS for patents in a CPC class.

    Args:
        token: OAuth2 access token
        cpc_code: CPC code string e.g. "G06N3/04"
        keywords: Space-separated keyword string
        date_range: (start_date, end_date) in "YYYY-MM-DD" format

    Returns:
        Raw OPS JSON response dict
    """
    # CPC query
    query = (
        f'cpc="{cpc_code}" '
        f'AND txt="{keywords}" '
        f'AND pd within "{date_range[0]},{date_range[1]}"'
    )

    response = requests.get(
        f"{EPO_OPS_BASE}/published-data/search",
        headers={
            "Authorization": f"Bearer {token}",
            "Accept": "application/json"
        },
        params={
            "q": query,
            "Range": "1-25"
        }
    )
    response.raise_for_status()
    return response.json()

    patent_results = search_patents_patentsview(
        keywords=keywords,
        cpc_codes=cpc_codes,
        date_start=search_start,
        date_end=search_end,
        max_results=200
    )
    print(f"  Found {len(patent_results)} patent results")

    # 2. arXiv NPL search
    print("[2/3] Searching arXiv for NPL prior art...")
    npl_results = search_arxiv_prior_art(
        query=" ".join(keywords),
        categories=arxiv_categories,
        max_results=100,
        start_date=datetime.fromisoformat(search_start),
        end_date=datetime.now()
    )
    print(f"  Found {len(npl_results)} NPL results")

    # 3. Compile report
    print("[3/3] Compiling search report...")
    report = {
        "invention_title": invention_title,
        "search_date": search_date,
        "search_parameters": {
            "keywords": keywords,
            "cpc_codes": cpc_codes,
            "arxiv_categories": arxiv_categories,
            "date_range": (search_start, search_end)
        },
        "patent_results": {
            "count": len(patent_results),
            "results": patent_results[:20]  # Top 20 for report
        },
        "npl_results": {
            "count": len(npl_results),
            "results": npl_results[:20]
        },
        "search_log": {
            "databases_searched": ["USPTO PatentsView", "arXiv"],
            "databases_to_add":   ["Espacenet", "Google Scholar",
                                   "IEEE Xplore", "CNIPA"],
            "notes": "Preliminary automated search; manual review required"
        }
    }

    # Save to JSON for documentation
    filename = f"prior_art_search_{search_date.replace('-', '')}.json"
    with open(filename, "w") as f:
        json.dump(report, f, indent=2)
    print(f"\nReport saved to {filename}")
    return report

Setting Up Continuous Patent Monitoring

Prior art searching is not a one-time activity. The best practitioners set up continuous monitoring so they know about new publications in their technology area as soon as they appear. Use: (1) Google Scholar Alerts — set keyword alerts at scholar.google.com/scholar_alerts for email notifications; (2) Lens.org saved searches with weekly email alerts; (3) arXiv digest emails for specific category/keyword combinations; (4) The search_arxiv_prior_art() script above, scheduled via cron or a cloud scheduler (GitHub Actions, AWS Lambda) to run weekly and email you new results. This costs nothing and keeps your IDS current.

Chapter Summary

Key Takeaways from Chapter 3

  • Prior art can destroy your patent (§102/§103) or expose your product to infringement (FTO). Both require systematic searching before you file or ship.
  • Under AIA, the critical date is your effective filing date — not your conception date. File a provisional application early.
  • Your own publications become prior art if you wait more than 12 months before filing. Publish then file is dangerous; file then publish is safe.
  • No single database covers the global patent landscape. Use at minimum: USPTO + Espacenet + Google Patents + an NPL source (arXiv/Google Scholar).
  • For AI/ML inventions, arXiv is often the most relevant prior art source — and the most frequently missed. Search it first.
  • Master CPC codes: G06N for AI computing, G06F 40 for NLP, G06V for vision, G16H for health AI, C12N/C07K/A61K for biotech/pharma.
  • Claim mapping is the analytical core of both patentability and FTO analysis. Map every claim element to your product/invention before drawing conclusions.
  • Document every search. The IDS duty of disclosure is real — when in doubt, disclose the reference.
  • For High-risk FTO findings, always engage patent counsel. Design-arounds are often cheaper and cleaner than licenses.
