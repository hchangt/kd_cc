A patent is a legal right to exclude — not, on its own, a source of cash. Converting patent rights into revenue requires a disciplined commercial strategy that begins before the patent issues and runs through enforcement, licensing negotiations, and, sometimes, courtroom combat. This chapter addresses the full commercial lifecycle of a patent portfolio: how to structure licenses, price royalties, negotiate from strength, defend against challenges, and build a licensing program that generates sustainable returns. The analysis is grounded in United States law but addresses international considerations throughout.

1. From Patent to Revenue — The Monetization Spectrum

Patent monetization sits on a spectrum. At one end, companies hold patents purely for defensive purposes — to deter suits by competitors who fear a countersuit. At the other end, Non-Practicing Entities (NPEs), sometimes called patent assertion entities or, pejoratively, "patent trolls," derive all or most revenue from licensing and litigation. Most operating companies fall somewhere in between: using patents both defensively and as a revenue stream.

AI Companies and the Licensing Question

For AI companies, the monetization decision is particularly complex. On one hand, AI technology is maturing rapidly, and companies with genuine technological leadership in areas like training efficiency, model compression, or novel architectures may hold patents that competitors need. On the other hand, the AI ecosystem has strong open-source norms, and aggressive licensing can damage developer relations and talent acquisition. The tension is real and requires careful strategic analysis.

Consider the contrast: Qualcomm generates approximately $10 billion annually from patent licensing, primarily from cellular standards-essential patents. IBM licenses its patent portfolio for revenues historically exceeding $1 billion per year. In the AI space, we are still in early innings. As of 2025, AI-specific patent licensing revenues remain modest compared to these benchmarks — but that is changing as foundation model companies, AI chip designers, and inference optimization specialists build portfolios of genuine commercial significance.

📊
AI Patent Licensing: Industry Statistics

Global AI patent applications exceeded 500,000 between 2020 and 2024, with the United States, China, and South Korea leading. Large language model-related patents have grown at approximately 180% year-over-year since 2022. McKinsey estimates that AI patent licensing revenues could exceed $8 billion annually by 2028 as the technology matures and key players begin asserting their portfolios. Approximately 30% of AI patent assertions in 2024 came from NPEs, up from 18% in 2021 — a sign that the monetization ecosystem is developing rapidly.

Licensing as Moat vs. Revenue Stream

There is a meaningful distinction between a defensive patent moat — a portfolio held to deter infringement suits and secure design freedom — and a licensing revenue stream. These purposes are not mutually exclusive, but they pull in different directions. A company that licenses aggressively signals to the market that its patents are real and enforceable; that same signal can invite challenges (IPR petitions, declaratory judgment suits) that would not arise if the portfolio sat dormant. The strategic choice must be made deliberately, not by default.

2. Types of Patent Licenses

A patent license is a contract by which the patent holder (licensor) grants the licensee permission to engage in conduct that would otherwise constitute infringement. The scope, exclusivity, and financial terms vary enormously. Understanding the taxonomy of license types is essential before entering any negotiation.

License Agreement Key Provisions

Beyond license type, every patent license must address a core set of provisions. Failure to address these carefully is a leading source of disputes:

  • Grant clause: The precise scope of the licensed rights — which patents, which claims, which jurisdictions, which fields of use, and whether the license covers future patents in the same family (have-made rights, sublicense rights).
  • Royalty base: On what revenue or unit metric is the royalty calculated? In AI, this requires particular precision: inference calls, model deployments, API revenue, hardware units, or downstream product revenue.
  • Audit rights: Licensor's right to inspect licensee's books; audit frequency, notice period, and cost allocation.
  • Most-favored-nation (MFN) clause: Provision requiring licensor to offer licensee terms no worse than those given to any subsequent licensee. Can lock in unfavorable rates if market evolves.
  • Improvement grants: Whether licensor must license future improvements; whether licensee must grant back improvements (grant-back clauses). Grant-backs to exclusive rights raise antitrust concerns.
  • Term and termination: Life of the patent; events of default; cure periods; termination for non-payment vs. breach.
  • Representations and warranties: Licensor's representation of ownership; warranty of non-infringement for the licensed IP; disclaimer of consequential damages.
  • Marking: Whether licensee must mark products with patent numbers (35 U.S.C. § 287).
  • Dispute resolution: Arbitration vs. litigation; venue; governing law.
⚠
Grant Clause Drafting: The Most Litigated Provision

In practice, more patent license disputes arise from ambiguous grant clauses than from any other provision. Courts have held that ambiguous license language is construed against the drafter under general contract law principles, but the Federal Circuit applies specialized patent license interpretation doctrines. The scope of "have-made" rights — whether the licensee can have a third party manufacture licensed products on its behalf — is particularly contentious. Draft with precision: specificity beats generality in every negotiated patent license.

3. Royalty Structures

How a royalty is calculated matters as much as the rate itself. The royalty structure determines the alignment of incentives between licensor and licensee, the predictability of revenue, and the ease of audit and enforcement. For AI and ML technologies, new usage-based royalty models are emerging that have no direct precedent in traditional patent licensing practice.

Running Royalties

A running royalty is calculated on an ongoing basis — typically as a percentage of net sales revenue, a fixed dollar amount per unit, or, increasingly in AI contexts, a fee per API call, per inference, or per model deployment. Running royalties align licensor and licensee incentives: the licensor benefits when the licensee succeeds commercially. The tradeoff is audit complexity and payment uncertainty.

Royalty Base: The Smallest Salable Patent Practicing Unit

Under Federal Circuit precedent, particularly LaserDynamics v. Quanta (2012) and VirnetX v. Cisco (2014), a patent licensor must generally calculate damages (and by extension, reasonable royalties) based on the smallest salable patent-practicing unit (SSPPU), not the entire market value of the end product, unless the patented feature drives demand for the whole product. For AI patents, this distinction is critical: if a patented training optimization is one component of a $100/month AI subscription, the royalty base is not the full $100 — it is the value attributable to that specific optimization, likely a fraction of that amount.

4. Licensing Negotiation Strategy

Patent licensing negotiations are complex commercial negotiations, but they differ from ordinary business deals in one important respect: the underlying entitlement structure is defined by law (the patent's scope and validity), not purely by market power. Understanding the legal baseline shapes every negotiation tactic.

BATNA: Best Alternative to Negotiated Agreement

Fisher and Ury's concept of BATNA is essential in patent licensing. For the licensor, the BATNA is typically litigation (seeking an injunction and damages). For the licensee, the BATNA is typically designing around the patent, challenging its validity (IPR petition), or accepting the risk of litigation. Each party's BATNA determines its negotiating floor. Assess yours accurately — and the other side's — before you enter the room.

Georgia-Pacific Factors: The 15-Factor Reasonable Royalty Framework

In Georgia-Pacific Corp. v. United States Plywood Corp., 318 F. Supp. 1116 (S.D.N.Y. 1970), the court articulated the fifteen factors used to determine a reasonable royalty in patent infringement damages. These factors are now codified in Federal Circuit practice and form the backbone of every licensing negotiation — because they define what a court would award if negotiation fails.

Negotiation Process

A disciplined negotiation process follows a defined sequence. Deviating from this sequence — particularly by making demands before completing internal analysis — is the most common cause of failed licensing campaigns.

  1. Internal Portfolio and Claim-Mapping Analysis

    Before contacting a prospective licensee, map the asserted claims against the target's specific products or services. Identify the strongest 3–5 claims. Have outside counsel prepare a claim chart. Assess validity risks: prior art, §101, §112. Be honest about weaknesses — they will be raised in negotiation.

  2. Valuation and Rate Analysis

    Using the Georgia-Pacific framework, the SSPPU analysis, and comparable transaction data (from ktMINE, RoyaltySource, or prior licenses), establish a defensible range for your royalty ask. Set a walk-away number before you start. Determine your BATNA: what litigation would realistically yield.

  3. Licensing Outreach and Initial Contact

    Send an initial licensing letter (not a threatening demand) identifying your portfolio and expressing interest in a licensing discussion. Avoid explicit infringement accusations in an initial letter — they can trigger a declaratory judgment filing by the target. The goal is a meeting, not a confrontation.

  4. Technical Presentation

    Present the technology and your claim charts to the target's technical team and in-house counsel. This is not a legal proceeding — it is a conversation. Listen carefully. Their technical pushback often reveals their defense strategy. Do not over-disclose: you are a licensor, not an expert witness.

  5. Term Sheet Exchange

    After initial technical discussion, exchange term sheets. Start with a clear ask that reflects your valuation, but leave room to negotiate. Be precise about royalty base, rate, payment structure, scope of license, and representations. Sloppy term sheets beget sloppy agreements.

  6. Negotiation and Mediation (If Needed)

    Multiple rounds of negotiation are normal. If parties reach an impasse, voluntary mediation before a neutral (often a retired judge or experienced patent litigator) can unlock agreement. Mediation success rates in patent licensing exceed 70% in complex commercial cases.

  7. Agreement Drafting and Execution

    Use outside counsel to draft the license agreement. Do not use a form agreement — the specific structure of royalties, field of use, and grant clause must be negotiated precisely. Review the final agreement against your term sheet; errors introduced in drafting are a common source of post-execution disputes.

  8. Ongoing Compliance and Audit

    After execution, the licensor's job is not done. Track royalty payments, conduct audits per the schedule, and monitor for scope creep (unlicensed use outside the licensed field). Many licenses are breached quietly through expanded use, not intentional non-payment.

Comparable License Evidence

Courts require that damages experts anchor their royalty opinions in comparable license transactions. The Federal Circuit has held that a damages expert must carefully account for differences between the comparable license and the hypothetical negotiation. Licenses to settlement of litigation are less reliable comparables than licenses negotiated in the ordinary course of business, because settlement licenses are influenced by litigation risk rather than pure technology value.

5. Patent Valuation Methods

Valuing individual patents is notoriously difficult. The same patent may be worth $0 to a company without a relevant product and $100 million to a company that is actively infringing it and has no design-around. Context is everything. Nevertheless, three principal valuation methodologies provide a framework for estimating patent value in the licensing context.

Income Approach: Discounted Cash Flow

The income approach values a patent based on the present value of the future cash flows it is expected to generate — either through licensing royalties or through the cost savings/profit premiums it enables. In licensing contexts, DCF analysis typically projects the expected royalty revenue stream over the patent's remaining life, then discounts it at an appropriate risk-adjusted rate.

DCF Illustration — AI Patent Valuation

Consider a patent on a model quantization technique with 14 years remaining life. The patent holder estimates potential licensing revenue of $3 million/year for years 1–5 (as the technology gains adoption), $5 million/year for years 6–10 (peak adoption), and $2 million/year for years 11–14 (declining as successor techniques emerge). With a risk-adjusted discount rate of 15% (reflecting validity risk, competitive design-around risk, and market adoption uncertainty), the present value is approximately $22 million. Key inputs — annual royalty, discount rate, and patent life — each require defensible support from market data and expert analysis.

Market Approach: Comparable Transactions

The market approach values a patent by reference to what buyers and sellers have paid for comparable patents in arm's-length transactions. Several databases support this analysis:

  • ktMINE: The leading royalty rate database, with over 20,000 licensing agreements and rate data spanning multiple technology sectors. Particularly useful for software and hardware royalty benchmarks.
  • RoyaltySource: Royalty rate data and licensing agreement summaries; useful for cross-checking ktMINE data.
  • IPStat: Patent transaction data and portfolio valuation analytics; useful for tracking patent sale prices and portfolio metrics.
  • RoyaltyRange: European-focused royalty rate database with sector-specific analysis.
  • Derwent Analytics / Clarivate: Citation-weighted patent strength metrics that correlate with market value.

Cost Approach

The cost approach values a patent based on what it would cost to recreate the technology or develop a design-around. This method is most useful for establishing a floor value — a licensee should be willing to pay up to the cost of designing around or re-developing the technology. For AI patents on specific training techniques, the cost approach requires estimating the compute and engineering cost to develop an alternative — which can be surprisingly tractable given published benchmarks on training costs.

💡
Technology-Specific Valuation Factors for AI Patents

AI patents present unique valuation challenges: technology obsolescence is rapid (a 2020 training optimization may be superseded by 2023), design-around may be feasible given the mathematical nature of many ML innovations, and claim validity under § 101 remains uncertain. Defensible AI patent valuations must explicitly address: (1) the likelihood of claim survival in IPR, (2) the pace of design-around in the field, (3) whether the claimed method is actually being used vs. disclosed in prior academic literature, and (4) the competitive landscape of substitute technologies. Failure to address these factors invites aggressive discounting by licensees and their experts.

6. FRAND and Standard Essential Patents

Standard Essential Patents (SEPs) are patents whose claims must be practiced to implement a technical standard. When a standards development organization (SDO) adopts a standard, patent holders who participated in the standard-setting process typically must commit to license their SEPs on FRAND (Fair, Reasonable, and Non-Discriminatory) terms as a condition of having their technology included in the standard.

Standards Bodies and Their IP Policies

  • IEEE (Institute of Electrical and Electronics Engineers): Amended SEP policy in 2015 to require FRAND rates based on SSPPU; more licensee-favorable than predecessor policy. Governs Wi-Fi (802.11), Ethernet (802.3), and many communications standards.
  • 3GPP (3rd Generation Partnership Project): Governs cellular standards (4G LTE, 5G NR). SEP commitments to ETSI under FRAND; rate-setting through litigation and bilateral negotiation. Qualcomm, Ericsson, Nokia, Huawei are major SEP holders.
  • W3C (World Wide Web Consortium): Generally requires royalty-free (RF) licensing for Web standards, with limited exception for RAND licensing.
  • ISO/IEC Joint Technical Committee 1 (JTC1): Governs international information technology standards, increasingly including AI standards. JTC1/SC42 (Artificial Intelligence) is developing standards for AI trustworthiness, bias, and performance.
  • IETF (Internet Engineering Task Force): Open-internet standards; RAND or RF licensing typical.

FRAND Licensing Obligations and Constraints

A FRAND commitment limits a SEP holder's ability to seek injunctive relief (courts and competition authorities in many jurisdictions view seeking an injunction on FRAND-encumbered SEPs as abusing a dominant position), and constrains the royalty rate to what is "fair, reasonable, and non-discriminatory" — terms that are defined through litigation rather than clear market benchmarks.

Two structural problems plague the FRAND system:

  • Royalty Stacking: If a standard is covered by patents from 100 different holders, and each charges 0.5% of the device price, the aggregate royalty is 50% — potentially exceeding the product's margin. Courts and competition authorities increasingly assess aggregate stacking in determining FRAND rates for individual patents.
  • Holdout: Licensees may refuse to negotiate in good faith, knowing that the SEP holder cannot obtain an injunction, and wait for a judicial determination of FRAND. This "holdout" problem allows large implementers (Apple, Samsung, major Android OEMs) to delay payments while enjoying the benefits of the standard.

Recent FRAND Litigation Landmarks

FRAND rate-setting has been subject to high-profile litigation globally:

  • Ericsson v. Apple (N.D. Tex. 2023): Jury set FRAND royalty rates for 5G/LTE SEPs at rates higher than Apple had proposed; damages award of approximately $300 million. Illustrates that jury determination of FRAND rates can produce commercially significant results.
  • InterDigital v. Lenovo (E.D.N.C. 2023): Court set global FRAND rates; raised questions about the appropriate geographic scope of judicial FRAND determinations.
  • Unwired Planet v. Huawei (UK Supreme Court 2020): UK courts can set global FRAND licenses; refusal to accept court-set terms can lead to an injunction even for SEPs. This decision significantly shifted leverage to SEP holders in UK litigation.

AI Standardization and Emerging AI SEPs

Artificial intelligence is now the subject of active international standardization. ISO/IEC JTC1/SC42 is developing standards covering AI management systems (ISO 42001), AI risk management, bias testing, and explainability. NIST's AI Risk Management Framework (AI RMF) has become a de facto standard for U.S. government AI procurement. The W3C Web Machine Learning Working Group is standardizing WebNN, a browser-based neural network API.

As these standards mature, any patent claims that must be implemented to comply with a standardized AI framework will acquire SEP status. Companies participating in AI standards development should track their patent portfolios for potential SEP status and make appropriate FRAND commitments — or, if they prefer, ensure their patents do not become essential before standards are finalized. This is a genuine emerging risk for AI companies that participate actively in standards bodies without careful IP management.

⚠
AI Standards Participation: IP Trap for the Unwary

Participating in AI standards body meetings (ISO, NIST, IEEE, W3C) without a clear IP policy is a serious risk. If your engineers propose technical specifications that get incorporated into a final standard, and your company holds patents covering those specifications, you may be deemed to have implicitly committed to FRAND licensing — potentially without even knowing it. Companies like Qualcomm, Nokia, and Ericsson have sophisticated IP policies governing what engineers may and may not disclose in standards meetings. AI companies participating in SC42 or WebNN working groups should adopt similar policies immediately.

7. Patent Pools

A patent pool is an arrangement in which two or more patent holders aggregate their patents under a common licensing administrator, which then offers a single license to implementers covering all pooled patents. Patent pools reduce transaction costs, eliminate royalty stacking within the pool, and provide implementers with comprehensive coverage.

How Patent Pools Work

Pool formation typically follows a common structure: an independent pool administrator (often MPEG LA, Via Licensing, Access Advance, or a purpose-built entity) conducts a patent essentiality evaluation by independent experts, sets a pool rate through negotiation among members, establishes royalty-sharing formulas among patent holders, and markets the pool license to implementers. Individual pool members receive a share of pool revenue in proportion to the number and strength of their essential patents.

Are AI Patent Pools Coming?

The conditions that give rise to patent pools — a fragmented landscape of essential patents covering a widely deployed standard, high transaction costs for individual bilateral licensing, and risk of royalty stacking — are beginning to emerge in AI. As ISO/IEC SC42 standards mature and as AI inference, model compression, and AI safety frameworks become standardized, the conditions for pool formation will be present.

The economics of pool formation require a threshold level of patent density (enough essential patents from enough holders that the pool is more efficient than bilateral licensing) and a sufficiently large implementer market to generate meaningful pool revenues. Both conditions are likely to be met in AI inference and AI hardware standardization within the next five to seven years, based on the trajectory of AI patent filings and ISO/IEC SC42 development.

Pros and Cons for Patent Holders

Advantages of Pool Participation

  • Reduced licensing transaction costs
  • Access to royalty revenue from all implementers, including small players who would not respond to individual licensing
  • Pro-competitive image; reduces litigation risk
  • Pool administrator handles enforcement and auditing
  • Participation signals portfolio quality to the market

Disadvantages of Pool Participation

  • Revenue share may be lower than bilateral licensing to large implementers
  • Essentiality evaluation may exclude valuable non-essential patents
  • Pool rate subject to antitrust scrutiny and may be revised downward
  • Loss of bilateral negotiating leverage with major licensees
  • Portfolio evaluation disputes among members can be acrimonious

8. Enforcement Options

When licensing negotiation fails, patent holders have three principal enforcement venues: district court litigation, International Trade Commission (ITC) proceedings under Section 337, and patent validity challenges at the PTAB (which is defensive rather than offensive, but shapes the enforcement landscape). Choosing among these venues is a critical strategic decision that shapes cost, timeline, risk, and available remedies.

District Court: Where to File

Venue choice in district court matters enormously. The Supreme Court's TC Heartland decision (2017) restricted patent venue to where the defendant is incorporated or has a regular and established place of business. As a result, Delaware (where most corporations are incorporated) and the Western District of Texas (which attracted cases under Judge Albright's faster docket before his transfer off patent cases) have been major patent litigation venues. The Eastern District of Texas, once the dominant patent forum, saw reduced filings after TC Heartland. The Northern District of California handles significant AI patent cases given the concentration of technology defendants there.

The ITC: A Uniquely Powerful Tool Against Foreign Manufacturers

The ITC's exclusion order remedy is uniquely powerful against electronics manufacturers who produce goods abroad and import them to the U.S. market. An import ban — enforced by U.S. Customs — can be commercially devastating for companies whose entire supply chain is in Asia. For AI hardware (GPUs, AI accelerators, smartphones running AI inference software), the ITC is frequently considered alongside or in parallel with district court litigation. The absence of money damages is a significant limitation, but the ITC's speed (12–18 months vs. 2–4 years in district court) and the power of the exclusion order often make it the preferred venue for certain cases.

⚖
ITC Domestic Industry Requirement

To bring an ITC Section 337 action, the patent holder must satisfy the "domestic industry" requirement: it must show either that it is actively exploiting the patents in the United States through significant domestic investment in manufacturing, research and development, or licensing activities (the "technical prong"), and that there is a domestic industry with respect to the articles protected by the patent (the "economic prong"). For a pure NPE, the domestic industry requirement must be satisfied through licensing activities — typically requiring substantial licensing activity in the United States, not merely the issuance of licenses. This requirement filters out purely foreign patent assertion entities, but sophisticated domestic NPEs can and do satisfy it.

9. Pre-Litigation Strategy

The steps taken before filing a complaint shape the entire litigation and licensing campaign. Missteps at the pre-litigation stage — particularly triggering declaratory judgment jurisdiction or failing to build a record for enhanced damages — can determine the outcome before the case is even filed.

Cease-and-Desist Letters and Demand Letters

A cease-and-desist letter accuses the recipient of infringement and demands that it stop. A demand letter makes a financial demand (pay this royalty or license fee) while reserving the right to litigate if the demand is refused. The distinction matters legally: under MedImmune v. Genentech (2007) and subsequent Federal Circuit decisions, an explicit infringement accusation in a letter may vest the recipient with standing to file for declaratory judgment (DJ) of non-infringement or invalidity in a district court of the recipient's choosing — not the patent holder's preferred venue.

Best practices for licensing outreach letters:

  • Frame the initial contact as a licensing inquiry, not an accusation
  • Identify the patents and technology of interest without explicitly accusing the target of infringement
  • Request a meeting or technical discussion
  • Include a confidentiality agreement with the letter or propose one before substantive discussion
  • Only escalate to explicit infringement allegations in a formal demand letter after analysis confirms willingness to litigate

Willful Infringement and Enhanced Damages

Under 35 U.S.C. § 284, a court may increase damages up to three times the assessed compensatory damages for willful infringement. After the Supreme Court's decision in Halo Electronics v. Pulse Electronics (2016), willfulness requires only that the infringement was subjectively willful — that the infringer acted despite a known risk of infringement that was either objectively high or taken with subjective recklessness. Building a pre-litigation record that supports a willfulness finding — through licensing letters, claim charts, and any actual knowledge of the patent — is therefore strategically important.

Declaratory Judgment Risk

As noted above, a licensing letter that is too aggressive can trigger a DJ suit in the target's preferred venue. For a Silicon Valley AI company facing assertion from a smaller patent holder, filing a DJ suit in the Northern District of California (a patent-sophisticated, defendant-favorable forum) is a standard defensive maneuver. Patent holders can reduce DJ risk by: keeping communications focused on licensing rather than infringement; avoiding identifying specific accused products without filing; and filing suit first in their preferred venue if they have sufficient evidence and are prepared to litigate.

Litigation Hold and Pre-Suit Investigation

Before filing suit — or even sending a formal demand letter — the patent holder must ensure that all potentially relevant documents and electronically stored information (ESI) are preserved under a litigation hold. Failure to preserve evidence after litigation becomes "reasonably anticipated" can result in spoliation sanctions, including adverse jury instructions. For AI companies, this includes preserving training data logs, model version histories, performance benchmarks, and any internal documents discussing the patent or the patented technology.

10. Defending Against Patent Claims

When a patent assertion is received — whether a licensing letter, a complaint filed in district court, or an ITC Section 337 complaint — the recipient has multiple defensive tools. A sophisticated defense strategy typically employs several simultaneously.

IPR: The Primary Defensive Weapon

Inter partes review at the PTAB has become the dominant defensive tool against patent assertions since its creation by the AIA in 2012. IPR allows any person (or company) to challenge the validity of a granted patent on the grounds of anticipation (§ 102) or obviousness (§ 103) in light of prior art patents and printed publications. The PTAB institutes IPR on a preponderance of the evidence standard, and adjudicates on a preponderance standard — lower than the clear-and-convincing standard required to invalidate at trial.

Statistics are compelling: according to PTAB data, approximately 60–70% of all patent claims that are the subject of a final written decision in IPR are either cancelled or amended. In the AI and software technology areas, the cancellation rate has historically been even higher, reflecting the large body of prior academic literature that patent examiners may have missed during prosecution.

IPR Strategy in AI Patent Cases

In AI patent cases, the IPR prior art search should begin with the academic literature. The AI research community publishes extensively on arXiv, and major conference proceedings (NeurIPS, ICML, ICLR, CVPR, ACL) contain a density of prior art that dwarfs what a USPTO examiner can review in prosecution. A well-resourced IPR petitioner will engage AI researchers to identify the most relevant prior work and explain to the PTAB why the claimed invention was obvious in light of that work. This approach has been highly effective in invalidating AI patents that were granted over inadequate examination of the academic prior art.

Coordinating IPR with District Court Litigation

Filing an IPR petition often supports a motion to stay the district court litigation pending the PTAB proceeding. Courts grant such stays when there is a substantial overlap between the IPR and the district court claims, and when the stay will not unduly prejudice the patent holder. A stay from the time of IPR institution through the Final Written Decision (approximately 12 months) can significantly reduce litigation costs and provides the possibility of full invalidity before any trial.

💡
IPR Estoppel: The Hidden Trap

An IPR petitioner who obtains a Final Written Decision is estopped from arguing in district court that any claim found valid is invalid on grounds that the petitioner "raised or reasonably could have raised" in the IPR. This estoppel extends to prior art that the petitioner could have discovered with reasonable diligence. The practical implication: before filing an IPR petition, conduct an exhaustive prior art search. Grounds you do not raise — but should have — are lost forever in district court. The decision to file an IPR must be made deliberately, with a complete view of the available prior art.

11. Open Source and Patents

The AI research community has an unusually strong open-source culture — major model architectures, training frameworks, and datasets are routinely released under open-source licenses. Understanding the patent implications of open-source licensing is essential for any AI researcher, company, or counsel involved in the AI ecosystem.

Patent Pledge Programs

Several major technology companies have adopted patent pledge programs that provide broader protection against patent assertion than open-source licenses alone:

  • Google Open Patent Non-Assertion (OPN) Pledge: Google has pledged never to assert a defined list of patents against open-source software users, subject to a retaliation carve-out. The pledge does not cover commercial closed-source implementations.
  • Tesla's Open Source Pledge (2014): Elon Musk announced that Tesla would not initiate patent lawsuits against anyone who, in good faith, uses its technology for electric vehicles. A notable signal, but legally informal — not a binding license agreement.
  • Microsoft's Open Invention Network (OIN) Participation: Microsoft joined OIN in 2018 and contributed access to its entire portfolio (60,000+ patents) for Linux-related technologies. The largest technology company OIN commitment at the time.
  • LOT Network: A cross-license network specifically designed to address patent assertion entity risk. Members license their patents to other LOT members if and when those patents are transferred to an NPE. Over 3,500 members including Google, Amazon, Microsoft, and numerous AI companies. Highly effective at reducing NPE risk within the member community.

Open Source Release Strategy for AI Researchers

When an AI research team or company is considering releasing models, training code, or datasets under an open-source license, the patent implications must be considered. Key questions:

  • Does the release include a patent grant? If using MIT or BSD, it likely does not provide an explicit patent grant — potential users may have uncertainty about their rights.
  • Is the code or model based on patented third-party technology? Releasing code that uses someone else's patent does not license that patent; the open-source release creates no implied license against third-party patent holders.
  • Does the company hold patents covering the released technology? If so, Apache 2.0 or a similar license with an explicit patent grant is advisable — otherwise, releasing code while retaining the ability to assert patents against users creates a hostile dynamic and reputational risk.
  • Should LOT Network membership be obtained before release? LOT membership is inexpensive and significantly reduces NPE risk for both the releasing company and downstream users.

12. Building a Licensing Program

Establishing a patent licensing program from scratch — rather than responding opportunistically to individual infringement situations — requires sustained organizational commitment, specialized expertise, and a patient multi-year horizon. For AI companies with mature patent portfolios, a structured licensing program can generate meaningful revenue; for those just beginning, this section provides a roadmap.

Licensing Program Launch Timeline

Phase 1 — Months 1–4

Portfolio Audit and Strength Assessment

Conduct a systematic review of the entire patent portfolio. For each patent family, assess: claim strength (validity, breadth, clarity), commercial relevance (is anyone using this technology?), technical accuracy (do the claims read on current products?), and licensing potential (could this be enforced or licensed?). Use outside IP counsel for legal assessments and in-house technical staff for commercial relevance scoring. Output: a tiered portfolio map identifying Tier 1 (high value, broad, valid, commercially relevant), Tier 2 (moderate value, licensing potential), and Tier 3 (low value, maintenance-fee candidates for abandonment).

Phase 2 — Months 3–6

Market and Target Identification

For Tier 1 and Tier 2 patents, identify which companies are likely practicing the claimed inventions. Use product teardowns, technical publications, job postings, and conference presentations to map patent claims to specific products, services, and announced capabilities. Build a target list with an initial assessment of each target's likely exposure (how important is the patented technology to their business?) and ability to pay. Identify which targets are most likely to take licenses without litigation and which will require persuasion.

Phase 3 — Months 5–8

Claim Mapping and Infringement Analysis

For priority targets and Tier 1 patents, have outside counsel prepare formal claim charts mapping each claim element to specific accused products or services. This is the legal foundation for every licensing conversation that follows. Claim charts must be accurate — counsel who sends claim charts that misread the technology will destroy credibility with sophisticated targets. For AI patents, this requires outside counsel with genuine AI technical background, not generic patent litigators.

Phase 4 — Months 7–10

Licensing Outreach and Demand Letters

Begin licensing outreach to priority targets, following the letter-writing protocol described in Section 9 above. Sequence outreach strategically: consider starting with smaller, less sophisticated targets to build licensing precedent and comparable license evidence before approaching large technology companies with substantial litigation resources. Document all outreach, including dates, recipients, and responses, to support later willfulness arguments if litigation becomes necessary.

Phase 5 — Months 9–18

Negotiation and License Execution

Manage active negotiations with each target in parallel. Establish a licensing team (typically a senior in-house lawyer, a technical liaison, and outside litigation counsel on standby). Use a licensing management software platform (Anaqua, CPA Global, IP360) to track the status of each negotiation, key dates, and outstanding issues. Aim to close deals before litigation becomes necessary; litigation should be a last resort, not the first move.

Phase 6 — Ongoing

License Compliance, Audits, and Portfolio Maintenance

After execution, implement a compliance tracking system. Conduct royalty audits on the schedule specified in each agreement. Continue monitoring the market for new infringers and new products that may fall within existing claim scope. Review the patent portfolio annually to add new patents to the licensing program (as new applications issue), adjust maintenance fee decisions (abandon Tier 3 patents when maintenance fees exceed expected licensing value), and identify continuation filing opportunities to align claim scope with evolving market practices.

Licensing Department Setup for AI Companies

Building an in-house licensing capability requires investment in people, process, and technology. A mature AI company licensing department typically includes:

  • Chief IP Officer or VP of IP: Senior executive who owns the licensing strategy and reports to general counsel or directly to CEO. Must understand both technical AI and legal/commercial dynamics.
  • Licensing Counsel (1–3 attorneys): USPTO-registered patent attorneys with AI technical backgrounds, experienced in licensing negotiations and patent litigation.
  • IP Operations Manager: Manages the patent docket, annuity payments, licensing database, and compliance tracking systems.
  • Technical Licensing Specialist: Engineer (typically a former ML engineer or researcher) who translates patent claims into technical product analyses and supports claim mapping and infringement analysis.
  • Outside Counsel Network: Relationships with at least one Tier 1 patent litigation firm (for enforcement and complex negotiations) and one Tier 1 prosecution firm (for portfolio development).
Building a patent licensing program requires the same organizational discipline as building any revenue-generating function. The companies that generate meaningful patent licensing income — Qualcomm, IBM, InterDigital — invested in the function over decades before it became a significant revenue source. For AI companies beginning this journey today, the first five years should be viewed as investment, not immediate return. — Observation from Kirkland & Ellis patent licensing practice, 2025

What These Numbers Mean for AI Patent Holders

The data paint a specific picture for AI patent holders and defendants. AI patents face the highest invalidity risk of any technology sector — both at PTAB (65–70% claim cancellation) and at the § 101 eligibility stage in district court (~40–45% invalidity rate). This elevated risk must be priced into any licensing demand and any litigation budget. A patentee with a portfolio of AI patents should expect that a significant fraction of asserted claims will not survive PTAB scrutiny, and should build a licensing program that generates returns sufficient to justify the costs even after accounting for this attrition.

For defendants, the statistics are broadly encouraging: the combination of IPR and § 101 challenges gives AI defendants a robust toolkit. However, note the median damages award of $32M for AI/software cases that do reach trial — even with elevated invalidity risk, the downside of an adverse verdict is commercially significant. The decision to litigate vs. license must be made on a case-by-case basis, but for AI companies with the resources to litigate aggressively and the technical expertise to mount strong invalidity cases, the empirical landscape is favorable.

📊
Practitioner's Perspective: The Licensing vs. Litigation Decision

After twenty years of patent litigation and licensing practice, the most consistent observation I can offer is this: the parties who generate the best outcomes — whether as patent holders or defendants — are those who make the litigation vs. licensing decision rationally and early, rather than allowing the situation to drift toward litigation by inertia. A patent holder with a credible, well-analyzed portfolio and a realistic royalty demand will almost always do better through a structured licensing program than through litigation. A defendant who conducts a rigorous IPR analysis early, designs around where possible, and makes a fair licensing offer where appropriate almost always outperforms a defendant who fights on principle until the trial verdict. The economics of patent litigation favor settlement; the skill lies in achieving settlement on terms that reflect the actual merits, not the relative bargaining power of well-capitalized parties grinding down smaller ones.

Chapter Summary

Patent monetization is not a passive activity — it requires the same strategic discipline as any other revenue-generating business function. This chapter has covered the full commercial lifecycle of a patent portfolio: from the initial strategic question of whether to monetize at all, through the mechanics of license structures and royalty pricing, to the enforcement options available when licensing fails, and the defenses available to companies facing assertions.

Key takeaways for AI practitioners:

  • AI patents face elevated validity risk — from IPR (65–70% claim cancellation) and § 101 (40–45% invalidity rate). Any licensing strategy must account for this attrition.
  • Royalty structures must evolve for AI — per-inference-call and per-deployment-fee models are emerging as the natural royalty basis for cloud AI services, displacing traditional percentage-of-revenue structures in some contexts.
  • Georgia-Pacific factors determine the courtroom floor — licensing negotiations always occur in the shadow of what a court would award. Knowing the litigation outcome distribution — including the cost, timeline, and success probability — is essential to making rational licensing decisions.
  • Open source has patent consequences — Apache 2.0 provides explicit patent grants; MIT and BSD do not. LOT Network membership is inexpensive and dramatically reduces NPE risk.
  • FRAND obligations are coming for AI — as ISO/IEC SC42 standards mature, companies with patents covering standardized AI functionality will face FRAND licensing obligations. Monitoring AI standards activity and tracking potential SEP status is now a material IP management function.
  • Pre-litigation preparation determines outcomes — the quality of claim charts, the accuracy of target identification, the completeness of the prior art search for IPR purposes, and the early filing of demand letters (to establish willfulness) all shape the case before it begins.

The AI patent landscape is in its early commercial stages. The companies that invest now in building disciplined licensing programs — with strong portfolios, honest valuation, skilled negotiators, and litigation-ready enforcement capabilities — will be positioned to generate meaningful returns as AI technologies mature from research novelties into commercially indispensable infrastructure.
