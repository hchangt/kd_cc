1. The Gene-Editing Revolution and the Patent Wars

Few technologies in the history of biomedical science have moved from laboratory discovery to commercial deployment with the speed and societal consequence of CRISPR-Cas9 gene editing. Within a decade of the foundational papers, CRISPR-based therapies had received regulatory approval, agricultural applications were reshaping crop development, and a global ecosystem of biotechnology companies had been built — in substantial part — on the foundation of who held the relevant patents.

The question of who owns CRISPR-Cas9 has generated one of the most expensive and procedurally complex patent disputes in the history of American intellectual property law. On one side: Jennifer Doudna at the University of California, Berkeley and Emmanuelle Charpentier at the University of Vienna — the scientists who would win the 2020 Nobel Prize in Chemistry for developing the technology. On the other: Feng Zhang at the Broad Institute of MIT and Harvard, whose laboratory was the first to demonstrate CRISPR-Cas9 gene editing in eukaryotic (human and plant) cells.

The central legal contest has involved patent interference proceedings, inter partes review petitions, Federal Circuit appeals, and a second USPTO interference — generating thousands of pages of briefing and expert declarations, and costing the parties and their institutional backers tens of millions of dollars in legal fees. The commercial stakes explain the intensity: the entity holding the dominant CRISPR patent position could collect licensing royalties from virtually every therapeutic, agricultural, and diagnostic application of the technology for two decades.

"The CRISPR patent dispute is not just about who gets credit for a discovery. It is about who controls a foundational technology platform — and whether the academic culture of open science can be reconciled with the commercial logic of exclusive IP rights." — Arti K. Rai, "The CRISPR IP Thicket," 37 Yale J. on Reg. (2020)

Why This Case Study Matters for All Biotech Innovators

The CRISPR dispute is not a curiosity of academic science politics. It encapsulates, in unusually sharp relief, the core tensions that every biotech innovator must navigate:

  • Publication vs. protection: Both teams published landmark papers in Science before completing their patent prosecution — a decision with permanent consequences in the first-inventor-to-file system introduced by the America Invents Act.
  • Scope of the claims: The distinction between demonstrating a technique in a test tube vs. in a living eukaryotic cell turned out to be the most commercially important claim differentiation in modern biotech history.
  • Natural phenomena and §101: CRISPR-Cas9 exploits naturally occurring bacterial immune system proteins and RNA-DNA hybridization chemistry. How the claims survived the Mayo/Alice natural phenomena exclusion tells us everything about the limits of the natural product doctrine.
  • Academic-commercial IP coordination: University technology transfer offices, startup companies, and research institutions made irreversible IP strategy decisions in real time as the science unfolded — with consequences measured in billions of dollars.

2. The CRISPR Technology: A Technical Primer

Before examining the patent claims, we need a working understanding of what CRISPR-Cas9 actually does. This is not incidental: patent eligibility, written description, and claim scope all hinge on the precise technical distinction between what was demonstrated in each laboratory — a distinction measured at the cellular and molecular level.

2.1 Bacterial Origins: Nature's Own Gene Editor

CRISPR (Clustered Regularly Interspaced Short Palindromic Repeats) is a naturally occurring adaptive immune system found in many bacteria and archaea. When a bacterium survives a viral attack, it incorporates short sequences of the viral DNA into its own genome within CRISPR loci. These stored sequences — flanked by palindromic repeats — serve as a molecular memory. Upon reinfection, the bacterium transcribes these sequences into RNA molecules that guide defense proteins (Cas nucleases) to destroy the viral DNA.

The genus Streptococcus pyogenes encodes a particularly powerful and compact Cas nuclease: Cas9. Doudna, Charpentier, and their collaborators discovered that the natural two-RNA system directing Cas9 could be simplified into a single guide RNA (gRNA), and that this engineered system could be programmed to cut virtually any DNA sequence by simply changing the gRNA sequence — a profound simplification that put gene editing within reach of any molecular biology laboratory.

2.2 The Three Key Components

⚖

Component (a): Cas9 Protein (Ref. 100)

The molecular scissors. Cas9 is a large (~1,368 amino acid) endonuclease that creates double-strand breaks in DNA. It contains two nuclease domains (RuvC and HNH), each cleaving one strand of the double helix. Crucially, Cas9 will only cut at a location immediately upstream of a PAM sequence (protospacer adjacent motif: 5'-NGG-3' for SpCas9), providing a specificity checkpoint.

🔁

Component (b): Guide RNA (Ref. 102)

The programmable targeting module. The guide RNA contains a ~20-nucleotide "spacer" sequence that is complementary to the target DNA. By changing only this spacer sequence, researchers can redirect Cas9 to cut any genomic location of interest. The sgRNA also contains scaffold structures that bind and activate the Cas9 protein. The engineerability of the gRNA is the entire commercial value proposition of CRISPR as a platform technology.

💔

Component (c): Target DNA (Ref. 104)

The genomic sequence to be edited. The target must contain a protospacer sequence complementary to the gRNA spacer, and the protospacer must be immediately 5' of a PAM sequence. Once Cas9 creates a double-strand break at the cut site (Ref. 108), the cell's own repair machinery either introduces small insertions/deletions (NHEJ, often disabling a gene) or incorporates a provided DNA template (HDR, enabling precise edits).

Figure 1. The CRISPR-Cas9 gene-editing mechanism. The Cas9 protein (Ref. 100), complexed with the guide RNA (Ref. 102), locates a target DNA sequence (Ref. 104) complementary to the gRNA spacer and immediately 5' of a PAM sequence (Ref. 106), then creates a double-strand break at the cut site (Ref. 108). The cellular repair machinery subsequently edits the sequence.

2.3 Why Eukaryotic Cells Were the Commercial Prize

The original Doudna/Charpentier 2012 Science paper demonstrated CRISPR-Cas9 editing in a reconstituted in vitro (cell-free) biochemical system. This was a spectacular proof of concept — but the commercial value lay entirely in making CRISPR work inside eukaryotic cells: human cells for therapeutic applications, and plant cells for agricultural engineering.

Eukaryotic cells present obstacles that in vitro systems do not: chromatin packaging of DNA (which physically restricts Cas9 access), nuclear localization requirements, off-target editing concerns at the scale of 3 billion base pairs, and the challenge of delivering large protein-RNA complexes into cells with intact membranes. These engineering challenges were not trivial. Feng Zhang's laboratory solved them and published in Science in January 2013.

⚠
The Central Patent Law Question

Was CRISPR-Cas9 in eukaryotic cells an obvious extension of the in vitro system — such that the Doudna/Charpentier filing should encompass it — or was it a separate, non-obvious invention requiring independent protection? The PTAB's answer to this question controlled hundreds of millions of dollars in licensing revenue. The Board ultimately ruled it was a distinct technological achievement — giving Broad priority over the eukaryotic cell claims.

Track One: A $2,000 Decision Worth Hundreds of Millions

The Broad Institute paid the Track One (Prioritized Examination) fee to obtain accelerated prosecution of its CRISPR applications. Under Track One, the USPTO commits to making a final disposition within 12 months. The Broad's first patent issued in January 2013 — roughly 20 months before the UC Berkeley patent under standard examination. That temporal advantage gave Broad-licensed companies a material head start in structuring licensing agreements and fundraising pitches. For breakthrough technologies, Track One is almost always worth the expedited fee. See Chapter 8 for a detailed analysis of when to request Track One vs. standard examination.

4.1 Understanding Patent Interference Proceedings

A patent interference is an administrative proceeding at the USPTO to determine priority of invention — i.e., who invented the claimed subject matter first. Under pre-AIA law (which applies to applications filed before March 16, 2013), priority went to the first-to-invent, not the first-to-file. Because both the Berkeley provisional (May 2012) and the Broad application (December 2012) predated the AIA's switch to first-inventor-to-file, the pre-AIA interference system governed.

For an interference to be declared, the USPTO must find that the two parties are claiming the same or substantially the same subject matter — a determination called "interfering subject matter." The key legal question in the CRISPR interference was whether Broad's claims (directed to CRISPR-Cas9 in eukaryotic cells) and Berkeley's claims (broadly covering CRISPR-Cas9 systems without explicit cell-type limitation) were in fact directed to the same invention.

4.2 The PTAB's "No Interference in Fact" Ruling

In February 2017, the PTAB issued a 52-page ruling concluding that there was no "interference in fact" between the Broad's eukaryotic claims and Berkeley's broader claims. The critical finding: the person of ordinary skill in the art would not have had a reasonable expectation of success in applying the in vitro CRISPR system to eukaryotic cells based solely on the Berkeley disclosure. Because the two sets of claims were directed to patentably distinct subject matter, there was nothing to interfere — and the Broad's issued patents stood.

PTAB Ruling — Feb. 15, 2017

"The preponderance of the evidence shows that Broad has demonstrated that the parties are not claiming the same patentable invention. Therefore, there is no interference-in-fact... The evidence shows that those skilled in the art would not have had a reasonable expectation of success in using a CRISPR-Cas9 system in a eukaryotic cell based on Doudna's disclosure of the CRISPR-Cas9 system in vitro."

— PTAB, Patent Interference No. 106,048, Paper 657 (Feb. 15, 2017)

4.3 Federal Circuit Affirmance (2018)

The Federal Circuit affirmed in Regents of the University of California v. Broad Institute, Inc., 903 F.3d 1286 (Fed. Cir. 2018). The court emphasized that the PTAB's "no interference in fact" determination is reviewed under the APA's substantial evidence standard — a highly deferential posture — and that the expert testimony in the record provided substantial evidence supporting the PTAB's conclusion. The court declined to reach the underlying priority date merits, leaving the Broad's patents intact.

4.4 The Second Interference (2022) and Continued Disputes

Following the first interference, UC Berkeley continued to prosecute additional patent claims that it argued were directed specifically to eukaryotic CRISPR-Cas9 applications. The USPTO declared a second interference (No. 106,115) in 2019, and in 2022 the PTAB again ruled in favor of the Broad Institute. The Board found that Broad's conception and reduction to practice of CRISPR-Cas9 in eukaryotic cells predated Berkeley's evidence for the same work, and that Broad had not suppressed, concealed, or abandoned its invention.

5. What the Claims Actually Covered

Understanding the CRISPR patent dispute requires reading the actual claims — not press accounts of them. The difference between the two sets of claims is not merely stylistic: it reflects a fundamental strategic choice about claim breadth and the risk-reward tradeoff between covering a broad genus vs. a specific, commercially dominant embodiment.

⚠
Claim Strategy Lesson: Dependent Claims as Insurance

Berkeley's Claim 14 (eukaryotic cells) appeared in the application as a dependent claim — dependent on Claim 5 ("in a cell"), which was itself dependent on the primary in vitro claim. This hierarchical structure meant that Berkeley had claim language covering eukaryotic cells, but it was buried as a dependent claim. The Broad's independent Claim 1 made eukaryotic cells the central limitation of the claim, supported by extensive specification examples, expert declarations, and reduction to practice evidence specifically in human cells. The lesson: for commercially critical embodiments, consider dedicating independent claims — not merely dependent claims — to the highest-value use case.

5.3 Why Eukaryotic Scope Was the Commercial Prize

Broad's Eukaryotic Claims — Maximum Commercial Value

  • Human therapeutics: All CRISPR-based drugs require editing human cells — covered by Broad's claims
  • Agricultural biotech: Plant cell genome editing (corn, soy, wheat) requires eukaryotic cell editing
  • Base editing: Next-generation precision editing tools operate inside eukaryotic cells
  • Prime editing: Developed from Cas9 — eukaryotic application covered
  • Diagnostics in vivo: Tissue-level diagnostics depend on cellular delivery
  • Licensable to: Editas Medicine, Intellia, CRISPR Therapeutics, Beam Therapeutics, Monsanto/Bayer

Berkeley's In Vitro Claims — Limited Commercial Value Alone

  • Cell-free diagnostics: Some nucleic acid detection assays (e.g., SHERLOCK) use in vitro CRISPR
  • In vitro transcription: Research tool applications without living cells
  • Basic research reagents: Purified Cas9 protein for biochemical studies
  • Limitations: No human therapy, no agricultural biotech, no in vivo diagnostics
  • Lost to Broad: Licensing to major therapeutic companies was compromised
  • Mitigation: Berkeley still licenses via CRISPR Therapeutics, Caribou, ERS Genomics

6. The Biotech Patent Eligibility Issues: §101 Analysis

Both the Broad and Berkeley CRISPR patents survived examination without §101 natural phenomena rejections — a fact that deserves close analysis, given the post-Mayo/Alice doctrinal environment in which they were prosecuted. Understanding why requires comparing the CRISPR claims to the claims invalidated in Association for Molecular Pathology v. Myriad Genetics, 569 U.S. 576 (2013).

6.1 The Natural Phenomena Doctrine Applied to Biotech

Under 35 U.S.C. §101, natural phenomena are not patentable subject matter. In Mayo Collaborative Services v. Prometheus Laboratories, 566 U.S. 66 (2012), the Supreme Court invalidated claims directed to methods of measuring metabolite levels in the blood and adjusting drug dosages — finding that the claims simply recited a natural relationship between drug dosage and metabolite concentration without adding an inventive concept.

CRISPR-Cas9 presents obvious §101 concerns: Cas9 is a naturally occurring protein derived from S. pyogenes; guide RNA hybridization with complementary DNA sequences follows natural base-pairing rules; and the DNA cleavage mechanism is a natural enzymatic reaction. A hostile examiner might have argued that the CRISPR claims merely direct natural enzymes to perform their natural function, guided by naturally occurring RNA-DNA hybridization.

6.2 Why CRISPR Claims Survived §101

Several features of the CRISPR claims distinguished them from the natural phenomena exclusion:

What Made CRISPR Claims Patentable

  • Engineered single guide RNA: The natural CRISPR system uses two separate RNAs (crRNA + tracrRNA). The sgRNA is an engineered chimeric molecule — a non-natural construct requiring human design and synthesis. This is an "inventive concept" beyond the natural phenomenon.
  • Non-natural DNA targeting: The gRNA spacer sequence is specifically designed by the inventor to target a chosen genomic locus. This is analogous to the "specific application" step that Mayo requires.
  • Codon optimization: Broad's claims involved codon-optimized Cas9 constructs designed for human cell expression — another engineered, non-naturally occurring modification.
  • Nuclear localization signals: Appending nuclear localization sequences to Cas9 for eukaryotic delivery is a human engineering choice, not a natural configuration.
  • Functional combination: The specific combination of engineered sgRNA + codon-optimized Cas9 + NLS + eukaryotic delivery vector is a non-natural combination not found in nature.

What Would Not Be Patentable

  • Isolated natural Cas9: After Myriad Genetics, isolated naturally occurring DNA sequences and proteins are not patentable. Claim 1 covering "Cas9 protein as found in S. pyogenes" would fail.
  • Natural RNA sequences: A claim to the natural tracrRNA or crRNA sequences without modification would be directed to a natural product.
  • The natural phenomenon itself: A claim to "the principle that a 20-nt RNA will hybridize with a complementary DNA sequence and direct Cas9 to cleave it" is a natural law.
  • Purely diagnostic claims: A claim of the form "detecting natural CRISPR activity in a bacterial sample" with no inventive application step would likely fail under Mayo.
  • Genomic sequences per se: After Myriad, a claim to the isolated DNA sequence of a CRISPR locus in a bacterial genome is not patentable.

The "Markedly Different Characteristics" Test

In Diamond v. Chakrabarty, 447 U.S. 303 (1980), the Supreme Court upheld patent eligibility for a genetically engineered bacterium because it had "markedly different characteristics from any found in nature." CRISPR-Cas9 claims can be analyzed through this lens: the engineered sgRNA + codon-optimized Cas9 + NLS combination exhibits functional properties (targeting any chosen 20-nt genomic sequence in human cells) that are markedly different from the natural bacterial immune defense system from which the components derive. This distinction — not merely isolation, but engineering for a new functional purpose — is the key to surviving §101 in biotech after Myriad.

6.4 Post-Amgen Considerations for Broad CRISPR Claims

While the CRISPR claims have not faced serious §101 challenges, the Amgen Inc. v. Sanofi, 598 U.S. 594 (2023) enablement decision poses risks for any continuation applications seeking to claim CRISPR-Cas9 editing by function across all possible guide RNA sequences. Amgen held that claims directed to a genus of antibodies defined by their function (binding a target and blocking a receptor) were not enabled when the specification did not teach how to make the entire scope of the claimed genus. Analogously, a claim to "any method of editing any genomic target in any eukaryotic cell using CRISPR-Cas9" would need specification support demonstrating that the full scope was enabled at the time of filing.

7.3 Licensing Structure and Field Splitting

The CRISPR licensing ecosystem has evolved a sophisticated field-splitting structure that allows both Broad and Berkeley to capture value from different applications while reducing direct competition between licensees:

  • Therapeutic vs. agricultural fields: Many licenses are split between human therapeutic use and agricultural/agricultural biotech use, with different royalty structures reflecting different market sizes and development timelines.
  • In vivo vs. ex vivo therapeutics: In vivo editing (delivering CRISPR directly into a patient's body) and ex vivo editing (editing cells outside the body before transplant) present different technical and commercial challenges, and some licenses carve out these sub-fields separately.
  • Research use exceptions: Both Broad and Berkeley have largely maintained broad research-use exceptions for academic, non-commercial research — a deliberate policy choice intended to preserve the scientific ecosystem that generates future licensing opportunities.
💡
CRISPR IP as Core Asset: The VC Perspective

For biotech startups in the gene-editing space, CRISPR IP has become a fundamental diligence item for venture capital investors. Series A pitch decks routinely include a "patent landscape" slide showing which CRISPR licenses the company holds, from whom, and for what fields. A startup lacking a clear license from either Broad or Berkeley — or a credible freedom-to-operate position — will face significant fundraising headwinds. The practical lesson: IP strategy is not a post-launch concern in biotech. It is a pre-seed diligence question that shapes which investors will fund the company.

8. The Patent Thicket Problem

Beyond the Broad-Berkeley dispute, CRISPR has generated what scholars have termed a patent thicket: a dense web of overlapping intellectual property rights covering different components, delivery mechanisms, applications, and improvements to the core technology. This thicket creates transaction costs that disproportionately burden smaller innovators and raises important policy questions about whether the current patent system is optimally structured for platform biotech technologies.

8.2 Patent Pool Proposals and Cross-Licensing

The CRISPR patent thicket has prompted proposals for patent pool arrangements. MPEG LA (the licensing administrator responsible for patent pools in MPEG video compression standards) proposed a CRISPR patent pool as early as 2016. The pool would collect essential CRISPR patents from multiple holders under a single administrative license, reducing transaction costs for companies that needed access to multiple rights.

The proposal met resistance from the major institutional holders — the Broad and Berkeley — who preferred to maintain bilateral licensing relationships that allowed them to price-discriminate between therapeutic applications (high royalties, reflecting enormous commercial value) and agricultural or research tool applications (lower royalties, reflecting different market economics). As of 2025, no comprehensive patent pool has been established, though cross-licensing arrangements between the two major institutions have reduced some friction.

8.3 Impact on Small Biotech Startups

The practical impact of the CRISPR thicket on smaller innovators is significant. A startup developing a CRISPR-based diagnostic may need to engage in license negotiations with Broad, Berkeley, and one or more delivery technology holders simultaneously — each negotiation requiring legal fees, management time, and potentially a milestone/royalty structure that materially affects the company's business model. Several survey studies have documented that smaller biotech companies report CRISPR IP as a meaningful barrier to entry, while larger pharmaceutical companies with more negotiating leverage obtain more favorable terms.

8.4 The AI Parallel: Emerging Thicket in Foundation Model Patents

The CRISPR thicket foreshadows dynamics that are developing in large language model and foundation model patents. Multiple institutions are filing overlapping patents on transformer architectures, attention mechanisms, RLHF fine-tuning methods, and inference-time compute techniques. If this patent thicket matures in a fashion analogous to CRISPR, AI startups of the 2030s may face the same multi-layer licensing burden that biotech startups navigate today. The policy lesson — from both CRISPR and the software patent thicket of the 1990s–2000s — is that early coordination on licensing norms, combined with careful attention to claim scope during prosecution, can reduce transaction costs for the entire ecosystem.

ℹ
Patent Pools: A Partial Solution

Patent pools have resolved thicket problems in other technology sectors: the MPEG-2 pool (video compression), the DVD pool, and the 3G cellular standards pool all allowed implementers to pay a single licensing fee for access to essential patents held by multiple entities. For CRISPR, the challenge is that the "essential" patents are held by research institutions with conflicting interests and fiduciary duties to different sets of stakeholders. The Broad's licensees (Editas, Beam) compete directly with Berkeley's licensees (CRISPR Therapeutics, Caribou) — making a collaborative pool arrangement politically complex even if economically efficient.

9. Lessons for Biotech Innovators

The CRISPR patent saga offers a graduate seminar's worth of practical lessons for anyone seeking to protect breakthrough biotech inventions. These lessons are not hypothetical — they are drawn directly from the consequences suffered (and avoided) by the actual parties.

  1. File Early — the First-Inventor-to-File System Is Unforgiving

    Under the America Invents Act's first-inventor-to-file system (effective March 16, 2013), the priority date is almost always the date of your earliest effective filing. Berkeley filed its provisional on May 25, 2012 — more than six months before Broad's December 2012 filing. Had the AIA's first-to-file system applied, Berkeley would have had the earlier priority date for the subject matter it had reduced to practice. File a provisional application as soon as you have a working, sufficiently described conception — even before you have completed all experiments. A provisional costs roughly $3,200 in USPTO fees for a large entity and buys 12 months to develop the full application.

  2. Use Track One for Breakthrough Technologies

    The Broad Institute's Track One request was one of the most consequential $2,000 decisions in biotech history. It caused the Broad's eukaryotic CRISPR patent to issue in January 2013 — nearly two years before Berkeley's standard-track patent. This gave Broad-licensed companies a material temporal advantage in licensing negotiations, fundraising, and product development. For any technology where patent issuance timing has commercial significance — which includes virtually any breakthrough invention — Track One (currently ~$4,200 for large entities, ~$2,100 for small entities) should be the default, not the exception. See Chapter 8.

  3. File Before Publishing in Nature, Science, or Cell

    Both teams published in Science within months of their respective foundational experiments. Under U.S. law, the inventor's own publication triggers a one-year grace period within which the inventor can still file. But in most foreign jurisdictions — including the European Patent Office, Japan, China, and Korea — there is no grace period. Publication before filing is an absolute bar to foreign patent protection. Coordinate with your institution's technology transfer office before submitting manuscripts. "Publish then patent" is almost never optimal for breakthrough technologies.

  4. Distinguish from Natural Phenomena with Specific Technical Implementations

    The CRISPR claims survived §101 challenge because they claimed specific engineered constructs — chimeric single guide RNAs, codon-optimized Cas9, nuclear localization sequences — rather than the underlying natural principle of RNA-guided DNA cleavage. When drafting claims for any biotech invention, identify and claim the specific non-natural modifications that enable the technology. "A method of using naturally occurring Cas9 protein to edit DNA" would face serious §101 obstacles; "A method using an engineered single-guide RNA comprising a spacer sequence of 17–24 nucleotides fused to a scaffold sequence" is a specific non-natural implementation that adds significantly more than the natural phenomenon.

  5. Cover Both Broad Methods and Specific Commercially Dominant Embodiments in Independent Claims

    Berkeley's fatal strategic error was not claiming eukaryotic cell editing in an independent claim. Claim 14 of US 8,697,359 covers eukaryotic cells — but as a dependent claim. The PTAB's "no interference in fact" ruling turned on the distinction between the broad in vitro claims and Broad's eukaryotic-focused independent claims. Structure your independent claims to cover the full scope your specification enables, and draft additional independent claims specifically directed to the highest-value commercial embodiment. Do not rely on dependent claims to capture the most important use case.

  6. Think About the Full Application Space in Your Original Filing

    CRISPR-Cas9 has applications in therapeutics, diagnostics, agricultural biotech, basic research tools, epigenome editing, transcriptional regulation, base editing, and prime editing. The original Berkeley filing, while technically sound, was primarily conceptualized around in vitro biochemistry — missing the opportunity to provide specification support and claims specifically directed to each major downstream application space. A comprehensive IP strategy for a platform technology should map all foreseeable commercial applications and ensure the specification and claims cover them. File continuation applications as new applications emerge.

  7. Coordinate IP Strategy Across Academic and Commercial Partners from Day One

    The CRISPR dispute involved three institutional assignees on the Berkeley side (UC, University of Vienna, and Charpentier individually) and three on the Broad side (Broad, MIT, Harvard). Coordinating prosecution strategy, licensing decisions, and litigation positions across multiple institutions with different cultures, legal teams, and financial incentives is genuinely difficult. The Berkeley team's delayed and somewhat fragmented response to the Broad's Track One filing reflects, in part, the coordination challenge of multi-institutional ownership. Early, clear agreements on IP governance — who controls prosecution, who authorizes licenses, how revenue is split — are essential.

10. What Could Have Been Done Better: Hindsight Analysis

Hindsight is not entirely fair to the Berkeley team, who were operating in 2012 at the frontier of a new technology, with incomplete information about how rapidly the field would develop and what the PTAB would ultimately find decisive. That said, examining their strategic choices in light of subsequent events offers an unusually clear-eyed view of IP strategy trade-offs that are generalizable to any breakthrough technology.

10.1 Alternative Claim Strategies

What Berkeley Filed

  • Broad independent Claim 1 covering CRISPR-Cas9 methods in vitro and in cells generally
  • Eukaryotic cell editing in dependent claims only (Claims 14, 15)
  • Specific focus on the biochemical mechanism (RNA-guided DNA cleavage)
  • Standard examination track (no Track One request)
  • Filed May 2012 provisional — more than 6 months before Broad

Alternative Strategy (Hindsight)

  • Independent Claim 1: Eukaryotic cell editing — specifically the commercial prize — as the centerpiece claim
  • Independent Claim 2: In vitro method — covering research tool applications
  • Request Track One immediately to ensure issuance before any competitor
  • Include prophetic examples (reasoned predictions, not just demonstrated results) for eukaryotic application in the specification, supported by expert analysis
  • File continuation-in-part within 60 days of achieving eukaryotic results to capture and date-stamp that reduction to practice

10.2 The Academic Publication Culture Tension

The single most significant structural tension revealed by the CRISPR dispute is between the academic culture of rapid, open publication and the commercial logic of IP protection. Both Doudna and Zhang published groundbreaking results in Science promptly after achieving them — as the scientific community expects and rewards. Neither team, at the moment of experimental success, was principally focused on patent strategy.

This tension is not resolvable by simply criticizing scientists for not being more commercially savvy. Academic careers are built on publications, not patents. University promotion committees reward papers in Nature and Science, not patent portfolios. The incentive structure of academic science systematically deprioritizes IP protection relative to commercial practice.

"The real lesson of CRISPR for academic scientists is not 'patent before you publish.' It is 'build a relationship with your technology transfer office that begins on the day you design the experiment, not the day you submit the manuscript.'" — Observation from interviews with university technology transfer professionals

Institutional solutions — technology transfer office integration into laboratory management, mandatory IP disclosure protocols, and pre-submission review for commercially significant discoveries — are more sustainable than asking individual scientists to behave like commercial IP strategists. Several leading research universities have implemented these structures in the years since the CRISPR dispute made the stakes viscerally apparent.

10.3 What a Unified Claim Strategy Might Have Looked Like

Had the Berkeley team filed in May 2012 with specification examples specifically addressing eukaryotic cell editing — even prophetically, based on the scientific expectation that the in vitro system would extend to cells — and with Track One, the entire subsequent dispute might have been avoided. The legal standard for written description and enablement permits prophetic examples (hypothetical worked examples that a skilled artisan could reasonably follow), provided the specification discloses enough about the technology to give the skilled artisan confidence the claimed approach will work.

This is a nuanced point: Berkeley would have needed genuine scientific basis for claiming eukaryotic extension in May 2012 — a basis that a hostile PTAB might have found insufficient. But even if the prophetic example approach had been contested, it would have forced the interference proceeding to focus on the quality of the written description rather than the simple dichotomy between "demonstrated in vitro" and "demonstrated in eukaryotic cells."

11. Applicable Lessons for AI Researchers

This is a biotech case study, but its lessons translate with surprising directness to AI and machine learning patent strategy. The structural parallels between the CRISPR situation and the emerging landscape for foundation model patents are significant and worth examining explicitly.

ℹ
Priority Date Is Everything in First-Inventor-to-File

The America Invents Act converted U.S. patent law from first-to-invent to first-inventor-to-file effective March 16, 2013. This means that between two teams that independently develop the same AI technique, the one that files first wins — regardless of which team actually conceived the idea earlier. In the pre-AIA CRISPR dispute, the first-to-invent doctrine created the complex interference proceedings that consumed a decade of litigation. In today's first-to-file world, priority races are won or lost at the filing date. For AI teams, this means filing provisionals as soon as a key technique is demonstrated — not after the paper is accepted at NeurIPS. Academic publication timelines routinely run six to eighteen months after experimental results are achieved. Every month of delay is a month during which a competing lab can file first.

💡
Accelerated Examination Can Be Worth the Fee

The Broad Institute's Track One request for CRISPR cost roughly $2,000–4,000 in government fees (more with attorney time) and yielded a patent that issued nearly two years before the Berkeley standard-track patent. For AI research institutions and companies developing foundational techniques — attention mechanisms, novel training procedures, efficient fine-tuning methods — Track One examination is worth serious consideration. A patent that issues two years earlier can be licensed two years earlier, cited in fundraising materials two years earlier, and used in competitive positioning two years earlier. At typical licensing rates for foundational AI patents, the present value of two years of additional licensed revenue far exceeds the Track One fee.

⚠
Publication Before Filing Is Risky — Even With Grace Period

Both the Berkeley and Broad teams published in Science before completing patent prosecution, relying on the U.S. one-year grace period to preserve domestic patent rights. The same pattern is endemic in AI research: papers are posted to arXiv and submitted to conferences while patent applications are still being prepared. This is acceptable for U.S. purposes if the application is filed within one year, but it is an absolute bar to patent protection in virtually every other major jurisdiction — including the European Patent Office, Japan, China, South Korea, and Canada. For any AI technique with global commercial relevance, post-arXiv, pre-filing publication can permanently foreclose international patent rights worth far more than the domestic application. File the provisional application before posting to arXiv. The arXiv post can be submitted hours after filing.

🔌
Natural Law and Abstract Idea Exceptions Are Structurally Analogous

In biotech, claims that merely recite natural phenomena (Cas9 cleaving DNA) without adding significantly more fail under §101 after Mayo. In AI and software, claims that merely recite abstract ideas (training a neural network; calculating a mathematical function) without adding significantly more fail under §101 after Alice. The claim drafting strategy that survived §101 for CRISPR — claiming specific non-naturally occurring constructs (engineered sgRNA, codon-optimized Cas9, NLS sequences) rather than the underlying natural phenomenon — has a direct analogue for AI: claiming specific non-abstract implementations (particular network architectures with defined layer configurations, specific training procedures with defined hyperparameter choices, particular data processing pipelines applied to defined hardware environments) rather than the abstract mathematical concept. The structural question in both domains is the same: "Does the claim add something specific, technical, and non-obvious beyond the unpatentable abstraction?" See Chapter 2 for full §101 analysis for AI systems.

11.1 Foundational Technology = Generational Licensing Value

Perhaps the deepest lesson of CRISPR for AI researchers is quantitative: the licensing value of a genuinely foundational technology — one that becomes a platform on which an entire industry is built — is measured in the hundreds of millions to billions of dollars. The Broad Institute earns over $100 million annually from CRISPR licensing. The transformer architecture, if patented and aggressively enforced, would likely generate comparable licensing flows from the $500+ billion generative AI industry.

This is not an argument that everything should be patented and licensed aggressively. The CRISPR thicket illustrates the social costs of over-broad IP protection for platform technologies. But it is an argument that researchers and institutions developing foundational AI techniques should consciously decide their IP strategy — rather than defaulting to open publication and being surprised by its consequences. The transformer architecture was open published; RLHF fine-tuning methods were widely disclosed before significant patents were filed. Whether those were the right decisions requires a serious analysis of social welfare, competitive dynamics, and institutional incentives — not simply a default to the academic publication norm.

12. Summary: Key Patent Lessons from CRISPR

The CRISPR-Cas9 patent dispute is the defining biotech IP story of the past two decades. Its lessons encompass patent strategy, claim drafting, prosecution procedure, eligibility doctrine, licensing economics, and the structural tensions between academic and commercial IP culture. The following checklist distills the most important takeaways, applicable to any researcher or institution working on breakthrough technology.

  • File a provisional patent application before any public disclosure — including conference presentations, preprints, and grant applications submitted to non-governmental funders with public disclosure requirements.
  • Request Track One (Prioritized Examination) for any genuinely foundational technology — the $4,000 government fee is negligible relative to the commercial value of two years of additional licensed patent life.
  • In the first-inventor-to-file world, filing date is priority date — the pre-AIA interference system that bedeviled CRISPR no longer applies for post-March 2013 applications. File first; perfect the specification later.
  • Draft independent claims directed to the highest-value commercial embodiment, not just the broadest conceptual coverage. Berkeley's eukaryotic claim was dependent; Broad's was independent. This structural difference determined the outcome of a decade of litigation.
  • Write specification examples covering all commercially important cell types, delivery modes, and application fields — even prophetically, when you have scientific basis to believe they will work.
  • The §101 natural phenomena exclusion is navigable with engineering specificity — claim the engineered construct (sgRNA, codon-optimized protein, NLS fusion), not the natural principle (RNA-guided DNA cleavage).
  • After Myriad Genetics, isolated natural sequences are not patentable, but engineered modifications are — chimeric molecules, codon optimization, and novel protein fusions are all protectable.
  • After Amgen v. Sanofi, genus claims must be fully enabled — if you claim all possible guide RNA sequences for all possible genomic targets, your specification must teach how to make the full scope of the claim, or the claim is vulnerable to invalidity for lack of enablement.
  • Publication in a high-impact journal is an absolute bar to patent protection in most foreign jurisdictions — file the PCT application on the same day you submit to the journal, or accept permanent foreclosure of international rights.
  • Multi-institutional IP governance must be established before results are achieved, not after. Who controls prosecution, who can authorize licenses, how revenue is split — these are decisions that need to be made in the term sheet, not the press release.
  • The commercial value of foundational technology is generated by licensing, not enforcement — the Broad's >$100M annual licensing revenue comes from enabling licensees to build products, not from suing infringers.
  • A patent thicket in a foundational technology imposes costs on the entire ecosystem — both patent holders and policymakers bear responsibility for whether licensing arrangements are structured to minimize or maximize transaction costs for smaller innovators.
  • Scientific credit and legal priority are not the same thing — Doudna and Charpentier won the Nobel Prize; Feng Zhang won the CRISPR eukaryotic patents. Both outcomes reflect real contributions; the legal and scientific systems measure different things.
  • Track the entire application space, not just the core technology — diagnostics, agricultural biotech, base editing, prime editing, and epigenome editing each represent distinct commercial fields with distinct IP opportunities.
  • Continuation applications are the mechanism for capturing downstream applications — file continuation applications specifically directed to each major application as that application becomes commercially validated, using the priority date of the original filing.
  • Conception without reduction to practice does not create priority under pre-AIA law — maintain detailed, contemporaneous laboratory notebooks documenting both conception dates and reduction-to-practice experiments, with corroborating witness signatures. Digital equivalents (electronic lab notebooks with authenticated timestamps) satisfy the same evidentiary requirements.
  • For AI researchers specifically: the patent system does not automatically track publication — open-sourcing a model or releasing code does not eliminate patent rights if the application was filed before the release. Decide your IP strategy consciously, not by default.

The Central Strategic Insight: Claim What You've Actually Built, Not Just What You Understand

Berkeley understood CRISPR-Cas9 at a deep mechanistic level — deeply enough to win the Nobel Prize. But their patent claims were drafted around what they had demonstrated in vitro, not around what the technology would ultimately enable in living cells. Feng Zhang's team built the eukaryotic system and claimed it specifically. In patent law, as in software deployment, the gap between "we understand the principle" and "we have built the thing" is the gap that determines whether your claims hold up. Write claims for what you have actually reduced to practice, and draft your specification to enable everything you reasonably anticipate. Then file — before publishing.
