Introduction: The Drug That Redefined Oncology

In the annals of pharmaceutical history, few drugs have transformed a therapeutic area as completely, or generated commercial success as overwhelming, as pembrolizumab (Keytruda, Merck & Co.). From its first-in-human dose administered in 2011 to becoming the world's highest-grossing pharmaceutical product in 2023 at $25 billion in annual sales, the Keytruda story is simultaneously a triumph of Nobel Prize-winning immunological science, clinical development strategy, regulatory ingenuity, and commercial execution at the highest level.

This case study dissects every dimension of that journey. I have spent four decades in this industry. I was at Roche when trastuzumab (Herceptin) redefined HER2-positive breast cancer. I watched from BMS as ipilimumab (Yervoy) opened the checkpoint inhibitor era. And I have studied, from inside and outside, how Merck converted an initially inferior compound — at least on paper in 2012 — into the most commercially successful oncology drug ever developed. What emerges is a masterclass in how science, strategy, and execution compound upon each other when an organization truly commits.

This chapter is designed to be read as a standalone case study, but it references frameworks developed throughout this book — biomarker-driven development (Chapter 7), regulatory strategy (Chapter 9), launch excellence (Chapter 10), and lifecycle management (Chapter 11). Keytruda is the test of all of those frameworks in the most demanding possible environment: a crowded, fast-moving, scientifically complex market with a formidable, well-capitalized competitor already ahead of you.

"The secret to Keytruda's success was not any single decision. It was the compounding effect of getting the science right, the biomarker strategy right, the first pivotal trial right, and then relentlessly executing label expansion while the competition stumbled. Every link in that chain had to hold." — Committee Chair, reflecting on 18 months of stakeholder interviews conducted for this chapter

Part I: The Nobel Prize Science Behind PD-1/PD-L1

The Immune System's Off-Switch: How Cancer Learned to Hide

To understand Keytruda, one must first understand the discovery that made it possible — and the decades of scientific work that preceded it. The story begins not at Merck, but in academic laboratories where two scientists, working independently and from different directions, identified a fundamental mechanism by which the immune system regulates itself, and by which cancers co-opt that regulation for their own survival.

The immune system is a sophisticated surveillance apparatus. T cells, the soldier cells of adaptive immunity, patrol the body continuously, looking for foreign or aberrant proteins that signal infection or malignant transformation. But unchecked T cell activity carries its own danger — autoimmune destruction of healthy tissue. Evolution therefore equipped T cells with a series of "checkpoint" molecules: surface receptors that, when engaged, dampen or terminate T cell activation. These are the molecular brakes on immune response.

James P. Allison at the University of California Berkeley was working on CTLA-4 (cytotoxic T-lymphocyte-associated protein 4) in the 1990s. CTLA-4 is a checkpoint molecule expressed on T cells that competes with the activating receptor CD28 for the ligands B7-1 and B7-2 on antigen-presenting cells. When CTLA-4 wins that competition, T cell activation is suppressed. Allison's insight — profound in its simplicity — was that blocking CTLA-4 might release the immune system's brakes and allow pre-existing anti-tumor T cells to do their job. In mouse models, this worked dramatically. Tumors regressed. His work led directly to ipilimumab, the first checkpoint inhibitor approved by the FDA in 2011.

Tasuku Honjo at Kyoto University took a different path. In 1992, he discovered PD-1 (Programmed Death-1), a receptor expressed on activated T cells. The function of PD-1 was not immediately clear. Over the subsequent decade, Honjo's laboratory and others identified PD-L1 (Programmed Death-Ligand 1) and PD-L2 as the ligands for PD-1. The PD-1/PD-L1 axis, it emerged, is a peripheral tolerance mechanism — active in tissues rather than lymph nodes. When PD-L1 on a cell engages PD-1 on a T cell in the tissue microenvironment, the T cell is suppressed, often permanently exhausted. This is how peripheral tissues protect themselves from friendly-fire autoimmune attack.

The oncological implication was devastating in its elegance: tumor cells, under selective pressure from immune surveillance, upregulate PD-L1. They essentially disguise themselves as "normal tissue" and exploit the peripheral tolerance mechanism to escape immune killing. Block the PD-1/PD-L1 interaction, and those tumor-reactive T cells can be reactivated. In 2018, Allison and Honjo shared the Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine for their discoveries. It was one of the most clinically impactful Nobel Prizes in modern memory.

Merck's Entry: From Lambrolizumab to Pembrolizumab

Merck's path to pembrolizumab was not born of in-house academic discovery. In 2009, Merck Research Laboratories, under then-President Peter Kim, licensed a humanized anti-PD-1 antibody from Organon BioSciences, which had itself been acquired by Schering-Plough, which had just been acquired by Merck. The antibody — known internally as MK-3475 and later designated lambrolizumab before its INN became pembrolizumab — was a humanized IgG4 monoclonal antibody engineered to bind PD-1 with high affinity and minimal effector function.

The IgG4 isotype choice was deliberate and clinically important. IgG4 antibodies do not efficiently activate complement or mediate antibody-dependent cellular cytotoxicity (ADCC). For a checkpoint inhibitor, you want to block PD-1 without killing the T cells that bear it. An IgG4 backbone achieves this. A stabilizing mutation (S228P in the hinge region) was introduced to prevent IgG4 half-antibody exchange — a known pharmacokinetic liability of IgG4 class antibodies — making pembrolizumab a more stable, predictable molecule in the clinic.

The antibody binds PD-1 at a distinct epitope that blocks both PD-L1 and PD-L2 engagement. Its binding affinity (KD approximately 27 pM) is extraordinarily high — among the tightest PD-1 binders in clinical development at the time. This biophysical advantage would later translate into clinical activity across tumor types with varying levels of PD-L1 expression.

The Race: Pembrolizumab vs. Nivolumab

When Merck entered the clinic in 2011, Bristol-Myers Squibb was already ahead. Nivolumab, BMS's anti-PD-1 antibody (an IgG4 with a different stabilizing mutation, Q347R), had entered first-in-human trials in 2006. BMS had the head start, the larger data package, and crucially, the existing relationship with the oncology community built through ipilimumab. Merck was the clear underdog in the PD-1 race. What followed over the next four years would be one of the most consequential competitive battles in pharmaceutical history — a battle Merck ultimately won on a clinical and commercial basis, despite starting later.

Part II: Clinical Development — The KEYNOTE Program

KEYNOTE-001: The First-in-Human Trial That Changed Everything

The KEYNOTE program — the name derived from "Key Note" as an internal designation — began with KEYNOTE-001, a Phase I dose-escalation study in patients with advanced solid tumors. Initiated in 2011 and led by Dr. Edward Garon at UCLA and Antoni Ribas at UCLA (among other principal investigators), KEYNOTE-001 was designed as a classic 3+3 dose escalation in heavily pre-treated patients with solid tumors, with a subsequent expansion cohort in melanoma patients.

The dose escalation explored pembrolizumab at doses ranging from 1 mg/kg every two weeks to 10 mg/kg every two weeks, and later flat dosing at 200 mg every three weeks (the eventual commercial dose). What emerged from KEYNOTE-001 was not merely safety and preliminary efficacy data — it was the signal that would define pembrolizumab's entire development program.

In the melanoma cohort, objective response rates were unprecedented for a solid tumor: approximately 33% overall, with responses that were often durable (median duration of response exceeded 12 months in responders). In ipilimumab-naive patients, responses were even higher. But the truly transformative observation from KEYNOTE-001 was the relationship between tumor PD-L1 expression and clinical response.

The biomarker analysis, led by Merck's translational medicine team and external academic collaborators, showed a striking correlation: patients whose tumors expressed PD-L1 on ≥50% of tumor cells (TPS ≥50%) had dramatically higher response rates — above 45% — compared to patients with lower PD-L1 expression. This observation — prospectively defined, analytically rigorous, and clinically compelling — would become the cornerstone of Merck's entire regulatory and commercial strategy.

"The PD-L1 biomarker data from KEYNOTE-001 was not a surprise to us internally — the science predicted it. What was remarkable was how clean and actionable the signal was. It gave us a path to accelerated approval and a defined patient population that no competitor had at that moment." — Former Merck Chief Medical Officer, Oncology (paraphrased from committee interviews)

The Biomarker Strategy: PD-L1, TMB-H, and MSI-H

Merck's biomarker strategy for pembrolizumab evolved in three distinct phases, each expanding the eligible patient population while maintaining regulatory defensibility. Understanding this strategy is central to understanding how Keytruda achieved 40+ indications.

Phase 1: PD-L1 TPS as a Companion Diagnostic

The PD-L1 Tumor Proportion Score (TPS) — defined as the percentage of viable tumor cells showing partial or complete membrane staining at any intensity — became the first validated companion diagnostic for pembrolizumab. Merck partnered with Dako (now Agilent) to develop the 22C3 pharmDx assay, which became the FDA-approved companion diagnostic for pembrolizumab in NSCLC.

The TPS thresholds used across KEYNOTE trials were calibrated carefully: TPS ≥50% defined "PD-L1 high" patients where pembrolizumab monotherapy was clinically superior to chemotherapy; TPS ≥1% defined patients who benefited from pembrolizumab in combination with chemotherapy; TPS <1% represented the population where benefit was uncertain or minimal as monotherapy.

Phase 2: TMB-H as a Pan-Tumor Biomarker

Tumor Mutational Burden (TMB) — defined as the number of somatic mutations per megabase (mut/Mb) of sequenced DNA — emerged as a second predictive biomarker. The hypothesis: tumors with high mutational burden generate more neoantigens, creating more substrates for T cell recognition. In June 2020, the FDA granted accelerated approval for pembrolizumab in adult and pediatric patients with TMB-high (≥10 mut/Mb) unresectable or metastatic solid tumors whose disease had progressed after prior therapy. This was the second tumor-agnostic indication for pembrolizumab.

Phase 3: MSI-H/dMMR — The First Tumor-Agnostic Approval in History

The most consequential biomarker advance was MSI-H (microsatellite instability-high) and dMMR (mismatch repair deficiency). Tumors with deficient DNA mismatch repair accumulate thousands of insertion-deletion mutations in microsatellite sequences, generating extraordinarily high neoantigen loads. These tumors are exquisitely sensitive to PD-1 blockade.

In May 2017, the FDA granted accelerated approval to pembrolizumab for adult and pediatric patients with unresectable or metastatic MSI-H or dMMR solid tumors that had progressed following prior treatment, regardless of tumor histology. This was a landmark moment: the first time in FDA history that a drug was approved based entirely on a molecular biomarker rather than tumor site. It was tumor-agnostic approval, and Merck got there first.

Strategic Insight: The Biomarker Ladder

Merck's biomarker strategy was a deliberate ladder of expanding eligibility. Start with a narrow, high-response biomarker (PD-L1 TPS ≥50%) to secure the first approval and demonstrate clear benefit. Then broaden to lower thresholds (TPS ≥1%) to capture more patients as combinations justify benefit in the broader population. Finally, move to histology-independent biomarkers (MSI-H, TMB-H) to unlock every solid tumor type simultaneously. Each rung on this ladder was a new label, a new commercial opportunity, and a new barrier to competitive displacement.

The KEYNOTE Trial Architecture

The KEYNOTE program represents the most ambitious and well-executed oncology clinical trial portfolio in pharmaceutical history. With more than 590 individual trials enrolled, KEYNOTE systematically explored pembrolizumab as monotherapy, in combination with chemotherapy, in combination with other biologics, across every major tumor type, in every line of therapy, in every major geographic region. The program's organizational discipline — a centralized KEYNOTE nomenclature, standardized endpoints, consistent biomarker collection — is itself a model of clinical operations excellence.

KEYNOTE-024: The Trial That Won the War

If a single trial defines the Keytruda story, it is KEYNOTE-024. Published in the New England Journal of Medicine in October 2016, KEYNOTE-024 was a Phase III randomized trial in 305 patients with previously untreated, metastatic NSCLC (both squamous and non-squamous) with PD-L1 TPS ≥50% and no EGFR or ALK alterations. Patients were randomized to pembrolizumab 200 mg every 3 weeks or investigator's choice platinum-doublet chemotherapy.

The results were, by any historical standard, extraordinary. Median progression-free survival was 10.3 months with pembrolizumab versus 6.0 months with chemotherapy (HR 0.50; 95% CI, 0.37–0.68; P <0.001). The overall survival data at first analysis already showed a significant benefit (HR 0.60), and the trial was stopped early by the Data Safety Monitoring Board. At 24 months, 45.5% of patients treated with pembrolizumab were alive versus 29.7% with chemotherapy.

The competitive implication was equally decisive. At this moment, BMS's nivolumab was ahead in melanoma and renal cell carcinoma, but had just suffered a catastrophic setback with CheckMate-026 in first-line NSCLC (which did not define a PD-L1 cutoff prospectively). KEYNOTE-024's success — built on the discipline to define PD-L1 TPS ≥50% prospectively — gave Merck the first-line NSCLC label that BMS did not have. Lung cancer is the most common cancer globally. First-line NSCLC is the largest single oncology market. Winning here decisively shifted the competitive balance.

Strategic Insight: Why Merck Won NSCLC and BMS Did Not

The KEYNOTE-024 vs CheckMate-026 contrast is the single most instructive competitive lesson in modern oncology drug development. Both drugs bind PD-1. Both work. BMS ran CheckMate-026 without prospectively defining a PD-L1 cutoff that was validated enough to power the trial — they used a ≥5% threshold, which was too low to separate a biomarker-enriched population from the broad NSCLC population. Merck, building on KEYNOTE-001 data, committed to TPS ≥50% and powered the trial for that population. The biomarker call was made early, was based on biological plausibility and early clinical data, and was executed without compromise. This is the textbook definition of biomarker-driven development discipline.

Combination Strategies: Expanding the Eligible Population

Pembrolizumab monotherapy in PD-L1 high tumors was the first wave. The second, larger wave was combinations. The rationale was both biological and commercial. Biologically, chemotherapy induces immunogenic cell death, releasing tumor antigens and creating an inflammatory microenvironment that potentiates checkpoint inhibitor activity. Commercially, approving pembrolizumab combinations in any PD-L1 level unlocked the 50% of NSCLC patients (and the majority in other tumor types) who would not have qualified for monotherapy.

KEYNOTE-189 was the template. Adding pembrolizumab to pemetrexed-platinum doublet in non-squamous NSCLC produced a hazard ratio for OS of 0.56 — a 44% reduction in the risk of death — regardless of PD-L1 expression. This result recategorized the entire first-line NSCLC population as potential pembrolizumab candidates. The label effectively became: pembrolizumab monotherapy for PD-L1 TPS ≥50%; pembrolizumab + chemotherapy for everyone with NSCLC and no targetable driver mutation.

Similar strategies followed in esophageal cancer (KEYNOTE-590: pembro + chemotherapy), gastric cancer (KEYNOTE-590 analog), cervical cancer (KEYNOTE-826), and endometrial cancer (KEYNOTE-158 expansion). In each case, Merck used a combination backbone to expand eligibility beyond the biomarker-high fraction and into the broader histological population.

Part III: Regulatory Strategy — Navigating the Path to 40+ Indications

Breakthrough Therapy Designation: The Regulatory Accelerant

Pembrolizumab holds one of the most extensive Breakthrough Therapy Designation (BTD) portfolios in FDA history. The BTD program, established by FDASIA in 2012, provides intensive FDA guidance, rolling review, and organizational commitment to expedited development. For pembrolizumab, Merck used BTD not once but across a series of tumor types and settings, creating a systematic regulatory advantage over competitors who were moving through standard channels.

The first BTD for pembrolizumab was granted in January 2013 for melanoma, within months of the program's inception. This early BTD established the Merck-FDA relationship on pembrolizumab and created a collaborative development framework — essentially a standing dialogue between Merck scientists and FDA Oncology reviewers about trial designs, endpoints, and biomarker validation — that persisted for a decade. Subsequent BTDs in NSCLC (multiple), urothelial, HNSCC, and other tumor types each opened a dedicated communication channel that allowed Merck to design trials with maximum confidence in regulatory acceptability.

Accelerated Approval as a Strategic Tool

Merck was exceptionally disciplined in how it used the FDA Accelerated Approval pathway. Rather than treating it as a shortcut, Merck treated it as a sequenced strategy: obtain accelerated approval on an intermediate endpoint (typically ORR or PFS), launch commercially, generate commercial revenue and clinical experience, run the confirmatory trial in parallel, and convert to full approval upon confirmatory trial completion. This approach compresses the commercial clock by 2–4 years relative to waiting for full approval data.

The first Keytruda accelerated approval came in September 2014 for melanoma after prior ipilimumab therapy. The confirmatory trial (KEYNOTE-002) was already underway. Full approval followed in December 2015 — a textbook execution. In NSCLC, the sequence was equally clean: accelerated approval (KEYNOTE-010, 2015), followed by full approval building on KEYNOTE-024 and KEYNOTE-189 data.

The FDA's Office of Oncology Products, under Dr. Richard Pazdur's leadership, was a willing partner in this model because the clinical benefit was unambiguous and because Merck consistently fulfilled its post-marketing commitments. This trust, built over years of regulatory interaction, became a structural competitive advantage.

The MSI-H Decision: A Regulatory Breakthrough of Historic Proportions

The tumor-agnostic MSI-H/dMMR approval in May 2017 deserves its own analysis as a regulatory achievement. Traditional oncology drug approvals are organized by tumor type — breast cancer, lung cancer, colon cancer. The concept of approving a therapy for any tumor with a defined molecular feature was, before 2017, largely theoretical. The evidence base for the approval drew on five KEYNOTE trials (KEYNOTE-016, KEYNOTE-158, KEYNOTE-164, KEYNOTE-012, KEYNOTE-028), spanning 149 patients across 15 different tumor types, all with MSI-H or dMMR tumors.

FDA's decision to grant this approval was scientifically rigorous and practically pragmatic. The ORR across all tumor types was 39.6% with a complete response rate of 7.4% — remarkable numbers in heavily pre-treated patients. More importantly, the responses were durable: 78% of responses lasted ≥6 months. The decision set a precedent that has now been followed by larotrectinib (NTRK-agnostic), selpercatinib (RET-agnostic), and entrectinib (NTRK/ROS1-agnostic). Merck was first, and the commercial benefit of that precedent — in terms of market creation, prescriber education investment by Merck, and the institutional association of tumor-agnostic approvals with Keytruda — was enormous.

Part IV: Commercial Excellence — From $566M to $25B

The Sales Trajectory: Eleven Years of Compound Growth

Keytruda's revenue trajectory is unlike anything in pharmaceutical history. Launched commercially in September 2014 following its first FDA approval, pembrolizumab generated $566 million in its first full year (2015) — already a blockbuster by traditional definition. By 2019, it had surpassed $11 billion, overtaking Humira (adalimumab) in oncology to become the largest drug in a single category. By 2023, it had reached $25 billion globally, making it the best-selling pharmaceutical product on earth, surpassing even Humira's peak revenues. The compound annual growth rate from 2015 to 2023 is approximately 65% — an essentially unprecedented sustained growth rate for a drug of this scale.

Competitive Positioning: The Keytruda vs. Opdivo Battle

In 2014 and 2015, the conventional wisdom in pharmaceutical circles was that nivolumab (Opdivo, Bristol-Myers Squibb) would be the dominant PD-1 inhibitor. BMS was first with ipilimumab, had deeper oncology relationships, had entered the clinic earlier with nivolumab, and had more data. The ASCO 2014 and 2015 meetings were dominated by BMS data. Opdivo's initial approvals — melanoma (December 2014), squamous NSCLC 2L (March 2015), RCC 2L (November 2015) — came before several of Keytruda's early indications.

The reversal of fortunes was not gradual. It was sudden and hinge-point driven: CheckMate-026. BMS's trial to establish nivolumab as first-line NSCLC monotherapy failed in August 2016 because it lacked a validated, prospectively-defined PD-L1 threshold. Without a first-line NSCLC label, nivolumab was confined to second-line, which is a smaller patient population that gets re-analyzed with every new first-line approval. Meanwhile, KEYNOTE-024 reported in October 2016, and pembrolizumab became the standard of care for PD-L1 TPS ≥50% first-line NSCLC.

First-line NSCLC was the bellwether. Oncologists in the US and globally began defaulting to pembrolizumab for newly diagnosed NSCLC patients. Opdivo retained strengths in melanoma (combination with ipilimumab via CheckMate-067), RCC (CheckMate-214), and certain other tumors. But in terms of total revenue, the gap widened continuously: by 2019, Keytruda's $11B vs Opdivo's ~$7B; by 2022, Keytruda's $21B vs Opdivo's ~$8B. The NSCLC loss proved impossible for BMS to recover from commercially.

Pricing Strategy: $150,000 per Year

Pembrolizumab's US list price of approximately $150,000 per patient per year (at 200 mg Q3W; $18,750 per infusion cycle) places it at the premium end of oncology biologics but below the most expensive cell therapies and gene therapies. This pricing strategy reflects several deliberate choices. First, Merck did not attempt to price at the level of a rare disease orphan drug — the patient volumes are far too large for such pricing to survive payer scrutiny. Second, the price was set at a level that is uncomfortable for payers but defensible given the clinical benefit data — KEYNOTE-024 data showed pembrolizumab doubled overall survival in PD-L1 high NSCLC, generating quality-adjusted life years (QALYs) that most health economics models consider cost-effective at this price point in the US setting.

Outside the US, pricing is substantially different. In Europe, where health technology assessment (HTA) bodies including NICE (UK), HAS (France), G-BA (Germany), and others conduct value assessments before reimbursement decisions, pembrolizumab is priced significantly lower — estimated 50–70% of US list price — through confidential rebate arrangements. In Japan, where Merck partnered with MSD KK, prices are regulated under the National Health Insurance pricing system. Emerging markets receive tiered pricing and access programs.

The combination approval strategy also had pricing implications: when pembrolizumab is added to a chemotherapy backbone, the incremental drug cost is the pembrolizumab cost alone (chemotherapy is often generic). This makes the payer conversation cleaner — the incremental cost-effectiveness ratio is calculated relative to chemotherapy alone — and avoids the pricing complexity of two high-cost branded agents competing for reimbursement approval together.

Global Launch Sequencing

Merck's global launch strategy for pembrolizumab followed a disciplined sequencing: US first, Europe (EMA) second, Japan third, with rest-of-world (ROW) following. In the US, the FDA's Oncology Center of Excellence processed applications with Priority Review timelines (6 months). EMA approvals typically followed 6–12 months after FDA, reflecting the Committee for Medicinal Products for Human Use (CHMP) review cycle. Japan's PMDA approvals generally required additional bridging data in Japanese patients, adding 12–18 months to the timeline.

One underappreciated aspect of Merck's global commercial strategy was the investment in registrational trials designed to support global submissions simultaneously rather than sequentially. The KEYNOTE pivotal trials enrolled patients across the US, Europe, Asia-Pacific, and Latin America concurrently, generating datasets acceptable to FDA, EMA, PMDA, and NMPA (China) simultaneously. This organizational capability — global regulatory filing-ready trial design from the outset — compressed the global revenue ramp by 18–24 months relative to sequential regional trials.

Indication Sequencing as Commercial Strategy

Perhaps the most sophisticated commercial insight in the Keytruda story is how Merck used indication sequencing not just for label breadth but as a systematic commercial strategy. The principle: each new indication drives prescriber breadth, not just prescriber depth. Every new approved indication brings pembrolizumab to a new set of oncology subspecialists — melanoma oncologists, then thoracic oncologists, then urologists, then GI oncologists, then gynecologic oncologists, then breast oncologists, then hematologists (via Hodgkin lymphoma). Each new subspecialty group becomes a prescriber, generates experience, discusses at tumor boards, and reinforces pembrolizumab's role as the default checkpoint inhibitor.

This effect is multiplicative. An oncology practice that treats multiple tumor types has a single formulary decision for pembrolizumab that covers all its patients. Once pembrolizumab is on formulary for one indication, adding it for a new indication is formulary maintenance, not a new formulary inclusion decision. Merck invested heavily in tumor-board presence, medical education, and medical affairs resources to ensure that each new approval was immediately activated at the prescriber level.

"The indication sequencing strategy at Merck was not accidental. It was planned. We had a 10-year indication map in 2016 that looked remarkably like what actually happened. The science drove the biology of where IO would work, but the sequencing — which indication to pursue first, second, third in each tumor type, which combinations to run in parallel — was a deliberate strategic architecture." — Former Merck Oncology Strategic Planning Executive (paraphrased)

Part V: What Other Programs Can Learn from KEYNOTE

Lesson 1: Biomarker-Driven Development Is Non-Negotiable in IO

The contrast between KEYNOTE-024 and CheckMate-026 is the most teachable moment in modern oncology drug development. Both drugs work. Both target PD-1. The trial that defined a validated, biologically-plausible biomarker cutoff prospectively won. The trial that did not lost. The lesson is not merely statistical — it is strategic. Biomarker-driven development requires committing early, based on mechanistic understanding and early clinical signals, to a threshold that enriches for benefit. This commitment feels risky because it excludes patients who might benefit and narrows the addressable trial population. But it maximizes the probability of a clean, positive pivotal trial. And a clean pivotal trial in a well-defined population is more commercially valuable than an ambiguous result in a broad population.

The KEYNOTE-024 lesson also applies to the biomarker validation infrastructure: Merck's investment in the 22C3 pharmDx assay development, analytical validation, clinical validation against trial data, and FDA companion diagnostic submission was substantial and years-long. This infrastructure was built before the pivotal trial, not after. That sequencing — validate the test while running the trial — is essential for companion diagnostic success.

Lesson 2: First-in-Class vs. Best-in-Class — The Mechanics of Winning Later

The conventional wisdom in pharmaceutical strategy is that first-in-class wins. Keytruda is a counterexample that demands nuanced analysis. Nivolumab was first into pivotal trials, first to major approvals in melanoma and NSCLC (second-line), and had the larger early data package. Pembrolizumab was not first. It won by being better in the specific clinical dimension that mattered most: a validated PD-L1 companion diagnostic that enabled first-line NSCLC monotherapy approval while the competitor had none.

The lesson is not that being first-in-class is unimportant — it clearly confers advantages in prescriber education, formulary inclusion timing, and competitive moat building. The lesson is that the mechanism by which a later entrant can displace a first-mover is typically very specific: win a pivotal indication where the first-mover has failed or is absent, and that pivotal indication must be large enough to shift the overall market dynamics. For pembrolizumab, first-line NSCLC monotherapy was that indication. For every drug development program that is a "second" in a class, the strategic question is: which indication or patient population can we win definitively where the leader has not yet established itself?

Lesson 3: Breakthrough Therapy Designation — How to Use It, Not Just Get It

Many companies pursue Breakthrough Therapy Designation without fully understanding how to leverage it. A BTD is not a regulatory promise of approval — it is an invitation to intensive collaboration with FDA. The companies that benefit most from BTD are those that use the associated cross-disciplinary FDA review team meetings to resolve critical path uncertainties before committing to Phase III design. Merck's BTD interactions on pembrolizumab resolved questions about PD-L1 cutoff validity, acceptable response rate thresholds for accelerated approval, confirmatory trial design requirements, and acceptable companion diagnostic validation standards — all before Phase III enrollment was committed.

The timing of the BTD application also matters. Applying for BTD with Phase I data that shows compelling signals, strong biological rationale, and a well-specified biomarker hypothesis maximizes the chance of grant and maximizes the value of the subsequent FDA collaboration. A BTD application submitted with sparse Phase I data and no biomarker hypothesis receives less FDA engagement and provides less competitive advantage.

Lesson 4: Indication Sequencing Drives Peak Sales More Than Launch Dosing

In conventional pharmaceutical commercial models, peak sales are largely determined by the size of the initial approved indication, the depth of market penetration in that indication, and the level of competitive pressure. For biologics in large indications, these factors typically define a sales ceiling. Pembrolizumab broke this model by demonstrating that systematic, science-driven indication expansion can create a sales trajectory with no apparent ceiling for over a decade.

The economic mathematics are instructive. Each new indication in a major tumor type adds incrementally to the prescriber base (as new subspecialists begin using the drug) and to the patient base (as new patients become eligible). Because pembrolizumab is priced at ~$150,000/year, each additional approved indication that converts even 10,000 incremental patients per year adds $1.5 billion in annual peak sales potential. Merck's 40+ indications, each adding thousands to tens of thousands of eligible patients, compound into the $25 billion peak.

The operational implication for drug developers: lifecycle planning must begin at IND, not at NDA. The full indication map — which tumor types, which lines of therapy, which combinations, which biomarker-defined subpopulations — should be sketched before Phase III begins. This planning drives Phase I cohort expansion design (include enough tumor-type-specific cohorts to generate the Phase II-enabling data simultaneously), biomarker strategy (collect and store tumor samples for retrospective biomarker analysis), and regulatory strategy (engage FDA on the full indication development plan, not just the lead indication).

Lesson 5: Competition in IO — The Importance of Compound Portfolio Breadth

IO competition has intensified dramatically since 2017. Beyond nivolumab, pembrolizumab now competes with atezolizumab (Tecentriq, Roche; anti-PD-L1), durvalumab (Imfinzi, AstraZeneca; anti-PD-L1), avelumab (Bavencio, Pfizer/Merck KGaA; anti-PD-L1), cemiplimab (Libtayo, Regeneron/Sanofi; anti-PD-1), and a growing wave of next-generation IO agents. In China, a dozen domestic anti-PD-1/PD-L1 antibodies have been approved at substantially lower prices.

Keytruda's competitive durability in this environment reflects several factors beyond the label breadth: (1) the clinical evidence base, with KEYNOTE data now spanning 10+ years of follow-up with mature OS data; (2) prescriber familiarity and clinical inertia in the absence of clear superiority data for competitors; (3) the companion diagnostic ecosystem, where the 22C3 assay is widely implemented and reflexively ordered as part of tumor profiling; and (4) the Merck medical affairs and clinical liaison infrastructure that maintains engagement with prescribers across all 40+ indications.

Strategic Insight: The Companion Diagnostic Moat

The Dako 22C3 pharmDx assay is not just a regulatory requirement — it is a commercial moat. Every pathology laboratory in the US that processes NSCLC biopsies runs the 22C3 assay as a matter of clinical standard of care. The assay is embedded in NCCN guidelines as the preferred PD-L1 test for pembrolizumab treatment decisions in NSCLC. Switching a prescriber from pembrolizumab to a competitor requires either re-validating the existing PD-L1 result with a different assay (a clinical friction point) or accepting that competitor drugs are used without the validated companion diagnostic (a clinical risk the prescriber is unwilling to take). The companion diagnostic creates a tacit switching cost that pure drug comparison economics undervalue.

Part VI: Patent Expiration and Lifecycle Management — The $25B Cliff Ahead

The Patent Landscape: 2028 and Beyond

Pembrolizumab's intellectual property protection is complex, multi-layered, and actively contested. The primary patent protecting the pembrolizumab molecule in the US is expected to expire around 2028, with potential extensions through pediatric exclusivity and patent term restoration. Formulation patents and use patents (covering specific approved indications) may provide protection extending to 2032 and beyond in some jurisdictions.

The competitive biosimilar landscape is already forming. Multiple companies — including Samsung Bioepis, Celltrion, and others — have announced pembrolizumab biosimilar programs. The FDA biosimilar pathway for monoclonal antibodies requires demonstration of no clinically meaningful differences in safety, purity, and potency, and analytical comparability to the reference product. Given pembrolizumab's manufacturing complexity (a large IgG4 glycoprotein requiring specific cell line, fermentation, and purification processes), biosimilar development is scientifically demanding, but the economic incentive at $25B revenue is overwhelming.

Merck's lifecycle strategy to address this challenge is multi-pronged:

  • KEYTRUDA SC (subcutaneous formulation): Merck filed for approval of a subcutaneous co-formulation of pembrolizumab with berahyaluronidase alfa (Halozyme's ENHANZE technology), allowing administration as a 2–5 minute subcutaneous injection rather than a 30-minute IV infusion. This has significant patient convenience advantages and creates a product differentiation from IV-formulated biosimilars.
  • Next-generation IO combinations: Merck is investing heavily in novel combinations — pembrolizumab + antibody-drug conjugates (ADCs), pembrolizumab + TIGIT inhibitors (vibostolimab), pembrolizumab + LAG-3 inhibitors (favezelimab), pembrolizumab + VEGF inhibitors — generating new data, new approvals, and new commercial stories that extend Merck's IO franchise beyond any single compound.
  • MK-1308A (Coformulation): Pembrolizumab co-formulated with quavonlimab (anti-CTLA-4) represents an attempt to recapitulate the nivo+ipi competitive advantage while maintaining the pembrolizumab label.
  • Adjuvant and neoadjuvant market expansion: Early-stage cancer settings (adjuvant, neoadjuvant) involve treatment of patients who may be cured, represent larger patient populations than the metastatic settings where Keytruda initially launched, and generate treatment duration that is defined by trial protocols rather than disease progression. KEYNOTE-716 (melanoma adjuvant) and KEYNOTE-522 (TNBC neoadjuvant) are models of this strategy.

The Biosimilar Threat: Historical Lessons from Herceptin and Avastin

The erosion of trastuzumab (Herceptin) following biosimilar entry in the US (2019) provides the most relevant precedent. Trastuzumab biosimilars achieved approximately 70% market share in new prescriptions within 18 months of launch, with list price discounts of 15–30% but net price discounts (with rebate stacking) of 60%+ in some institutional contracts. Merck has observed this dynamic and is pre-positioning Keytruda for a managed biosimilar transition rather than a cliff-driven collapse.

The subcutaneous formulation is perhaps the most important defensive strategy. When Herceptin SC (trastuzumab + hyaluronidase) was launched in Europe, it maintained significant market share relative to IV biosimilars because the patient convenience (shorter administration time, no IV access required) was a genuinely differentiated clinical benefit. A pembrolizumab SC formulation that maintains efficacy and safety while offering a 5-minute injection versus a 30-minute IV infusion will be preferred by patients, nurses, and infusion centers dealing with capacity constraints — and that preference translates to maintained market share even at a price premium over IV biosimilars.

Part VII: Synthesis — The Keytruda Framework

The Six Pillars of Pembrolizumab's Success

Having examined every dimension of the Keytruda story, I want to synthesize what I believe are the six essential pillars of its success. These are not sequential — they are simultaneous and mutually reinforcing. Remove any one of them and the story of Keytruda might have been very different.

Pillar 1: Nobel Prize-Level Science as Foundation. Pembrolizumab is built on the PD-1/PD-L1 biology identified by Tasuku Honjo, validated by dozens of academic laboratories worldwide, and mechanistically coherent with everything we know about tumor immunology. It is not a me-too drug in a crowded class — it is one of the two best-in-class anti-PD-1 antibodies, built on a scientifically validated mechanism. No commercial strategy compensates for a drug that doesn't work. Keytruda works because the science it is based on is correct.

Pillar 2: Biomarker Discipline from Day One. The decision to invest in the 22C3 PD-L1 assay and to prospectively validate the TPS ≥50% threshold in KEYNOTE-001 before committing to KEYNOTE-024 was the single most consequential development decision in the program. It required scientific confidence, regulatory partnership, and commercial willingness to accept a narrower initial target population. Every subsequent biomarker decision — the CPS score for gastric/cervical/TNBC, the MSI-H agnostic strategy, the TMB-H agnostic strategy — was built on the discipline established in this early decision.

Pillar 3: Regulatory Partnership, Not Adversarial Interaction. Merck's relationship with the FDA Oncology Center of Excellence over a decade of BTD interactions, rolling reviews, accelerated approvals, and confirmatory trial fulfillments was collaborative and trust-based. Merck did not play games with confirmatory trial timelines. It did not submit applications with incomplete datasets. When the FDA asked questions, Merck answered with data. This track record of regulatory integrity accelerated every subsequent application and reduced uncertainty on every subsequent approval.

Pillar 4: Indication Sequencing as a Deliberate Architecture. The KEYNOTE program was not an opportunistic collection of trials. It was a deliberate architecture, designed from a 10-year indication map, optimized for the scientific priorities (start with the highest-response tumors to build the evidence base), the regulatory opportunities (use accelerated approval where possible, maximize BTD coverage), and the commercial logic (sequence through the largest oncology indications first, use biomarker expansion to grow within each). The organizational capability to run 590+ trials simultaneously, with consistent data collection and standardized endpoints, is a genuine and replicable competitive advantage that most organizations — even large pharma — do not have.

Pillar 5: Commercial Execution at Scale, Across 40 Indications. Launching a drug in 40+ indications across every major oncology subspecialty simultaneously requires commercial and medical affairs capabilities that are themselves extraordinary. Merck built a dedicated oncology commercial organization with tumor-type-specific teams, deep KOL relationships in each subspecialty, and medical liaison coverage that maintained scientific engagement between label updates. Each new indication was launched with full commercial support — not grafted onto existing resources. This organizational investment is itself a barrier to entry that most competitors cannot replicate.

Pillar 6: Lifecycle Planning from IND Through Patent Cliff. The subcutaneous formulation, the combination program with next-generation IO agents, the adjuvant/neoadjuvant expansion, the ex-US growth story — these are not reactions to the patent cliff. They are planned evolutions of a lifecycle strategy that was designed to maximize total revenue over a 20-year product lifespan. Companies that do lifecycle planning reactively — when the cliff becomes visible on the 5-year horizon — are already too late. Merck started planning the post-2028 Keytruda strategy in 2018, when the drug was at $7 billion and the cliff was 10 years away.

"I have studied every blockbuster in the history of this industry. Lipitor, Humira, Enbrel, Crestor, Gleevec, Herceptin — each was remarkable in its own way. Keytruda is different in one essential respect: it was designed to be generationally relevant across all of oncology, not optimized for a single indication. That ambition, and the organizational capability to execute against it, is what makes this the most consequential pharmaceutical launch in history." — Committee Chair

Critical Caveats: What Could Have Gone Wrong

The Keytruda story, as I have told it, reads almost like an inevitability. It was not. Let me identify the moments where the story could have broken very differently.

First, the PD-L1 biomarker could have failed as a predictive marker. If the correlation between PD-L1 TPS and clinical response observed in KEYNOTE-001 had not held in KEYNOTE-024, Merck would have had an approved drug in second-line melanoma with no path to a biomarker-defined first-line NSCLC approval. The science was right, but it was not guaranteed to be right at trial initiation.

Second, the accelerated approval strategy depends entirely on confirmatory trial success. If KEYNOTE-002 had failed to confirm the KEYNOTE-001 melanoma signal, or if KEYNOTE-024 had been underpowered or flawed in execution, the FDA would have withdrawn the accelerated approval, creating a regulatory and commercial catastrophe. Merck ran excellent trials, but trial excellence is not a certainty — it is a product of investment, talent, and discipline.

Third, the competitive dynamics in first-line NSCLC were not foreordained. CheckMate-026 failed. But if BMS had designed that trial with a validated PD-L1 cutoff prospectively, or if the FDA had granted nivolumab first-line NSCLC approval on the basis of a different endpoint, the competitive landscape would be fundamentally different today. Merck benefited from a competitor's strategic error as much as from its own strategic excellence.

Fourth, the immuno-oncology space could have generated evidence of unexpected serious immune-related adverse events at scale that would have constrained clinical use or required black-box labeling changes. The immune-related adverse events (irAEs) of pembrolizumab — pneumonitis, colitis, hepatitis, endocrinopathies — are real and sometimes fatal, but they occur at rates (serious irAEs in approximately 15% of patients, fatal irAEs in <1%) that are clinically manageable with corticosteroids and established monitoring protocols. A higher irAE rate, or a more severe unexpected toxicity, would have materially changed the benefit-risk assessment and constrained the label.

Implications for the Next Generation of Oncology Drug Development

The Keytruda case will be taught in pharmaceutical strategy courses for decades. But its lessons must be contextualized for the next generation of oncology innovation, which will increasingly involve:

  • Antibody-Drug Conjugates (ADCs): The new blockbuster class in oncology. Programs like trastuzumab deruxtecan (Enhertu) are already demonstrating Keytruda-like potential across multiple tumor types. The lessons of biomarker discipline, indication sequencing, and regulatory partnership apply directly.
  • Bispecific Antibodies: Programs like amivantamab and others targeting two tumor antigens or an immune cell and a tumor antigen simultaneously. The clinical development complexity is higher, the biomarker strategy is more nuanced, but the KEYNOTE framework of systematic indication expansion remains applicable.
  • Cell Therapies and CAR-T: At $400,000–$500,000 per treatment, CAR-T economics are entirely different from pembrolizumab, and the indication sequencing logic is constrained by manufacturing scalability. Nevertheless, the BTD + accelerated approval strategy that Merck pioneered is now the standard operating procedure for CAR-T approvals.
  • Cancer Vaccines: Merck's own mRNA neoantigen vaccine program (mRNA-4157, in partnership with Moderna) is perhaps the most intriguing extension of the Keytruda franchise. If a personalized cancer vaccine can augment pembrolizumab's activity in adjuvant melanoma — as preliminary KEYNOTE-942 data suggest — Merck will have created a novel, difficult-to-replicate combination that extends the pembrolizumab franchise into entirely new scientific territory.

The common thread across all of these next-generation platforms is that the organizational, regulatory, and commercial capabilities that Merck built for pembrolizumab — companion diagnostic development, BTD portfolio management, accelerated approval discipline, global trial execution, indication-specific commercial infrastructure — are directly transferable. The company that has learned the KEYNOTE lessons and built the institutional muscle to execute against them has a structural advantage in whatever comes next in oncology.

Chapter 12 Key Takeaways

  • Pembrolizumab's success is the compounded result of six pillars: Nobel Prize science, biomarker discipline, regulatory partnership, indication sequencing architecture, commercial execution at scale, and proactive lifecycle planning.
  • The KEYNOTE-024 vs. CheckMate-026 contrast is the definitive case study in biomarker-driven development: prospective validation of a specific PD-L1 threshold won Merck first-line NSCLC while BMS's broader approach failed.
  • Tumor-agnostic approvals (MSI-H 2017; TMB-H 2020) represent a new regulatory paradigm that applies across the oncology pipeline. Being first with tumor-agnostic data confers lasting clinical authority and market credibility.
  • Indication sequencing is the most underrated driver of peak pharmaceutical sales. With 40+ indications at $150,000/year per patient, each marginal indication adds hundreds of millions in annual sales potential.
  • The companion diagnostic ecosystem (22C3 pharmDx) creates a clinical switching cost that pure pharmacoeconomic analysis misses. Prescriber infrastructure inertia is a structural competitive advantage.
  • Patent cliff preparation must begin 10 years before expiry. The subcutaneous formulation, combination pipeline, and next-generation IO agents are not reactions to the 2028 cliff — they are the planned continuation of a lifecycle strategy designed in 2018.
  • Merck's competitive win over BMS benefited from a competitor's strategic error. In pharmaceutical strategy, outcomes depend on both your own execution and your competitor's mistakes. Monitor the competition's trial designs as carefully as your own.
