Introduction: The Translation Problem

I have spent four decades watching extraordinary science fail to become medicine. Compounds that cured mice in seventeen different tumor models collapsed in Phase II. Targets with compelling genetic evidence produced drugs that did nothing in unselected patient populations. Mechanisms validated in primary human cells simply did not engage in living, breathing, heterogeneous human beings. This is the translation problem, and it remains the defining challenge of modern drug development.

Translational medicine exists as a discipline precisely because the gap between preclinical promise and clinical proof is real, wide, and lethally expensive. At its core, translational medicine is the systematic application of biomarkers, mechanistic studies, and human biology to answer one overarching question at every stage of development: does this drug do what we think it does, in the right patients, at the right dose, at the right time?

When I was at Eli Lilly in the early 1990s, translational science was practiced informally — a few pharmacokineticists and clinical pharmacologists translating animal PK to human starting doses. By the time I was working with groups at Roche and Genentech, translational medicine had become a formal function, with dedicated teams, integrated biomarker programs, and co-developed diagnostics. Today, no serious development program launches into human studies without a translational strategy. The FDA expects it. Payers increasingly demand it. And most importantly, the biology demands it.

"The reason drugs fail in Phase II is almost never that the chemistry was wrong. It's that we asked the wrong question of the wrong patients with the wrong evidence that anything was happening biologically. Translational medicine is the discipline of asking the right questions before they become $500 million mistakes." Personal observation, based on 40 years of program reviews across Eli Lilly, J&J, GSK, AbbVie, Novo Nordisk, Roche, Pfizer, AstraZeneca, Novartis, Merck, BMS, and Gilead

The T1/T2/T3 Translation Spectrum

The field formally organizes translation into a spectrum of knowledge transfer that moves from bench to bedside to population. Understanding which translation gap you are trying to close determines what kind of evidence you need, what kinds of biomarkers are relevant, and how your program should be structured.

Figure 1 — The T1/T2/T3 Translation Spectrum

T1 translation is the original meaning of "bench to bedside" — moving from a laboratory finding into the first human experiments. This is where pharmacodynamic (PD) biomarkers are critical: you need to know that your drug actually engages its target in a living human being, that you're achieving pharmacologically relevant exposures, and that the mechanism you validated in cell lines is actually operating in human tissue.

T2 translation is the harder problem: demonstrating that a mechanism that engages in humans also produces meaningful clinical benefit in a defined patient population, at a scale sufficient for regulatory approval. This is where predictive biomarkers and companion diagnostics live, and where the vast majority of late-stage failures occur.

T3 translation addresses whether an approved therapy, which works in the controlled environment of a clinical trial, actually produces benefit in real-world clinical practice — with its heterogeneous patients, co-medications, incomplete adherence, and variable diagnostic access. Monitoring biomarkers and resistance markers are central here.

Biomarker Taxonomy: The FDA/NIH BEST Framework

For most of my career, the word "biomarker" was used loosely enough to be nearly meaningless. A colleague at AstraZeneca once told me that their oncology team counted seventeen different internal definitions in use simultaneously. The FDA/NIH BEST (Biomarkers, EndpointS, and other Tools) framework, published in 2016 and continuously updated, finally gave the field a rigorous shared vocabulary. Every translational scientist needs this framework embedded at the level of reflex.

The Predictive/Prognostic Distinction: The Most Important Conceptual Clarity in Translational Medicine

I have watched hundreds of millions of dollars wasted on this confusion. A prognostic biomarker tells you what will happen to the patient regardless of treatment. A predictive biomarker tells you whether a specific treatment will help a specific patient. They can overlap — but assuming a prognostic biomarker is predictive without a treatment interaction analysis is one of the most common and consequential errors in development. The classic example: KRAS mutation is prognostic in colorectal cancer (wild-type patients have better outcomes generally), but it is also predictive for anti-EGFR therapy — KRAS-mutant patients do not respond. You need both a treatment arm and a control arm, with biomarker-stratified analysis, to establish predictive value. A single-arm trial cannot prove a biomarker is predictive.

Biomarker Development: Discovery, Qualification, and Validation

A biomarker hypothesis is not a biomarker. The path from biological observation to regulatory-grade evidence is long, structured, and frequently underestimated by discovery scientists who hand off to development teams with the assumption that their ELISA kit is "basically ready." It is not.

The FDA Biomarker Qualification Program, launched under the Critical Path Initiative that Janet Woodcock championed, provides a formal pathway for biomarkers to be qualified independent of any specific drug program. Biomarkers qualified through this program — such as eGFR in chronic kidney disease, or specific cerebrospinal fluid Alzheimer's biomarkers — become public goods that any sponsor can use without re-establishing the evidentiary base. This is an underutilized pathway that industry should engage with far more proactively.

Pharmacodynamic Biomarkers: Proving Mechanism in Humans

Every first-in-human study should answer two questions before it answers anything else: Is the drug reaching its target? Is the target doing what we predicted when the drug engages it? Pharmacodynamic biomarkers are the instruments that answer these questions. Without them, a Phase I study that shows acceptable safety and reasonable PK is telling you almost nothing about whether you have a drug.

I learned this lesson viscerally at AstraZeneca during a kinase inhibitor program. The drug had beautiful preclinical PK/PD relationships — we could suppress phosphorylation of the target substrate in tumor xenografts with exquisite dose-dependency. We went into Phase I, achieved human PK exposures that matched our predictions, and reported clean safety data. Then the drug went into Phase II based on those PK data and failed completely. When we went back and measured the actual target pathway suppression in tumor biopsies — which we should have done in Phase I — we found virtually no suppression at clinically tolerable doses. The drug was an excellent oral compound that distributed everywhere except the tumor. The human PK/PD relationship bore no resemblance to the mouse data. We had not proven mechanism in humans. That failure cost $180 million and three years.

Constructing a PK/PD Model in Drug Development

A proper PK/PD program in translational medicine integrates:

  • Exposure measurement — plasma or tissue drug concentrations (Cmax, AUC, Ctrough) mapped over time
  • Target engagement — direct measurement of drug-target occupancy (receptor occupancy studies, covalent binding assays, thermal shift assays in accessible tissues)
  • Downstream pathway readout — modulation of a signaling node downstream of the target (e.g., phospho-substrate levels, cytokine production, gene expression changes)
  • Clinical signal — linking pathway modulation to a measurable clinical parameter (tumor regression, symptom score, lab value)

The PK/PD relationship — the mathematical function relating drug exposure to biological effect — is the translational bridge. If this relationship is well-characterized in preclinical species and then confirmed in humans, it provides the rational foundation for dose selection and trial design. If it cannot be confirmed in humans, that is the signal to stop and investigate, not the signal to proceed to Phase II with fingers crossed.

Predictive Biomarkers and Patient Enrichment Strategies

The shift from unselected to enriched development is the single most consequential methodological evolution in clinical drug development over the past thirty years. When I started in this industry, we enrolled "all-comers" and hoped the signal would emerge. Today, we understand that many biological targets are not uniformly relevant across a disease population — and enrolling patients whose biology is irrelevant to your mechanism is not a scientific neutral act, it is a statistical catastrophe that will sink your trial.

Enrichment Designs

The FDA Enrichment Strategy Guidance (2019) outlines three categories of enrichment approaches:

  • Prognostic enrichment — selecting patients at higher risk of the outcome of interest, improving statistical power without requiring treatment interaction (e.g., enrolling only high-risk MI patients in a cardioprotective trial)
  • Predictive enrichment (biomarker-selected) — selecting patients whose biology predicts differential benefit from the drug (e.g., enrolling only HER2+ patients for trastuzumab)
  • Feasibility enrichment — selecting patients likely to complete the study and provide evaluable data (adherence biomarkers, pharmacogenomic exclusions for known poor metabolizers at toxic risk)

Predictive enrichment is the highest-value strategy when a companion biomarker exists, but it comes with strategic choices. An all-comers design with pre-specified subgroup analysis allows you to test both the overall population and the enriched population, but requires larger sample sizes and risks diluting the signal in biomarker-negative patients. A biomarker-selected design is more efficient but forecloses opportunities in the unselected population. I generally advise sponsors to pursue the biomarker-selected design when the biological rationale is compelling and the companion diagnostic is technically feasible — the efficiency gains almost always outweigh the foreclosed opportunities.

Companion Diagnostics: Regulatory Requirements and Co-Development

A companion diagnostic is not an afterthought. It is not something you call a diagnostics company about in Phase III. I have seen this mistake made at every major pharma company I have worked with, and the consequences are invariably the same: either the drug approval is delayed waiting for the CDx approval, or the drug gets approved with a suboptimal diagnostic that does not adequately identify responding patients, undermining the drug's effectiveness in practice.

The FDA defines a companion diagnostic as "an in vitro diagnostic device that provides information that is essential for the safe and effective use of a corresponding therapeutic product." The word "essential" carries regulatory weight — it means the FDA will require the CDx to be approved concurrently with or prior to drug approval if the drug's labeling specifies its use.

CDx Regulatory Requirements (FDA vs. EMA)

In the US, a companion diagnostic for a new molecular entity is regulated as an in vitro diagnostic (IVD) under FDA's device regulatory pathway. If the test is essential to safe and effective use of the drug, the FDA will require a Premarket Approval (PMA) for the CDx — the most stringent device approval pathway, requiring clinical data demonstrating analytical and clinical validity.

In practice, the FDA Center for Drug Evaluation and Research (CDER) and Center for Devices and Radiological Health (CDRH) coordinate on CDx/drug co-submissions. The drug sponsor and the diagnostic manufacturer file separate applications, but they must be cross-referenced and reviewed in parallel. I have sat in the FDA building during co-review meetings, and the coordination between CDER and CDRH, while improving, remains a logistical challenge that smart sponsors plan for explicitly.

The EMA pathway is structurally different: the European IVD Regulation (IVDR, Regulation EU 2017/746) requires conformity assessment for companion diagnostics but without the same PMA-equivalent process. Sponsors working globally must plan for regulatory divergence in the CDx pathway, which can affect manufacturing specifications, analytical thresholds, and even the choice of diagnostic platform.

Surrogate Endpoints: Qualification, Risks, and Rewards

A surrogate endpoint is a laboratory measurement or physical sign used as a substitute for a clinically meaningful endpoint. If the surrogate is validated, demonstrating a drug's effect on the surrogate is sufficient evidence of clinical benefit. If it is not, you may approve a drug that improves your biomarker but does not benefit the patient — a mistake with serious consequences for both science and public trust.

The AIDS crisis of the 1980s and 1990s produced the most important case study in surrogate endpoint use and misuse. CD4 count and HIV RNA viral load were proposed as surrogates for clinical endpoints (AIDS-defining illnesses, mortality). CD4 count, despite biological plausibility, turned out to be an unreliable surrogate — several drugs that raised CD4 counts failed to improve survival or actually increased mortality. Viral load proved to be a far more reliable surrogate, and its validation transformed HIV drug development. The lesson: biological plausibility alone is not sufficient. You need epidemiological evidence that the surrogate is on the causal pathway between drug and outcome, and that the drug's effect on the surrogate accounts for a large fraction of its effect on the clinical outcome.

FDA Evidentiary Standards for Surrogate Endpoints

Accelerated Approval (21 CFR Part 314.510): FDA may approve a drug based on a "reasonably likely" surrogate endpoint, with post-marketing confirmatory trials required. Examples: objective response rate (ORR) in oncology, viral load in HIV, eGFR in CKD. This pathway has been transformative for rare diseases and oncology but has been criticized when confirmatory trials fail to materialize or show no benefit.

Full Approval: Requires validated surrogates — where the relationship between the surrogate and the clinical outcome is established through randomized trials with clinical endpoints. LDL-C, HbA1c, and blood pressure are the canonical validated surrogates in metabolic/cardiovascular disease.

The REMS 2022 reform: The Omnibus spending bill of 2022 significantly tightened the confirmatory trial requirements for accelerated approvals, addressing a historical weakness where drugs remained on market under accelerated approval indefinitely without confirmatory data.

Genomics in Translational Medicine: GWAS, NGS, and Somatic Mutations

The sequencing of the human genome, and the subsequent dramatic reduction in sequencing costs, fundamentally altered translational medicine. We moved from single-gene pharmacogenomics (CYP2D6 polymorphisms affecting drug metabolism) to genome-wide discovery and somatic cancer genomics at scale. This shift created both enormous opportunity and considerable analytical complexity.

Genome-Wide Association Studies (GWAS)

GWAS approaches scan hundreds of thousands to millions of single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) across the genome to identify associations with disease or drug response. The successes have been genuine — GWAS identified variants in PCSK9 that became the target for a whole class of lipid-lowering drugs, validated variants in IL-23R and NOD2 in inflammatory bowel disease, and identified APOE4 as a major Alzheimer's risk modifier. But GWAS findings are often in non-coding regulatory regions with small individual effect sizes, creating complex paths from GWAS signal to drug target to clinical biomarker.

The biobank era — UK Biobank (500,000 participants), FinnGen, All of Us — is amplifying GWAS power and enabling rare variant discovery at population scale. The translational challenge is determining which GWAS loci are druggable, which have effect sizes sufficient to be clinically useful predictive biomarkers, and how to validate them prospectively.

Next-Generation Sequencing and Somatic Mutations in Oncology

Next-generation sequencing (NGS) — encompassing targeted gene panels, whole-exome sequencing (WES), and whole-genome sequencing (WGS) — transformed oncology by revealing the somatic mutational landscape of tumors. The recognition that cancers of the same histological type could have entirely different mutational drivers, and that the same driver mutation could appear across multiple histological types, broke apart the organ-based classification of cancer and created the framework for tumor-agnostic approvals.

Tumor mutational burden (TMB) — the number of somatic mutations per megabase of sequenced genome — emerged as a predictive biomarker for immune checkpoint inhibitor response. FDA granted accelerated approval to pembrolizumab for TMB-high solid tumors in 2020 based on this biomarker, though subsequent data has shown heterogeneous predictive performance across tumor types, illustrating that even well-intentioned tumor-agnostic biomarker strategies require careful prospective validation in each histological context.

Proteomics, Metabolomics, and Multi-Omics Integration

Genomics tells you the code. Proteomics tells you what is being made. Metabolomics tells you what is happening biochemically. None of them alone gives you the full picture, and the integration of multiple omics layers — multi-omics — is where the most sophisticated translational programs now operate.

Proteomics in translational medicine has been driven by mass spectrometry (data-independent acquisition, or DIA-MS) and proximity extension assay platforms (Olink, SomaScan) capable of measuring thousands of proteins simultaneously in plasma or tissue. Roche, through its proteomics collaborations, built plasma protein panels that predict treatment response in multiple sclerosis. Novartis integrated proteomics into its Phase II biomarker programs for heart failure. The challenge has always been reproducibility and normalization across sites and platforms — problems that have been substantially but not completely resolved.

Metabolomics profiles small molecule metabolites in biofluids or tissues, with particular strength in identifying metabolic pathway engagement, off-target effects on metabolism, and gut microbiome interactions (through measurement of microbially-derived metabolites). In CNS drug development, metabolomic signatures in CSF have begun to complement neuroimaging and traditional CSF protein biomarkers.

Multi-omics integration requires sophisticated computational infrastructure — methods like MOFA (Multi-Omics Factor Analysis), DIABLO, and graph-based integration approaches — and raises genuine questions about which data types are truly independent and which are redundant. The translational medicine teams I have advised that do this well share one characteristic: they start with a biological question and then select their omics layers to answer it, rather than generating every possible dataset and hoping patterns emerge.

Liquid Biopsy: ctDNA, Circulating Tumor Cells, and Applications

Liquid biopsy — the detection of tumor-derived material in peripheral blood — is among the most consequential technical advances in translational oncology of the past decade. It addresses one of the most persistent problems in cancer biomarker development: the impracticality of repeated tissue biopsies to track disease evolution, treatment response, and resistance development.

Circulating Tumor DNA (ctDNA)

ctDNA consists of cell-free DNA fragments shed from tumor cells into the bloodstream, carrying tumor-specific somatic mutations, copy number alterations, and methylation patterns. Digital PCR and NGS-based detection (ddPCR, BEAMing, CAPP-Seq, Guardant360, FoundationOne Liquid CDx) enable detection of ctDNA at allele frequencies as low as 0.01-0.1% — well below the sensitivity of standard sequencing.

Clinical applications that are now established or rapidly establishing:

  • Genotyping: Identifying actionable mutations (EGFR, KRAS, PIK3CA) when tissue is insufficient or inaccessible
  • Minimal residual disease (MRD) detection: Post-surgical ctDNA detection predicts recurrence in colorectal, lung, and breast cancer; prospective MRD-guided trials are underway (CIRCULATE, DYNAMIC)
  • Resistance monitoring: Serial ctDNA tracks emergence of resistance mutations in real time (EGFR T790M, HER2 amplification, ALK resistance mutations)
  • Early detection: Multi-cancer early detection (MCED) tests (Galleri, CancerSEEK) aim to detect cancer signals years before clinical presentation — a T3 translational challenge requiring massive prospective validation

Circulating Tumor Cells (CTCs)

CTCs are intact tumor cells shed into circulation. CellSearch (FDA-cleared) quantifies CTCs in metastatic breast, colorectal, and prostate cancer; CTC count is prognostic in all three. The FDA cleared the first CTC-based predictive test — AR-V7 detection in CTCs — for identifying castration-resistant prostate cancer patients unlikely to respond to enzalutamide or abiraterone. CTC platforms also enable single-cell characterization of tumor biology at a depth not possible with bulk ctDNA analysis.

Disease Models: Selecting the Right Preclinical System

The translatability of a preclinical result depends almost entirely on how faithfully the model recapitulates the human disease biology being targeted. This is where the T1 gap originates — not in the chemistry, not in the PK, but in the model. I cannot overstate how much time and money has been burned on models that were technically excellent and biologically irrelevant.

The classic example is the xenograft model in oncology. For decades, subcutaneous human tumor xenografts in immunodeficient mice were the workhorse of preclinical efficacy evaluation. They are easy to grow, easy to measure, and highly reproducible. They are also poor predictors of clinical activity in most settings, because they lack the tumor microenvironment, lack an intact immune system, grow in the wrong tissue context, and are almost always derived from cell lines with decades of adaptation to in vitro culture conditions.

Model Selection Framework

The right model depends on the question being asked:

  • For target validation: Genetically engineered mouse models (GEMMs) expressing the human disease-relevant driver provide the most mechanistically authentic in vivo environment, at the cost of throughput and speed
  • For immuno-oncology: Syngeneic mouse models (MC38, CT26, B16) with intact immune systems, or humanized mouse models, are essential — conventional xenografts are completely inappropriate
  • For human-specific biology: Patient-derived xenografts (PDX) — direct implantation of fresh patient tumor into immunodeficient mice — maintain tumor heterogeneity, stromal elements, and the mutational landscape of the original tumor far better than cell line xenografts; PDX biobanks (PDMR, Champions, Champions TumorGraft) now enable biomarker-outcome analyses across hundreds of models
  • For CNS diseases: Species differences in blood-brain barrier transport, synaptic biology, and neuroinflammation make rodent models particularly treacherous; non-human primates (NHPs) are sometimes required for target engagement validation, but are expensive and carry their own translatability limitations
  • For rare diseases with limited animal biology: Induced pluripotent stem cell (iPSC) models — differentiating patient-derived iPSCs into the disease-relevant cell type — enable human disease biology in a dish; organoids from patient tissue (intestinal, hepatic, lung) model tissue-specific biology with unprecedented fidelity

The Replication Crisis and Preclinical Translation

A landmark 2011 analysis by Glenn Begley and Lee Ellis at Amgen found that only 6 of 53 "landmark" preclinical oncology papers could be reproduced. A similar Bayer analysis found that only ~25% of published target biology could be confirmed internally. This reproducibility crisis — driven by publication bias, underpowered studies, cell line authentication failures, reagent variability, and statistical malpractice — is a direct driver of the T1 translation gap. The solution is not simply more rigor (though rigor helps), but a fundamental shift to treating preclinical data as hypothesis-generating rather than hypothesis-confirming, and designing Phase I studies to test the hypothesis, not simply to establish safety and PK.

The Translational Gap: Why Drugs Fail in Phase II

The three canonical reasons drugs fail in Phase II — wrong patient, wrong dose, wrong endpoint — are not a list of bad luck. They are a map of translational failure modes, each traceable to specific gaps in the preclinical-to-clinical knowledge transfer.

Setting Up a Clinical Biomarker Program

A biomarker strategy document that lives in a PowerPoint deck is not a biomarker program. At Pfizer, I helped build one of the earliest formal translational medicine functions, and the lesson learned repeatedly was that the organizational infrastructure — labs, sample management, assay qualification, data integration, regulatory interface — is as important as the scientific content.

Laboratory Requirements: CAP/CLIA and Reference Labs

In the United States, clinical biomarker testing for regulatory submissions requires testing in laboratories certified under CLIA (Clinical Laboratory Improvement Amendments) and accredited by CAP (College of American Pathologists). This applies to any biomarker data that will support a regulatory decision — including patient eligibility testing in clinical trials. The distinction between a "research-use-only" (RUO) assay and a clinical trial assay is not semantic; the FDA expects CLIA/CAP-certified testing for enrollment decisions and clinical data.

Most large pharma companies maintain a network of contracted central reference laboratories (Quest Diagnostics, LabCorp, Covance/Labcorp Drug Development, PPD, Eurofins) for clinical trial biomarker testing, supplemented by academic reference centers with specific platform expertise (e.g., MD Anderson for IHC, Broad Institute for NGS). The sponsor's responsibility is:

  • Ensuring the assay is analytically validated before clinical use
  • Verifying CLIA/CAP certification of the reference lab (or equivalent international accreditation)
  • Establishing chain-of-custody procedures for sample collection, handling, shipment, and storage
  • Implementing quality control procedures (blinded replicates, reference controls, periodic audit)
  • Pre-specifying the biomarker analysis plan in the clinical protocol (or statistical analysis plan) before unblinding

Biomarker Strategy by Disease Area

Oncology

Oncology has led the development of the entire translational medicine paradigm, for both biological and regulatory reasons. The molecular heterogeneity of cancer — essentially every tumor is a unique genetic entity — demands patient selection. The FDA's willingness to accept objective response rate (ORR) as an accelerated approval surrogate endpoint has enabled faster development cycles. And the severity of the disease creates a regulatory and ethical context where the risk/benefit calculation for biomarker-selected populations is clearer than in chronic conditions.

The standard biomarker stack in contemporary oncology development includes: tumor molecular profiling (panel NGS or WES/WGS), PD-L1 immunohistochemistry (for immune checkpoint programs), TMB, MSI/MMR status, ctDNA at baseline and serially on treatment, and tissue biopsies for target engagement (when accessible). Programs that do not include this stack in 2026 face significant questions from FDA reviewers, DSMB members, and savvy investors.

CNS Diseases

CNS drug development has the worst overall translational track record of any therapeutic area, driven by several compounding factors: poor animal model translatability, extreme difficulty accessing target tissue for PD measurements (requiring lumbar puncture for CSF or PET imaging for brain readouts), long clinical endpoint timelines, and disease heterogeneity masked by DSM diagnostic categories that aggregate biologically distinct conditions.

The Alzheimer's field illustrates both the problem and the emerging solution. Decades of failed trials targeting amyloid were conducted in clinically-diagnosed patients who, we now understand, included a significant fraction without amyloid pathology — the "wrong patient" problem at massive scale. The adoption of amyloid PET and CSF amyloid/tau biomarkers for patient selection has finally enabled the field to enroll biologically-defined patients, leading to the first disease-modifying approvals (lecanemab, donanemab). The broader lesson is that CNS biomarker programs must invest heavily in imaging (PET ligands for target engagement), fluid biomarkers (CSF and now plasma p-tau, NfL, GFAP), and digital biomarkers (cognitive assessments, wearable biosensors) to build the translational infrastructure the biology demands.

Metabolic Disease

Metabolic diseases (diabetes, obesity, NAFLD/MASH, CKD) operate in a more favorable translational environment in one sense — the core biomarkers (HbA1c, fasting glucose, LDL-C, body weight, eGFR, liver enzymes) are analytically mature, reproducible, and accepted by regulators as meaningful endpoints. The translational challenge shifts from "does the drug do anything measurable?" to "does improvement in this biomarker predict long-term outcomes in this patient?"

The GLP-1 agonist story (semaglutide, tirzepatide) illustrates sophisticated translational thinking in metabolic disease: starting with a validated PD biomarker (GLP-1 receptor engagement, measured by glucagon suppression and insulin secretion patterns), moving to validated surrogates (HbA1c, body weight), and then confirming clinical outcomes benefit in dedicated cardiovascular outcomes trials (LEADER, SUSTAIN-6, SURMOUNT-4). The SURPASS and SURMOUNT programs used detailed metabolic biomarker panels to characterize the mechanism of weight loss (adipose tissue lipolysis markers, adipokines, inflammatory biomarkers) and distinguish the drug's mechanism from diet-alone or prior drugs.

Three Case Studies That Defined the Field

HER2 in Breast Cancer: Herceptin

Dennis Slamon's recognition in the mid-1980s that HER2 gene amplification — present in approximately 25% of breast cancers — defined a biologically distinct and particularly aggressive disease subtype, and that antibody-mediated HER2 blockade could reverse this aggressiveness in cell lines, was the founding insight of precision oncology. But the path from that insight to trastuzumab (Herceptin) approval in 1998 required solving every component of what would later be called translational medicine.

The HER2 biomarker had to be analytically validated — immunohistochemistry for protein overexpression (scored 0, 1+, 2+, 3+) and FISH for gene amplification were developed, cross-validated, and incorporated into CDx tests. The patient selection criterion (HER2 3+ or FISH-amplified) was prospectively applied in the pivotal Phase III trial, which showed dramatic survival benefit in the selected population. The co-approval of trastuzumab and the HercepTest CDx established the CDx paradigm for oncology. The subsequent recognition that HER2-low disease (IHC 1+ or 2+/FISH-negative) can also respond to antibody-drug conjugates (trastuzumab deruxtecan, T-DXd) has further refined the biomarker landscape — showing that even validated biomarkers evolve as biology and technology advance.

BCR-ABL in CML: Gleevec

Imatinib (Gleevec) and BCR-ABL represent the purest example of translational medicine working as intended. The Philadelphia chromosome translocation, producing the BCR-ABL fusion kinase, was established as the causal driver of CML through decades of cytogenetic and molecular biology research. Brian Druker's demonstration that STI571 (imatinib) could selectively inhibit BCR-ABL kinase activity and induce complete hematologic remission in CML patients was not a lucky discovery — it was the direct product of mechanism-based translational thinking, with a molecularly-defined patient population, a clear PD biomarker (BCR-ABL kinase activity), and a monitoring biomarker (BCR-ABL transcript levels by PCR) that tracked response and enabled early detection of resistance.

The imatinib story also illustrates T3 translation: the emergence of resistance mutations (most famously ABL T315I, the "gatekeeper" mutation) was detected through serial ctDNA/PCR monitoring, driving the development of second-generation (dasatinib, nilotinib) and third-generation (ponatinib, asciminib) inhibitors. The entire arc — from BCR-ABL discovery to the current landscape of mechanism-specific resistance-informed treatment sequencing — is a complete translational medicine success story spanning forty years.

PD-L1 in NSCLC: Keytruda

The pembrolizumab/PD-L1 story is more complicated and more instructive. The hypothesis — that PD-L1 expression on tumor cells reflected adaptive immune resistance and could predict which patients were most likely to respond to PD-1 blockade — was biologically sound. The IHC 22C3 assay development and analytical validation were executed carefully. The KEYNOTE-001 Phase I dose-expansion study, which prospectively measured PD-L1 expression and correlated it with response, provided the clinical validation that enabled the biomarker-selected design of KEYNOTE-024 (the pivotal trial).

But the PD-L1 biomarker story also illustrates translational complexity. PD-L1 expression is dynamic (changed by prior therapy, radiation, specimen age), heterogeneous within tumors, and measured differently by different antibodies on different platforms. The PD-L1 landscape in NSCLC involved four different drugs with four different CDx assays using four different antibodies — 22C3, 28-8, SP263, SP142 — with different scoring systems (TPS vs. CPS vs. IC scoring) and different cutoffs. The Blueprint PD-L1 IHC Assay Comparison Project attempted to harmonize these, with partial success. The ongoing imperfection of PD-L1 as a biomarker — it's predictive but not deterministic — has driven intense interest in better biomarkers (TMB, tumor microenvironment signatures, TCR diversity, gut microbiome composition) and has reinforced the lesson that a useful biomarker is not the same as a perfect biomarker.

Closing Perspective: What Translational Medicine Demands of Leaders

After forty years, I am convinced that the failure rate in drug development is not primarily a scientific problem. We understand biology well enough to design drugs that engage targets. The failure is translational: our persistent unwillingness, as an industry, to invest adequately in understanding human biology before committing to Phase III. The pressure to "move fast," to protect sunk costs, to avoid the difficult conversations that come when a PD biomarker shows no target engagement in humans — these organizational pathologies are as responsible for clinical failure as any scientific uncertainty.

Great translational medicine requires institutional courage. It requires leaders who will halt a program when the Phase I PD data are absent rather than convenient, who will invest in the longitudinal biosample collections that take years to generate meaningful data, who will design adaptive Phase II trials that answer biomarker questions rather than simply checking Phase II boxes. And it requires deep respect for the patients who volunteer for clinical trials — whose time, tissue, and trust are the raw materials of every translational dataset we generate.

"We owe it to patients to be honest about what we know and what we don't know before we ask them to participate in a clinical trial. A biomarker strategy is not a bureaucratic requirement — it is a moral commitment to not wasting the most precious resource in drug development: the willingness of sick people to help us learn." Observation after thirty-five years of patient interactions in clinical programs across Roche, Genentech, AbbVie, and Novartis
