This chapter is different. Every other chapter in this book draws on four decades of watching others build, break, and occasionally transform the pharmaceutical industry from inside its largest institutions. This chapter draws on something more immediate: the experience of building my own company right now, at this moment, with all the fear and exhilaration that entails.

I spent forty years inside the machine. Eli Lilly taught me what disciplined process looks like at scale. J&J showed me how a diversified health care conglomerate thinks about portfolio risk. GSK gave me a front-row seat to the brutal efficiency drives of the 2010s. AbbVie, Novo Nordisk, Roche, Pfizer, AstraZeneca, Novartis, Merck, Bristol Myers Squibb, Gilead — each one layered something onto my understanding of how drugs actually get made, approved, and sold. Harvard and MIT let me step back and think more carefully about why so much of that process is broken.

Then, two years ago, I stopped watching. I started building.

What follows is not theory. It is not a cleaned-up retrospective. It is the founder's playbook I wish I had held in my hands on the day I signed my first term sheet with a VC, the day I hired my first employee, the day my co-founder and I argued over equity splits at 11pm in a Cambridge coffee shop. I will share the frameworks that held up and the assumptions that shattered. I will name the mistakes I made so you don't have to repeat them. And I will try, throughout, to be honest about the parts that are still hard — because the romanticization of biotech entrepreneurship does real damage to the people who attempt it naively.

The Decision to Start: When to Leave Big Pharma

The most common question I receive from mid-career pharma executives and academic scientists is some variant of: "How do I know when it's time?" The answer I've arrived at is not the one they expect.

The question is not "when is it time?" The question is "what, specifically, am I building, and for whom, and why can I do it better than anyone else on the planet right now?" If you can answer that question with precision and conviction, timing becomes secondary. If you cannot, no amount of readiness will save you.

That said, there are structural conditions that make founding more tractable than others. The AI era has collapsed many of the barriers that previously required a large organization to clear. Protein structure prediction, generative chemistry, clinical trial simulation, regulatory document automation — each of these previously demanded teams of 50 to 200 specialists. A founding team of four with the right AI stack can now accomplish in eighteen months what took a Big Pharma R&D department five years. This changes the calculus of when to leave significantly.

In concrete terms, I left when three conditions converged:

  1. Scientific clarity: I had a specific mechanistic hypothesis I believed in deeply enough to bet my career on, supported by unpublished data I had helped generate during my academic work.
  2. Market confirmation: Conversations with fifteen patient advocates, eight treating physicians, and four payers had convinced me that the unmet need was real, severe, and commercially addressable.
  3. Team conviction: Two people I genuinely respected — one a computational biologist, one a former CFO from a mid-size biotech — had agreed to join me. I was not starting alone.
"The best founders I've backed didn't quit Big Pharma because they were frustrated. They quit because they were obsessed with a specific problem they couldn't solve from inside a large organization. Obsession is the only fuel that burns long enough." — Bruce Booth, Atlas Venture

Idea Validation: Before You Spend a Dollar

The single most expensive mistake in biotech is falling in love with a scientific idea before validating whether it constitutes a viable business. I have watched brilliant scientists spend three years and $30 million building compounds for a disease that had a competitor eighteen months ahead of them — a fact that a week of rigorous competitive landscaping would have revealed on day one.

The idea validation framework I now use and teach involves four distinct analyses, each of which must be completed before any capital is committed beyond a few thousand dollars of your own time:

1. Scientific Hypothesis Stress-Testing

Write a two-page document explaining, in plain language, why your biological hypothesis should be true. Then give it to three scientists who are not your friends and ask them to try to break it. The ones who push back hardest are the most valuable. If your hypothesis survives that process with its core intact, you have something.

2. Unmet Need Documentation

Talk to patients first, physicians second, payers third. In that order, always. Patient advocacy organizations will take your call even when you have no company name. The conversations will either validate your assumption that the current standard of care is genuinely inadequate, or they will reveal that "unmet need" is more nuanced than it appeared from the literature.

3. Competitive Landscape Mapping

Use Citeline, Cortellis, and ClinicalTrials.gov to map every program in your target indication at every stage of development. Map them on two axes: mechanism and stage. Where is the white space? If you are entering an area with three assets in Phase 3, your differentiation argument needs to be bulletproof. If you are genuinely first-in-class, your biological rationale needs to be airtight.

4. IP Freedom-to-Operate Preliminary Screen

Before any formal filing, spend $3,000-$5,000 on a preliminary FTO screen through a patent attorney. Confirm that the composition of matter or method of use you intend to pursue is not already claimed in a way that would block your path. This is not a substitute for a full FTO analysis — which you will need at the Series A — but it prevents the catastrophic scenario of building a company on encumbered science.

Choosing the Founding Team

Investors fund teams, not ideas. This is not a platitude. The same scientific hypothesis in the hands of two different founding teams will produce wildly different outcomes. The question of who to found with deserves more care than most first-time founders give it.

The founding team for a drug discovery startup needs, at minimum, four capabilities: deep domain biology, medicinal or computational chemistry or both, drug development operations (IND to proof-of-concept), and business development/finance. In a four-person founding team, each person covers one primary domain with overlap. In a two- or three-person team, some of these roles must be covered by early hires or scientific advisory board members — which is acceptable but creates gaps that compound over time.

What I did not appreciate before starting is how much the interpersonal architecture of the founding team matters. Founding a company with someone is more intimate than marriage in several respects: you will be under severe financial and emotional stress simultaneously, you will make decisions with incomplete information constantly, and you will need to trust your co-founder's judgment in domains where your own expertise is limited. I now ask prospective co-founders a question that sounds strange the first time you hear it: "What do you do when you're wrong?" People who have never been wrong are the most dangerous founding partners.

Founding Team Capability Checklist

  • Scientific credibility: Publications in target biology; relationships with key opinion leaders; ability to attract SAB members
  • Drug development experience: At least one IND filing; clinical operations exposure; regulatory familiarity
  • Business/finance: Term sheet literacy; financial modeling; investor network
  • Operational execution: Vendor management; hiring; organizational design under capital constraints
  • Conflict resolution: Demonstrated ability to disagree constructively; track record of admitting mistakes

Company Formation: The Legal Foundation

Get this right. The formation documents you sign in month one will shadow your company for fifteen years. Lawyers who specialize in life sciences startups — firms like Cooley, Gunderson, Ropes & Gray, Wilson Sonsini — have seen every variant of founder mistake. Hire one of them, even if it feels expensive. It is not expensive relative to the cost of unwinding a poorly structured founding.

Delaware C-Corporation

Incorporate in Delaware. Not your home state, not a simple LLC. Institutional investors, at every stage from seed to IPO, require Delaware C-corps. Delaware corporate law is the most developed and predictable in the world for governance disputes. The additional administrative cost of incorporating out of state is trivial. Do this on day one.

IP Assignment Agreements

Every founder, every early employee, every contractor must sign an IP assignment agreement before they do a single hour of work for the company. This agreement must clearly state that all work product, inventions, and developments created in connection with their role belong to the company, not the individual. Failure to execute this agreement properly is the most common reason early-stage biotechs fail due diligence at the Series A. I have personally seen a financing collapse because a founding scientist had failed to formally assign a key patent from his university lab to the company.

Equity Splits and Founder Vesting

Founding equity should reflect each founder's actual contribution going forward, not their historical status. A common mistake is splitting equity equally among founders out of politeness when the actual contribution profile is highly asymmetric. Have the uncomfortable conversation before you sign anything. It is far easier to have it then than to renegotiate after twelve months of unequal effort.

All founder equity must vest. The standard structure is a four-year vesting schedule with a one-year cliff: nothing vests in the first twelve months; 25% vests at month thirteen; the remaining 75% vests monthly over the following thirty-six months. The one-year cliff protects the remaining founders if someone exits early. The monthly vesting after the cliff prevents a co-founder from leaving after two years with half their equity having vested in a single lump sum. Investors will require this structure. Implement it from day one regardless.

IP Strategy from Day One

Intellectual property is the asset around which your entire company is built. Not the team, not the data, not the relationships — though all of those matter — but the IP. If your IP is weak, contested, or encumbered, you do not have a company. You have a science project.

University IP Transfers

If your founding science originated in a university laboratory, you almost certainly do not own it. Universities own IP generated by their faculty, staff, and students using university resources. Technology transfer offices (TTOs) license that IP to startups through exclusive or non-exclusive licenses, typically in exchange for equity, upfront fees, milestone payments, and royalties. Negotiating with a TTO requires patience and a specialized attorney. Common sticking points include royalty rates (typically 1-5% of net sales), sublicensing rights (critical for partnership deals), field-of-use restrictions, and diligence obligations. Understand every term before you sign the license agreement. These terms cannot easily be renegotiated once your company has value.

Former Employer IP

If you developed your founding hypothesis while employed at a pharmaceutical company, you may face a claim that the IP belongs to your former employer. Review your prior employment agreement carefully with a lawyer before founding. Most pharmaceutical employment agreements include broad IP assignment clauses that cover work done using company resources or related to the company's business. "Related to" is interpreted broadly. If there is any ambiguity, have your attorney seek a written confirmation from your former employer that they are not asserting a claim to the relevant technology. Do not rely on a verbal assurance.

Provisional Patents and the 12-Month Window

File a provisional patent application on your core technology as early as possible. Provisionals are relatively inexpensive ($1,500-$3,000 in attorney fees for most biotech claims), establish your priority date, and give you twelve months to decide whether to convert to a non-provisional PCT or US application. Use those twelve months to generate the data that will strengthen your claims. Do not use the twelve-month window to do nothing. The clock runs regardless.

Funding the Company: The Full Journey

Biotech company financing is a relay race, not a sprint. Each funding stage has a specific purpose, a specific audience, and specific expectations about what you will have delivered before asking for the next check. Confusing these stages — approaching Series A investors before you have seed-stage proof points, for example — wastes time and destroys credibility in a small community where reputations are long-lived.

Non-Dilutive Funding: SBIR, STTR, and Grants

Many founders overlook non-dilutive government funding, which is a significant mistake. The NIH's Small Business Innovation Research (SBIR) program awards Phase I grants up to $300,000 and Phase II grants up to $2 million to small companies conducting health-related research. The Small Business Technology Transfer (STTR) program operates similarly but requires a formal partnership with a research institution — ideal for university spinouts. BARDA funds medical countermeasures development and can provide substantially larger grants to companies working in infectious disease, chemical/biological/radiological/nuclear threats, and pandemic preparedness. These grants do not dilute your equity. They are also powerful validators: a successful SBIR application demonstrates that your scientific premise has passed external peer review, which meaningfully reduces investor skepticism at the seed stage.

The IPO and Alternative Paths to Liquidity

The traditional Nasdaq biotech IPO remains the gold standard path to public markets. The process takes six to twelve months from the decision to go public to your first day of trading. Key milestones include: selecting lead underwriters (Goldman, JP Morgan, Morgan Stanley, Jefferies, and Cowen dominate the biotech IPO space); filing the S-1 registration statement with the SEC; completing an SEC review and comment process; conducting an institutional roadshow of approximately two weeks; pricing the offering; and beginning public trading. Lock-up agreements, typically 180 days, prevent insider selling immediately post-IPO and create a period of acute vulnerability to stock price volatility.

SPAC mergers and reverse mergers offer alternative routes to public markets with shorter timelines but have fallen significantly out of favor following the SPAC boom and bust of 2020-2022. Most institutional investors view SPAC-listed biotechs with skepticism. If your goal is a high-quality public company with long-term institutional sponsorship, pursue a traditional IPO.

Building the Organization: Virtual vs. Integrated Models

One of the most consequential early decisions a biotech founder makes is the model of organizational build-out. The spectrum runs from fully virtual — where a small core team manages a network of CROs, CMOs, and specialized service providers — to fully integrated, with dedicated laboratory space, in-house chemistry and biology teams, and owned manufacturing capabilities. Most successful early-stage biotechs operate somewhere in between, but the pendulum has shifted decisively toward virtual and semi-virtual models over the past decade, and AI is accelerating that shift further.

Board Governance and the Scientific Advisory Board

Board Composition and Dynamics

Your board of directors will be your most important institutional relationship and your most challenging management challenge simultaneously. A typical early-stage biotech board has five to seven members: two or three investor directors (representatives of your lead VCs), one or two independent directors with specific domain expertise (regulatory, commercial, or prior CEO experience), and one or two management directors (typically the CEO, and sometimes the CSO).

Choose your independent directors as carefully as you choose your co-founders. The best independent directors have built or sold companies in your disease area, have FDA or senior regulatory experience, or have built commercial organizations in your target market. They bring perspective that investor directors, who typically sit on many boards simultaneously, cannot provide. They also serve as a check on investor directors when your interests diverge — and they will diverge, because investors are optimizing for portfolio returns and you are optimizing for your specific company.

The board dynamic I was least prepared for was the pace at which investor directors recalibrate their conviction based on public market comps. In 2021 and early 2022, every board meeting felt like an accelerant; in late 2022 and 2023, as public biotech valuations corrected sharply, the same investors were asking harder questions about capital efficiency and partnership alternatives. This is rational behavior on their part. It is emotionally difficult on yours. Develop a thick skin early.

Scientific Advisory Board (SAB)

The SAB is your external scientific credibility engine. A well-constructed SAB tells investors, partners, and regulators that the world's leading experts in your disease area believe your scientific approach is sound. A weak or misaligned SAB tells them the opposite.

Recruit SAB members who are genuinely expert in your specific target biology, not just broadly famous in adjacent fields. The SAB member who has published the defining papers on your target pathway is more valuable than a Nobel laureate whose work is tangentially related. Aim for four to eight members. Compensate them with stock options — typically 0.1% to 0.25% of fully diluted shares each, vesting over two to three years — not cash, which aligns their incentives with the company's success.

Make SAB meetings productive. I have seen companies use their SAB as a rubber-stamp audience for management presentations. This is a waste of extraordinary expertise. Come to SAB meetings with your hardest scientific questions, your most concerning data, and your genuine uncertainty. The best SAB interactions I have had were ones where I left with a fundamentally different understanding of a key scientific question. That kind of intellectual friction is what you are paying for.

Key Milestone Planning: From Seed to IPO

Biotech companies do not advance on time; they advance on milestones. Every dollar you raise is tied to a specific set of scientific and regulatory deliverables that, when achieved, will de-risk the investment sufficiently to justify the next financing. Understanding the logic of milestone sequencing — what must be true before investors will commit to the next stage — is one of the most important things a founder can know.

Value Inflection Point Logic: The Investor's Lens

Investors are not paying for what you have done; they are paying for what you will be able to demonstrate with the capital they provide, and for the resulting increase in your company's value. Every financing should be structured around a specific set of milestones that, if achieved, will materially increase your valuation and your ability to raise the next round on better terms. If you cannot articulate those milestones precisely, you are not ready to raise.

  • Seed → Series A: Lead molecule selected; in vivo proof of concept; IND-enabling studies initiated; IP filed
  • Series A → Series B: IND clearance; Phase 1 dosing begins; preliminary safety data; second asset in pipeline
  • Series B → Series C/Crossover: Phase 1 complete; proof of mechanism in patients; Phase 2 design finalized
  • Crossover → IPO: Phase 2 efficacy signal; pivotal trial enrollment on track; regulatory path clear; experienced commercial team in place

AI-Enabled Pharma Startup: The Changed Equation

I want to be direct about something: the AI era has not changed the fundamental challenge of drug development. Getting a molecule safely and effectively into humans remains as biologically complex as it has ever been. Phase 2 failure rates are not yet lower because of AI. The FDA has not shortened its review timelines. Clinical trial enrollment remains slow, variable, and expensive.

What AI has changed is the early discovery phase and the organizational equation. A team of four scientists with access to AlphaFold structure prediction, generative chemistry platforms, and large language models for literature synthesis can now do the work that previously required a team of forty. That collapses the capital required to reach a lead candidate by a factor of five to ten. It compresses the timeline to IND from four to five years to eighteen to thirty months. And it means that the founding team of a credible AI-enabled biotech can be meaningfully smaller than was possible five years ago, which in turn means your dilution at the seed stage is lower and your ownership at the IPO is higher.

The specific AI tools that matter most for a drug discovery startup in 2025 are: protein structure prediction and dynamics (AlphaFold 3, Boltz-1, RoseTTAFold); generative chemistry (Schrödinger, Insilico Medicine, Recursion's platform); clinical trial design optimization and patient enrichment (TriNetX, Flatiron, Medidata AI); and regulatory document generation (specialized tools that have emerged from Veeva, Salesforce Life Sciences, and several well-funded startups).

What AI cannot yet do: replace genuine biological insight about why a target is relevant in human disease; replace clinical judgment about trial design; replace regulatory wisdom about what the FDA will and will not accept; and replace the human relationships with key opinion leaders, patient advocates, and payers that ultimately determine whether a medicine reaches patients. Those remain the core competencies of the experienced drug developer — which is precisely why experienced drug developers who can deploy AI tools have an extraordinary advantage in this moment.

Regulatory Strategy as a Startup

Many early-stage founders treat FDA engagement as something that happens after the science is mature. This is wrong. Regulatory strategy should begin at the founding of the company. The choice of indication, patient population, primary endpoint, and comparator — all of which shape your clinical development plan — has profound regulatory implications that must be understood before you commit capital to IND-enabling studies.

The FDA offers several mechanisms for early engagement that are underused by startup founders. Pre-IND meetings allow you to discuss your planned IND package with FDA reviewers before submission. Type A and Type B meetings can be requested to get FDA feedback on specific development questions. Breakthrough Therapy designation, Fast Track designation, and Rare Pediatric Disease designation each offer benefits — including more frequent FDA interaction and, in some cases, priority review vouchers worth hundreds of millions of dollars — that can be transformative for a small company's development timeline and economics.

Hire a regulatory advisor with FDA relationships early. The cost — typically $200-$500 per hour for a senior regulatory consultant, or a small equity package for a part-time Chief Regulatory Officer — is trivial relative to the cost of a regulatory misstep that delays your development program by twelve to eighteen months.

Strategic Options: Build and Sell vs. Build and Commercialize

When I was at Big Pharma companies, I participated in dozens of business development discussions about acquiring early-stage biotechs. The pattern I observed was consistent: companies built explicitly to be acquired, with clean IP, a well-chosen indication, and a management team focused on value creation rather than empire building, consistently received better acquisition terms than companies that had drifted into sale discussions reluctantly after IPO underperformance.

The "build and sell" vs. "build and commercialize" question is ultimately a question of what kind of company you want to build and what your investors have agreed to fund. Most early-stage VC funds are comfortable with either outcome; what they are not comfortable with is a management team that is ambivalent or strategically inconsistent about which path they are pursuing. Choose your strategy, communicate it clearly to your board, and execute accordingly.

My current company is explicitly built to be a platform that we intend to commercialize. That means we have invested more heavily in building organizational capabilities — regulatory, commercial, medical affairs — earlier than a pure "build and sell" company would. It means we have a longer capital timeline. And it means that when Big Pharma companies come to us with partnership discussions — and they will — we negotiate from a position of not needing the deal.

Partnership with Big Pharma: When to Take the Deal

Every successful biotech founder I know has received partnership overtures from Big Pharma that, in retrospect, they either took too early, too late, or at the wrong terms. Getting this right is genuinely difficult because the right answer depends on your specific scientific risk profile, your capital position, your pipeline depth, and the strategic alternatives available to you at that moment.

The deals to take: upfront payments of $50M+ for a single asset with a market cap below $500M; co-development deals that provide clinical execution capability you genuinely cannot build internally; partnerships with strategic partners who bring patient access infrastructure in markets where you have no commercial presence.

The deals to decline: deals that give away global rights to your platform or lead asset for less than full value; partnerships with companies whose commercial infrastructure is misaligned with your patient population; deals structured as options that give the partner the right to acquire you at a pre-specified price — these are almost always underpriced.

Common Founder Mistakes

The Mistakes I Made and the Mistakes I Watched Others Make

  • Hiring for credentials instead of velocity: The Nobel laureate who cannot execute in a startup environment is a liability. Hire people who have built things under resource constraints.
  • Raising too little in the seed round to preserve dilution: If you run out of money nine months before your next milestone, you will raise a bridge at punitive terms. Raise enough to reach a genuine value inflection with three months of buffer.
  • Not terminating underperforming hires quickly enough: Startup organizations are too small to carry underperformers. The cost of a wrong hire compounds every week they remain.
  • Letting the scientific narrative drift from the commercial narrative: Investors, partners, and regulators need a consistent story about what you are building and why it matters to patients. Inconsistency in that narrative is fatal.
  • Neglecting the SAB: Assembling a world-class SAB and then never engaging them substantively is one of the great wasted resources in biotech. Their networks alone are worth the equity you give them.
  • Taking a board seat from an investor whose fund lifecycle doesn't align with yours: A VC whose fund is in year eight with a ten-year life has different incentives than a VC in year three. Know whose money you're taking.
  • Forgetting that regulatory strategy is business strategy: The fastest scientific path is not always the fastest regulatory path. Always run these in parallel.

Case Studies: How Three Companies Built From Nothing

Case Study 1: Moderna — Platform Before Product

Moderna was founded in 2010 by Robert Langer, Derrick Rossi, and colleagues at Flagship Pioneering on a scientific insight: modified mRNA could instruct the body to produce its own therapeutic proteins. The founding hypothesis was platform-level, not asset-level — meaning the scientific bet was that mRNA could work as a medicine category, not that a specific product in a specific indication would succeed.

This platform-first approach had profound implications for how Moderna was funded and built. Flagship provided the first capital and shepherded the company through its first five years. Moderna raised more than $2 billion in private financing before its IPO in 2018 — one of the largest biotech IPOs in history at that point. The COVID-19 pandemic, which produced the mRNA-1273 vaccine in eleven months from sequence to emergency use authorization, validated the platform hypothesis in the most dramatic possible way.

Founder lesson: A defensible platform IP position, even without near-term clinical proof, can attract extraordinary capital if the scientific rationale is compelling and the founding team has world-class credibility. However, platform companies require patient capital, strong investor conviction, and the ability to survive years without clinical proof points.

Case Study 2: Karuna Therapeutics — Asset-Centric Precision

Karuna was founded in 2018 around a single asset: KarXT, a combination of xanomeline (a muscarinic receptor agonist with known antipsychotic properties, originally studied by Eli Lilly in the 1990s) and trospium (a peripherally restricted muscarinic antagonist designed to mitigate the side effects that had stopped xanomeline's development). The scientific hypothesis was elegant: the mechanism was validated, the tolerability problem was understood, and the solution was mechanistically rational.

Karuna raised a $32 million Series A in 2018, went public on Nasdaq in 2019, and produced Phase 3 clinical data demonstrating significant efficacy in schizophrenia without dopamine-related side effects. Bristol Myers Squibb acquired Karuna in 2024 for $14 billion — a return that delivered extraordinary value to its founders and investors.

Founder lesson: An asset-centric company with a mechanistically differentiated hypothesis and clean IP can create extraordinary value with relatively modest capital by choosing its indication precisely and executing its clinical program flawlessly. The $14 billion acquisition returned roughly 200x on the earliest seed capital.

Case Study 3: Roivant Sciences — The Asset-Centric Platform

Roivant, founded by Vivek Ramaswamy in 2014, pursued a fundamentally different model: rather than discovering new drugs, Roivant licensed de-prioritized assets from Big Pharma companies — assets that had been abandoned for business rather than scientific reasons — and built subsidiary companies ("Vants") around them with focused, experienced teams and accelerated development timelines. Each Vant (Myovant, Aruvant, Enzyvant, etc.) had its own management team, its own investors, and its own IPO trajectory.

The model was premised on the insight that large pharmaceutical companies regularly discontinue scientifically viable assets due to internal resource constraints, portfolio prioritization decisions, and therapeutic area strategy shifts. Roivant's competitive advantage was the ability to identify those assets, negotiate licenses quickly, and execute clinical development with a lean, experienced team.

Founder lesson: Innovation in biotech does not require discovering new science. Identifying undervalued or under-resourced assets, licensing them on favorable terms, and building focused development teams around them can generate extraordinary returns. The business model innovation was as important as any scientific insight.

The Emotional Architecture of Founding

I did not expect to write this section. I am a scientist and a businessman, not a therapist. But I would be doing a disservice to every person who reads this chapter as they contemplate founding a company if I did not address what the experience actually feels like from the inside.

Founding a pharmaceutical company is an exercise in sustained uncertainty management. Every week brings decisions that must be made with incomplete information, under capital pressure, with the knowledge that failure means not just financial loss but the failure to bring a medicine to patients who need it. That weight is real, and it is heavier than anything I experienced in forty years at large organizations where individual decisions rarely felt existential.

The things that have sustained me: a co-founder I genuinely trust, whose judgment I respect even when I disagree with it. A board independent director who has been through it before and gives me honest counsel without agenda. A small scientific advisory board that believes in the biology as deeply as I do. And the patients. I have met seven patients with the disease we are targeting. They are the reason this is worth the difficulty. When the financing falls apart, when the data comes back ambiguous, when the team is struggling — I think about those seven people.

"Starting a company in your sixties after forty years in pharma is an act of audacity. But audacity backed by forty years of pattern recognition is not the same as recklessness. It is the best possible timing for the work that matters most." — A mentor's words, on the day I handed in my resignation letter

Chapter Summary: What Forty Years Taught Me About Founding

Building a pharmaceutical company from scratch is not for the faint-hearted, but it is not as impossible as it looks from inside a large organization. The structural advantages that large pharma companies once held — scale, resources, regulatory experience, commercial infrastructure — have been substantially democratized by the CRO ecosystem, by AI tools that collapse discovery timelines, and by a deep and sophisticated venture capital community that has learned how to fund and build biotech companies systematically.

What you bring as a founder that no tool or investor can replicate: deep domain knowledge of a specific disease and its biology; credibility with the scientific community; relationships with patients, physicians, and regulators; and the judgment to make good decisions with incomplete information under time pressure. That combination is rare. If you have it, the ecosystem will provide the rest.

The decision I made two years ago — to leave the security of institutional science and build something — was the most difficult professional decision of my life. It was also, I am increasingly certain, the right one. The chapter that follows, on the future of pharma, will explore where the industry is heading over the next decade. This chapter was about what happens when one person decides not to wait for the industry to change itself, but to be part of changing it.

Start. The patients are waiting.
