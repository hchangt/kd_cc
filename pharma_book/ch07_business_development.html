7.1 The Business Development Function: Architecture and Purpose

Business Development in the pharmaceutical industry occupies a peculiar position of extraordinary power exercised through perpetual uncertainty. In my four decades spanning Eli Lilly, J&J, GSK, AbbVie, Novo Nordisk, Roche, Pfizer, AstraZeneca, Novartis, Merck, BMS, and Gilead, I have witnessed BD transform from a relatively peripheral licensing function into the central engine of pharmaceutical value creation. Today, well over 60% of all approved medicines in the United States originated from or were substantially shaped by an external transaction. The BD professional is therefore not a deal-maker in the narrow transactional sense — they are the architect of a company's scientific future.

The organizational structure of a BD function reflects the company's strategic philosophy. At most large pharma companies, BD sits at the intersection of R&D, commercial, and corporate strategy. The Chief Business Officer (CBO) — a title that has largely replaced the older "SVP Business Development" — typically reports directly to the CEO and holds a seat on the executive committee. This elevation is not cosmetic. When Pfizer's Albert Bourla called Ian Read for counsel on the BioNTech mRNA COVID vaccine deal in early 2020, BD professionals were in the room making multi-billion-dollar decisions in real time with incomplete data. That is the job.

7.1.1 Organizational Structure

A mature BD organization within a large-cap pharma typically organizes along three axes: therapeutic area alignment, deal-type specialization, and geographic coverage. Therapeutic area (TA) BD directors maintain deep scientific fluency within a domain — oncology, immunology, neuroscience, cardiovascular — and serve as the primary scientific interlocutors with external partners. Deal-type specialists focus on M&A (supported by a dedicated Corporate Development team), licensing transactions, or alliance management. Geographic teams cover the US, Europe, Asia-Pacific, and emerging markets, reflecting the reality that a significant portion of BD activity now originates from Chinese biotech, Korean pharma, and Japanese specialty companies.

The BD organization maintains a continuous pipeline of opportunity across several stages: (1) deal sourcing and origination, (2) scientific screening and initial assessment, (3) full due diligence, (4) term sheet negotiation and deal structuring, and (5) post-deal integration and alliance management. Each stage has distinct skill requirements, and the best BD leaders build teams that can move fluidly across all of them.

7.1.2 BD's Role in Corporate Strategy

BD is the implementation mechanism for pipeline gap analysis — the systematic identification of where a company's internal R&D will leave it exposed in five to ten years. At AbbVie, the strategic imperative to address Humira's biosimilar cliff beginning in 2023 drove the $63B acquisition of Allergan in 2020, diversifying into aesthetics, neuroscience, and eye care. At AstraZeneca, Pascal Soriot's deliberate strategic decision to rebuild oncology capability through BD — the $1.6B deal for Daiichi Sankyo's trastuzumab deruxtecan (Enhertu) in 2019, later expanded to $6.9B — transformed the company from a mid-tier player into an oncology powerhouse. These were not opportunistic transactions. They were the execution of precisely articulated portfolio strategies.

Pharma transactions span a wide spectrum from narrow, territory-limited co-promotion agreements to full corporate acquisitions. Understanding the strategic logic, risk profile, and governance implications of each structure is foundational to BD competency.

7.3 Deal Sourcing: Where the Best Transactions Are Found

The most dangerous misconception in BD is that deals find their way to you. They do not. The best assets — those that will define a company's next decade — are rarely presented through formal RFP processes or inbound banker calls. They emerge from relationships built over years, scientific intelligence gathered at conferences, and the kind of trust that only develops when a BD professional has demonstrated both scientific credibility and institutional follow-through.

7.3.1 Conference Intelligence

The major scientific and industry conferences are not merely venues for scientific exchange — they are the marketplace for BD deal flow. Understanding how to work each conference is a core BD competency.

J.P. Morgan Healthcare Conference (San Francisco, January): The premier event for CEO-to-CEO deal discussions. At JPM, the formal presentations are almost irrelevant — the real activity happens in the suite meetings at the Westin St. Francis and Mark Hopkins hotels, where companies have 30-minute pre-scheduled meetings that run from 7am to 10pm for four days. A senior BD team from a large pharma may schedule 60–80 meetings in three days. The goal is not to close deals at JPM; it is to identify which 5–10 companies merit deeper scientific diligence in Q1.

BIO International Convention (rotating US cities, June): The largest partnering conference in the industry, with a structured one-on-one meeting system (BIO's "One-on-One Partnering" platform) that generates tens of thousands of meetings annually. BIO is particularly valuable for early-stage assets and smaller biotechs that lack the CEO relationships to get JPM suite access. A disciplined BD team will use BIO's partnering system to surface 150–200 pre-screened companies and prioritize 40–50 in-person meetings.

ASCO Annual Meeting (Chicago, June): The American Society of Clinical Oncology annual meeting is the single most important data readout event in oncology. For oncology BD teams, ASCO abstracts (published 2 weeks before the meeting) trigger intensive internal scientific review. A data readout at ASCO that validates a novel mechanism can generate 20+ competing term sheets within 72 hours. BD professionals who have built relationships with KOLs (Key Opinion Leaders) and biotech CEOs before the data drops have a significant advantage.

Other critical events include ASH (hematology), EASD/ADA (diabetes), AASLD (liver), ESC/AHA (cardiovascular), and the specialized academic symposia that precede major publications in journals like NEJM, The Lancet, and Nature Medicine.

7.3.2 Proactive Scouting and Scientific Intelligence

World-class BD teams maintain a systematic scientific scouting function — sometimes called "scientific intelligence" or "innovation scouting" — that operates independently of the conference cycle. This involves: monitoring ClinicalTrials.gov for novel mechanism registrations; tracking IND filings; analyzing patent landscapes for emerging platforms; building relationships with academic PIs at target institutions; and maintaining intelligence on VC portfolio companies before they reach the public-facing partnering stage.

At Roche, we maintained a team of 12 scientific scouts embedded in Boston, San Francisco, New York, London, and Basel whose exclusive mandate was to identify assets 18–36 months before they would otherwise come to market. This proactive sourcing generated several of Genentech's most important licensing transactions — not through banker-led processes, but through PI relationships and VC network engagement built over years.

Key Insight: The Network Multiplier Effect

In pharma BD, your personal network has a half-life: the relationships you built at your last company retain value for roughly 5–7 years before requiring active renewal. The most effective BD professionals deliberately cultivate multi-directional networks — biotech CEOs, VC partners, academic PIs, regulatory consultants, and competitor BD colleagues. The last group may seem counterintuitive, but competitor BD teams frequently collaborate on deals (co-development structures), and maintaining collegial relationships enables the intelligence-sharing that benefits all parties in a non-zero-sum way.

7.4 Due Diligence: The Unglamorous Engine of Deal Quality

The deal negotiation is what people see. Due diligence is what determines whether the deal is actually worth doing. In my experience, the most expensive mistakes in pharma BD — the deals that destroyed shareholder value — were almost invariably the result of inadequate diligence, not poor negotiation. A 10% improvement in deal terms is irrelevant if the underlying asset doesn't work.

7.4.1 Scientific Due Diligence

Scientific diligence operates at three levels: target validation, mechanism of action plausibility, and data quality assessment. Target validation asks whether the biological target is genuinely implicated in the disease and whether modulation of that target will produce the desired clinical effect. This requires independent review of the genetics literature (Mendelian randomization, GWAS data, rare variant analyses), animal model data, and any ex vivo or biomarker data from human subjects.

Mechanism of action review assesses whether the proposed therapeutic modality — small molecule, antibody, ADC, RNA therapy, cell therapy — is appropriate for the target, and whether the PK/PD relationship is understood well enough to define a therapeutic window. The most common error in early-stage scientific diligence is over-weighting animal data without scrutinizing the translational assumptions embedded in moving from rodent to human.

Data quality assessment is where most scientific diligence teams spend insufficient time. The questions are brutal but necessary: Were the preclinical studies conducted with blinding and appropriate controls? Were the in vitro efficacy assays conducted at physiologically relevant concentrations? Is the biomarker strategy defined? Has the manufacturing process been de-risked at scale? For clinical-stage assets, the statistical analysis plan, patient selection criteria, and endpoint definitions require forensic scrutiny — particularly for single-arm Phase 2 studies where objective response rates are not contextualized against historical control rates.

7.4.2 Commercial Due Diligence

Commercial diligence is simultaneously the most important and the most frequently poorly-executed component of pharmaceutical BD. Its purpose is to produce a defensible forecast of the asset's commercial opportunity under realistic assumptions — not the assumptions that make the deal look attractive, but the assumptions that represent the honest probability-weighted central case.

Market sizing begins with epidemiology: how many patients carry the relevant diagnosis, how many are in the targetable treatment pathway, and what proportion will realistically receive this therapy given cost, access, competitive alternatives, and physician behavior. The most common error is conflating diagnosed prevalence with addressable market — a mistake that systematically overstates opportunity in rare diseases, where diagnostic rates may be 20–40% of true prevalence, and in oncology, where biomarker-selected populations may represent a small fraction of all-comers.

Competitive landscape analysis requires not just cataloguing existing approved therapies but modeling the pipeline of assets likely to be approved within the forecast horizon. If the asset under diligence will reach market in 2030 in a disease area where three additional therapies are currently in Phase 3 trials, the competitive dynamics in 2030 will look nothing like today's landscape.

Pricing diligence must incorporate the full payer dynamics — US gross-to-net trajectory, EU multi-country HTA assessments, Japan MHLW drug pricing rules, and the emerging impact of the IRA's Medicare drug price negotiation. The era of pricing a drug at whatever the market will bear and adjusting downward through rebates is over for most therapeutic categories.

7.4.3 Financial Due Diligence

Financial diligence for a licensing transaction differs fundamentally from M&A financial diligence. In licensing, the financial model is fundamentally a probability-adjusted forecast of future royalties and milestone income — what the acquirer will receive — layered against the development costs the acquirer will bear. In M&A, the model encompasses the entire P&L, balance sheet, cash flows, and synergy assumptions of the combined entity.

The core tool of licensing financial diligence is the risk-adjusted Net Present Value (rNPV) model. Unlike simple DCF, rNPV applies stage-specific technical and regulatory success probabilities to each future cash flow before discounting. A Phase 1 oncology asset reaching the market has historically required three successful development transitions each with their own probability: Phase 1 to Phase 2 (~60%), Phase 2 to Phase 3 (~35%), and NDA submission to approval (~85%). The cumulative probability of approval from Phase 1 entry is therefore approximately 18%, meaning that 82% of the forecasted revenue must be probability-discounted to zero before discounting for time value.

7.5 Deal Valuation: Science, Art, and the Tyranny of NPV

Deal valuation in pharma is simultaneously the most quantitative and most qualitative activity in the industry. The financial models are complex — Monte Carlo simulations, decision tree analysis, real options theory — but the inputs are profoundly uncertain, and the outputs are only as credible as the assumptions that generated them. The experienced BD professional treats the model not as an answer but as a framework for structuring uncertainty and communicating assumptions.

7.5.1 The rNPV Framework

The risk-adjusted NPV (rNPV) model is the industry-standard valuation tool for development-stage assets. The model projects net cash flows for each year of the asset's commercial life, applies success probabilities at each development transition, and discounts all probability-weighted flows to present value using a risk-adjusted discount rate (typically 10–12% for large pharma, higher for early-stage assets in smaller companies).

The key inputs that drive rNPV are: peak sales forecast, time to peak, commercial lifetime (loss of exclusivity), probability of technical and regulatory success (PTS/PRS), development cost assumptions, cost of goods, selling expenses, and royalty/milestone structure. Small changes in peak sales or PTS have disproportionate effects on rNPV — a 20% increase in peak sales forecast and a 5% improvement in assumed PTS can increase rNPV by 30–40%.

7.5.2 Comparables Analysis

Comparables ("comps") analysis benchmarks the proposed deal against historical transactions involving similar assets at similar stages. The challenge in pharma is that every asset is genuinely different — disease area, target class, modality, competitive context, and IP position all create material differences that make direct comparisons problematic. Nevertheless, comps analysis serves as a valuable sanity check. If every comparable deal for a Phase 2 oncology ADC involved upfronts in the $100–300M range and the target asset is demanding $800M upfront, there needs to be a compelling justification.

A rigorous comps analysis requires access to deal databases (Evaluate Pharma, Citeline, GlobalData), which capture disclosed deal terms including upfronts, total deal value (biobucks), and milestone structures. It's important to note that "total deal value" figures in press releases are almost always highly misleading — they represent the sum of all possible milestones, most of which will never be paid. The real economic value is in the upfront payment plus the probability-weighted present value of development milestones.

7.5.3 Monte Carlo Simulation

Monte Carlo simulation treats the key value drivers as probability distributions rather than point estimates. Instead of assuming peak sales of exactly $1.5B, the model samples from a distribution (log-normal, reflecting that commercial outcomes are right-skewed) centered on $1.5B with standard deviation reflecting genuine uncertainty. Running 10,000 simulation iterations produces a distribution of rNPV outcomes — a mean, confidence intervals, and a clear picture of downside risk. This is particularly valuable for Board presentations where communicating the range of outcomes is more credible and more useful than a single-point estimate.

7.6 Term Sheet Negotiation: Where the Science Meets the Spreadsheet

The term sheet is the document that defines the economic relationship between licensor and licensee. It is not the final legal agreement — that comes later in the definitive License Agreement — but the terms agreed in the term sheet almost invariably survive into the final contract. Concessions made in the term sheet cannot be easily recovered in legal drafting. Therefore, every line of a term sheet deserves the same analytical rigor applied to the scientific diligence.

7.6.1 Economic Terms

Upfront payment: The non-refundable payment made at signing. For the licensor, upfront is the most certain economic value in the deal — milestones may never be reached, royalties depend on commercial success. For the licensee, upfront is the cost of access to the asset before certainty of outcome. The appropriate upfront reflects both the asset's current demonstrated value and the competitive tension among potential partners.

Development milestones: Payments triggered by achievement of defined clinical events — IND filing, Phase 1 initiation, Phase 1 completion, Phase 2 initiation, Phase 2 completion (or data readout), Phase 3 initiation, completion, NDA/BLA submission, and approval in each major market. The negotiation of milestone definitions is as important as the milestone amounts — "Phase 2 completion" can mean very different things, and ambiguous language will produce disputes.

Sales milestones: One-time payments triggered when cumulative or annual net sales cross defined thresholds (e.g., $500M, $1B, $2.5B). Sales milestones represent shared upside for the licensor — they are earned only if the asset becomes a commercial success.

Royalty tiers: Percentage of net sales paid to the licensor, typically structured as escalating tiers based on sales levels. A common structure for a Phase 2 asset might be: 12% on net sales <$500M; 16% on net sales $500M–$1B; 20% on net sales >$1B. The definition of "net sales" — what deductions are permissible — is one of the most contentious negotiation points in pharmaceutical licensing.

7.6.2 Non-Economic Terms

Non-economic terms frequently determine the practical success of an alliance more than the economic structure. Key non-economic negotiation points include: diligence obligations (is the licensee required to use "commercially reasonable efforts" or "best efforts"?), sublicensing rights (can the licensee sublicense without consent?), change of control provisions (what happens to the deal if either party is acquired?), termination rights and consequences, and governance structure including Joint Steering Committee composition, decision-making authority, and dispute resolution mechanisms.

7.7 M&A Strategy: The Build vs. Buy Imperative

The fundamental strategic question in pharmaceutical M&A is not "should we acquire?" but rather "what problem does this acquisition solve, and is acquisition the most efficient solution?" The history of pharma M&A is littered with transactions that were strategically justifiable but operationally catastrophic — where the asset or capability sought was destroyed by the integration process that followed.

7.7.1 Strategic Rationale Categories

Pharmaceutical acquisitions generally fall into one of five strategic categories: (1) pipeline access — acquiring assets that fill specific therapeutic area gaps; (2) platform access — acquiring technology (e.g., CRISPR, CAR-T, mRNA) that enables multiple future assets; (3) commercial scale — acquiring revenue and commercial infrastructure to improve P&L metrics; (4) geographic entry — accessing markets where organic entry is prohibitively slow; and (5) defensive consolidation — preventing a competitor from acquiring the target. The fifth category, while real, is the most dangerous justification for M&A — deals driven primarily by competitive anxiety rather than genuine strategic rationale have a poor track record.

7.7.2 Premium Analysis and Synergy Modeling

Pharmaceutical M&A transactions typically require a premium of 40–80% over the target's undisturbed stock price to secure Board approval and shareholder support. The acquirer must demonstrate that the present value of synergies — revenue synergies from pipeline acceleration and commercial leverage, and cost synergies from overlapping G&A, manufacturing, and R&D — exceeds the premium paid. In practice, cost synergies are more predictable and are delivered faster than revenue synergies. The AbbVie-Allergan deal ($63B, 2020) was rationalized in part on $2B in cost synergies — largely achieved — and revenue diversification that reduced Humira dependence — directionally achieved but challenged by the COVID impact on Allergan's aesthetics business.

Key Insight: The Synergy Credibility Test

Every M&A synergy model should be subjected to the "day 1 achievability test": if this synergy cannot be clearly traced to a specific organizational action (headcount reduction in defined departments, facility closure, elimination of duplicate vendor contracts), it is not a synergy — it is a hope. Revenue synergies that depend on cross-selling into the acquiree's customer base or accelerating the pipeline through superior manufacturing capability are real but should be modeled conservatively with explicit timing assumptions and sensitivity analysis.

7.10 Post-Deal Integration and Alliance Management

The signed agreement is not the end of the BD process — it is the beginning of a relationship that may span decades. The transition from deal execution to deal management is one of the most underinvested capabilities in pharmaceutical BD, and failures of alliance management have destroyed more value than failures of deal negotiation.

7.10.1 Day 1 Planning and Governance Launch

For licensing transactions, Day 1 planning encompasses the establishment of the Joint Steering Committee (JSC), definition of working group structures (Clinical, Commercial, Regulatory, CMC subcommittees), and the transfer of all relevant information and responsibilities from the licensor to the licensee or to the joint governance structure. The JSC charter — which defines decision-making authority, quorum requirements, deadlock resolution mechanisms, and reporting obligations — must be drafted carefully and enforced consistently from the first meeting.

For M&A transactions, Day 1 planning is more comprehensive and more consequential. The acquiring company must have a fully operational integration management office (IMO) on close date with clear ownership of every integration workstream: IT systems, HR and compensation harmonization, manufacturing and quality systems, regulatory affairs, commercial infrastructure, and cultural integration. The integration plan should be substantially complete before regulatory approval of the acquisition — not developed in the weeks after close.

7.10.2 Alliance Management as a Discipline

Large pharma companies with active alliance portfolios — Roche, J&J, Pfizer, Novartis, AstraZeneca — have dedicated Alliance Management functions whose purpose is to maintain productive, functional partnerships with external parties throughout the term of each alliance. Alliance managers are not BD professionals — they are relationship managers with scientific fluency and process discipline who serve as the day-to-day interface between the large pharma's internal teams and the external partner's management.

The most common alliance failure mode is not contractual dispute — it is misaligned priorities. A large pharma company may have 40 active alliances; the biotech partner may have this single alliance as its primary or only relationship. The asymmetry of attention creates resentment, delayed decision-making, and — at its worst — the quiet deterioration of a partnership that should have been productive. The Alliance Manager's role is to compensate for this asymmetry through proactive communication, rapid escalation of issues, and genuine advocacy for the partner within the large pharma's internal systems.

7.11 Portfolio Strategy and Pipeline Gap Analysis

BD cannot be executed effectively without a rigorous, quantitative, and honestly-constructed portfolio strategy. The portfolio strategy defines not just what the company wants to acquire, but what it will walk away from — and at what price. Companies without a well-defined portfolio strategy will pursue deals reactively, chasing whatever presents itself rather than filling genuine strategic gaps.

7.11.1 The Pipeline Gap Analysis Framework

A formal pipeline gap analysis projects the company's revenue trajectory from its current portfolio under base case assumptions (approved products declining with patent expiry; pipeline assets advancing through development with probability weighting), then compares this trajectory to the company's revenue targets. The gap between the two curves defines the size of the BD opportunity that must be filled. The gap analysis also identifies the time horizon of the need — a gap materializing in 2028–2032 requires different BD activity (licensing Phase 2 assets today) than a gap in 2035+ (which might be filled by early-stage research collaborations or platform acquisitions today).

7.11.2 Therapeutic Area Focus Decisions

Not all therapeutic areas are equal from a BD perspective. The TA focus decision involves assessing internal R&D capability, commercial infrastructure, competitive position, and strategic differentiation. A company with deep oncology commercial capability and strong KOL relationships will extract more value from an oncology asset than from a comparable-quality cardiovascular asset — and should price that differential capability into its valuation. Conversely, entering a new TA through BD requires not just the asset but the surrounding ecosystem: medical affairs capability, reimbursement expertise, patient advocacy relationships, and relevant clinical expertise.

7.12 Competitive Intelligence in BD

Competitive intelligence (CI) in the BD context means understanding not just what competitors have in their pipelines, but predicting their BD moves. If three of the five major oncology-focused large pharma companies have identified the same Phase 2 asset as a priority, the resulting competitive tension will drive the upfront to levels that strain any individual buyer's rNPV calculus. The BD team that knew this three months earlier — and either moved first or decided the asset was mispriced for competitive dynamics — has a structural advantage.

CI tools for BD include: systematic monitoring of IND and NDA filings, ClinicalTrials.gov updates, patent filings, SEC disclosures (Form 8-K for material partnerships), conference presentations, analyst day transcripts, and tracking of key executives' career moves (when a Roche BD head joins a biotech, that biotech becomes a plausible Roche acquisition candidate). Some companies also commission systematic expert network interviews with former employees of target companies, which — when conducted ethically and with appropriate legal guidance — can provide material intelligence about pipeline status and BD activity.

7.13 The Biotech Perspective: How to Pitch a Deal to Big Pharma

Having sat across the table from thousands of biotech executives presenting their assets to large pharma BD teams, I can identify the characteristics that separate the presentations that generated deal discussions from those that did not.

7.13.1 What BD Teams Are Really Evaluating

In the first 15 minutes of a partnering meeting, the BD team is not primarily evaluating the data you show them. They are evaluating your scientific credibility, your honesty about limitations, your command of the competitive landscape, and whether they would trust you to execute a $500M+ partnership. Data packages can be reviewed asynchronously — the meeting is for assessing people and strategic fit. CEOs and CSOs who present as advocates for their asset rather than honest brokers of its prospects immediately reduce their credibility with experienced BD professionals.

The most effective pitches structure information in the order that a BD professional needs to make their internal recommendation: (1) the unmet medical need and why existing therapies are inadequate; (2) the biological target and mechanism with evidence for target validation; (3) the compound or therapeutic modality with differentiation from competitors; (4) the clinical data package — complete, including negatives; (5) the regulatory pathway; (6) the commercial opportunity with realistic sizing; and (7) what you are specifically seeking from a partner and why.

Key Insight: The Honest Data Package

The most damaging thing a biotech CEO can do in a BD partnering meeting is to selectively present data — to show the favorable efficacy without mentioning the patient who died on drug, to present the best-responding patient subgroup without clarifying it was a post-hoc analysis. Experienced BD teams always ask: "What is the worst-case interpretation of this data?" If you have not already addressed that question proactively, the meeting will become adversarial. The companies that consistently get good deals present their data like scientists, not salespeople.

7.13.2 Building Competitive Tension — Ethically

BD professionals negotiate more favorable terms when they have genuine competitive interest from multiple parties. Biotech companies seeking partnerships should run a structured process: identify the 8–12 companies most likely to value the asset strategically, brief them sequentially with brief initial discussions before full data room access, create a structured timeline with defined decision points, and be transparent (without naming names) that a competitive process is underway. The goal is to create legitimate competitive tension — not artificial urgency through false signals, which will destroy trust and can generate legal liability.

7.14 Early-Stage vs. Late-Stage BD: Different Worlds

The skills, judgment, and risk tolerance required for early-stage BD differ fundamentally from those required for late-stage transactions. Early-stage deals — preclinical through Phase 1 — require deep scientific conviction about target validation and mechanism of action, tolerance for data that is necessarily incomplete, and the imagination to envision a clinical and commercial path that does not yet exist. Late-stage deals — Phase 3 through marketed — require commercial due diligence sophistication, legal complexity management, and the project management capability to close and integrate deals that may involve thousands of moving parts.

The financial structures also differ. Early-stage deals rely heavily on milestone architecture to manage risk — the upfront is small relative to the total deal value because most of the economic exchange is contingent on future success. Late-stage deals have larger upfronts reflecting reduced development risk, but may involve complex structures including contingent value rights (CVRs), earnouts, and revenue-sharing arrangements that require sophisticated financial and legal management over years.

At Roche, our early-stage BD team was staffed predominantly by PhD scientists with postdoctoral training who could engage peer-to-peer with academic PIs and biotech CSOs. Our late-stage BD and corporate development team included more MBA-trained individuals with M&A execution experience, legal background, and commercial modeling expertise. This deliberate differentiation of team composition reflected the different capabilities required at different stages of the deal funnel.

Barbara Hauser (Roche): "The most durable competitive advantage in BD is not financial capacity — it is scientific credibility. Partners choose you because they trust your teams to develop their assets responsibly, to make the hard decisions rigorously, and to honor the spirit as well as the letter of the agreement. Build that reputation over decades; protect it on every deal."

David Epstein (Novartis/Seagen): "Portfolio strategy without resource discipline is wishful thinking. The companies that outperform in BD are those that can say no to 98% of what they see, at speed, without ambiguity. The discipline of the 'no' is what makes the 'yes' count."

The exceptions — Roche-Genentech, AZ-Daiichi Sankyo — share a common feature: the acquirer had a genuine scientific view of the asset's potential that was not reflected in the market price. BD teams that outperform are those that develop scientific conviction independent of the market."
