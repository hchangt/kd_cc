I want to begin this chapter with an admission that will surprise no one who has been around this industry for more than a decade: we were, for a very long time, remarkably bad at listening to patients. We designed clinical trials around our operational convenience. We built drug delivery systems around manufacturing constraints. We measured outcomes that regulators cared about, not always the ones patients lived with every day. And then — gradually, then suddenly — the world changed underneath us.

The smartphone penetration curve crossed 50% of the US adult population around 2012. Fitness trackers arrived on wrists by the millions. The Apple Watch gained FDA clearance for ECG monitoring in 2018. Continuous glucose monitors shed their prescription-only tether and found their way onto the arms of non-diabetic health enthusiasts. By 2025, a significant fraction of trial-eligible patients were walking into investigator sites carrying in their pockets more physiological monitoring capability than existed in entire hospital wards when I started my career at Eli Lilly in the 1980s.

This chapter maps the resulting transformation — what digital health actually means in a pharmaceutical context, where the genuine scientific value lies, where the hype exceeds the evidence, and how the most sophisticated organizations are integrating these tools into their clinical, regulatory, and commercial strategies. I will be direct: the digital health space contains more noise than signal, more venture capital than validated endpoints, more press releases than peer-reviewed publications. Navigating it requires the same rigor we apply to any other therapeutic modality. No less should be expected.

The Digital Health Landscape

The term "digital health" has become so elastic as to be nearly meaningless without qualification. When I speak to boards, I insist on a taxonomy before anyone utters the phrase. At the broadest level, digital health encompasses any technology-enabled approach to health and healthcare delivery — from a simple SMS medication reminder to a closed-loop insulin delivery system that makes autonomous dosing decisions based on real-time CGM data. These are not the same thing, not by a factor of a thousand, and treating them as equivalent in strategic discussions is a recipe for misallocated capital.

For pharmaceutical companies, the relevant digital health universe breaks into five operational domains, each with distinct regulatory, commercial, and scientific implications.

Wearables and Connected Devices

Consumer wearables — smartwatches, fitness bands, rings, patches — represent the most visible digital health category. The Apple Watch Series 9 and Ultra 2 can measure heart rate, ECG, blood oxygen saturation, skin temperature, and accelerometry. Oura Ring captures sleep architecture, heart rate variability, and respiratory rate. Garmin devices offer advanced running dynamics including ground contact time and vertical oscillation. Continuous glucose monitors from Abbott (Libre) and Dexcom (G7) provide interstitial glucose readings every five minutes, twenty-four hours a day.

What matters to pharmaceutical developers is not the consumer appeal of these devices but their measurement validity. This is where the field remains genuinely contested. A wrist-based optical heart rate sensor may perform beautifully during rest and unreliably during vigorous exercise — precisely the conditions where a cardiac drug's effects matter most. Validation against reference standard measurements in the specific patient population and disease context of interest is not optional. It is the foundational work without which any downstream clinical inference is scientifically compromised.

Mobile Health Applications

Beyond passive sensing, mobile applications serve active roles: capturing patient-reported symptoms, delivering behavioral interventions, supporting medication adherence, connecting patients to care coordinators. The sheer volume — over 350,000 health apps across iOS and Android as of 2024 — obscures the reality that the vast majority have never been evaluated in published clinical research. A 2023 analysis found that fewer than 2% of mental health apps in major app stores had any randomized controlled trial evidence supporting their efficacy claims.

This matters because pharma partnerships with app companies, whether for patient support programs or as clinical trial tools, carry scientific and reputational risk proportional to the gap between what is claimed and what is evidenced. I have sat in too many meetings where a digital health startup's beautifully designed app was evaluated primarily on its user interface, not its evidence base. The interface is the last thing that should concern a pharmaceutical company's scientific leadership.

Digital Therapeutics

Digital therapeutics (DTx) occupy a distinct regulatory category — software-based interventions that prevent, manage, or treat a medical disorder or disease. They are, in the most rigorous segment of the field, essentially drugs delivered through software. Prescription Digital Therapeutics (PDTs) require FDA authorization, have labeled indications, are prescribed by clinicians, and are subject to post-market surveillance. This is the category where pharma's drug development expertise is most directly transferable and where the most rigorous scientific standards apply.

Digital Biomarkers: The Science of Continuous Measurement

The concept of a digital biomarker — a digitally collected and processed measurement that serves as an indicator of normal biology, pathological processes, or response to intervention — represents perhaps the most scientifically consequential development in clinical measurement since the invention of the spirometer. The potential is genuine. The execution challenges are substantial.

Traditional clinical endpoints have always been constrained by measurement episodics: a patient comes to clinic, a reading is taken, and everyone pretends that the resulting number reflects something more than a single noisy snapshot in time. A resting blood pressure taken at a physician's office — in a setting that reliably elevates the reading in 15-20% of patients through the well-documented white coat effect — has driven treatment decisions for millions of people. Continuous ambulatory blood pressure monitoring exists and is demonstrably superior, yet episodic office measurement remains the norm in most of the world. Digital biomarkers offer a systematic solution to this problem of sampling inadequacy.

Categories and Clinical Applications

The range of physiological phenomena now accessible through consumer-grade wearable sensors is extraordinary. Heart rate variability — a sensitive marker of autonomic nervous system function with implications for cardiac risk, stress, and recovery — can be measured continuously from wrist-worn optical sensors with reasonable accuracy under controlled conditions. Gait speed and variability, measurable from smartphone accelerometers or dedicated inertial measurement units, are now recognized as powerful predictors of cognitive decline, falls risk, and frailty in elderly populations. The passive digital phenotyping work emerging from labs like that of John Torous at Beth Israel Deaconess Medical Center has demonstrated that smartphone usage patterns — screen time, typing speed, GPS mobility radius — can detect mood episodes in bipolar disorder weeks before clinical manifestation.

The most rigorous digital biomarker programs I have observed share three characteristics. First, they begin with analytical validation — demonstrating that the sensor measures what it claims to measure, under what conditions, with what level of precision. Second, they establish clinical validation — demonstrating that the measurement correlates with or predicts a clinically meaningful outcome. Third, they pursue utility validation — demonstrating that acting on the measurement improves outcomes compared to not having it. Most digital biomarker programs in industry stall at the first level and never reach the third.

Wearables in Clinical Trials

The integration of consumer wearables into clinical trial protocols has accelerated dramatically since 2018, driven by device capability improvements, regulatory openness signaled by FDA's Digital Health Center of Excellence guidance, and sponsor appetite for richer longitudinal data. The Apple Heart Study — a collaboration between Apple and Stanford Medicine that enrolled over 400,000 participants entirely through an iPhone app — was a landmark demonstration of what digital recruitment and passive monitoring at population scale looks like. It was also a sobering lesson in what it is not: the positive predictive value of the irregular pulse notification for AF was 0.84, excellent for a consumer notification but insufficient as a standalone diagnostic in a regulatory context.

In practice, the most mature wearable trial integrations I have seen at companies like Novartis, AstraZeneca, and Roche follow a tiered architecture. A consumer-grade wearable (Apple Watch, Fitbit, or Garmin, depending on the patient population's demonstrated preferences and existing device ownership) provides high-frequency, high-volume passive data. A research-grade device (ActiGraph for activity, Holter-equivalent patch for cardiac, validated spirometer for pulmonary) provides lower-frequency reference measurements for calibration and anchoring. The consumer device generates the longitudinal signal; the research device validates that the signal is measuring what you think it is measuring.

The Oura Ring has gained particular traction in oncology and immunology trials because of its high patient acceptance, low burden, and validated HRV and temperature measurements that serve as proxies for immune activation and systemic inflammation. Several pharma companies have enrolled post-surgical cancer patients in continuous Oura monitoring programs to detect infectious complications before clinical presentation, with promising early results in reducing hospitalization duration.

Electronic Clinical Outcome Assessments

Electronic Clinical Outcome Assessments (eCOA) represent the digital evolution of what were once paper-based, site-administered, or self-completed questionnaires — the regulatory foundation of how patients communicate their experience of illness and treatment to the clinical trial system. The move from paper to electronic is not a trivial technological upgrade. It changes the epistemics of data collection in ways that matter for regulatory review.

ePRO: The Patient's Voice in Digital Format

Electronic Patient-Reported Outcomes (ePRO) solve a well-documented problem with paper instruments: the discrepancy between when patients experienced symptoms and when they recorded them. Ecological momentary assessment research in the 1990s demonstrated that patients completing weekly paper diaries often filled them in retrospectively — sometimes within hours of clinic visits, reconstructing a week's experience from imperfect memory. This "parking lot effect" introduced systematic bias that inflated or deflated scores depending on current symptom status and the social desirability of reporting improvement.

ePRO systems with time-stamped entry, compliance monitoring, and ecological momentary assessment designs have substantially reduced this problem. FDA has been explicit that validated ePRO instruments are preferred for patient-reported endpoint collection in modern trials. The qualification process for ePRO instruments follows the same COA qualification pathway as their paper predecessors — content validity, construct validity, test-retest reliability — but must additionally demonstrate measurement equivalence between paper and electronic formats when transitioning legacy instruments.

The most sophisticated ePRO deployments now use adaptive assessment algorithms that shorten administration time by terminating early when sufficient precision is achieved (computerized adaptive testing) and ecological momentary designs that capture assessments multiple times per day in patients' natural environments rather than at fixed weekly intervals. These approaches generate richer data with lower patient burden — a combination that sounds too good to be true but is genuinely achievable with thoughtful instrument design.

eClinRO and eObsRO

Electronic Clinician-Reported Outcomes (eClinRO) and electronic Observer-Reported Outcomes (eObsRO) digitize the assessments completed by trained clinicians and patient proxies respectively. In pediatric trials, eObsRO completed by caregivers has become standard for conditions where children cannot self-report reliably. Digital platforms with branching logic, mandatory field completion, and training modules embedded at the point of use have improved data quality and reduced site training burden in ways that translate directly to lower clinical trial costs and faster database lock timelines.

Decentralized Clinical Trials: Reimagining the Research Architecture

Of all the digital health disruptions affecting pharmaceutical R&D, decentralized clinical trials represent the most fundamental structural change to how we recruit, monitor, and retain participants. DCTs — sometimes called virtual trials, hybrid trials, or site-less trials depending on the degree of decentralization — move some or all trial activities from traditional investigator sites to the patient's home, community setting, or local healthcare provider.

I have lived through enough innovation cycles to know that the DCT conversation contains real substance beneath its promotional framing. The most important substance is this: the traditional site-centric trial model systematically excludes the patients most likely to be prescribed a drug if it is approved. A clinical trial that requires monthly visits to an academic medical center in Boston or Chicago enrolls patients who can get to Boston or Chicago monthly — which is to say, patients who are relatively healthy, mobile, financially stable, and typically white. The drug then gets approved for use in patients who look nothing like the trial population. This is not a minor methodological inconvenience. It is a fundamental validity problem that has distorted the evidence base of medicine for decades.

Operational Reality: The Hybrid DCT as the Dominant Model

Despite considerable enthusiasm for fully decentralized, site-less trials, the operational consensus that has emerged across major pharma companies is that hybrid DCTs — retaining one or two site visits for procedures requiring in-person administration or assessment while decentralizing everything else — are the practical optimum for most Phase II and III studies. Fully site-less designs are appropriate for specific indications (mild-to-moderate conditions where telemedicine assessment is validated) but introduce insurmountable challenges in indications requiring physical examination, imaging, biopsies, or complex infusion procedures. The goal is not maximum decentralization; it is optimal patient access and data quality for the specific trial context.

Digital Therapeutics: Software as Medicine

Prescription Digital Therapeutics represent the most intellectually interesting category in digital health for pharmaceutical companies, because they most directly mirror the drug development paradigm while challenging some of its fundamental assumptions. A PDT must demonstrate clinical efficacy through randomized controlled trials with pre-specified primary endpoints, receive FDA authorization through the De Novo pathway (for novel device types without predicate) or 510(k) clearance (if a predicate exists), be prescribed by licensed clinicians, carry prescribing information with labeled indications and contraindications, and be subject to post-market surveillance requirements.

The analogy to a drug is imperfect but useful. Where drugs modulate biology through molecular mechanisms, digital therapeutics modulate psychology and behavior through information delivery, cognitive training, biofeedback, or structured behavioral programs. Cognitive behavioral therapy for insomnia (CBT-I) — the gold standard non-pharmacological treatment for chronic insomnia, with clinical evidence comparable to or superior to sleep medications across most outcomes — is the paradigmatic example of an efficacious behavioral intervention that the healthcare system has historically struggled to deliver at scale due to the scarcity of trained CBT-I therapists. Somryst (formerly SHUTi, by Pear Therapeutics) brought FDA-authorized CBT-I delivery through a smartphone app — an elegant solution to a real access problem.

The FDA De Novo Pathway for DTx

The De Novo pathway, established under the 21st Century Cures Act and clarified through FDA's Software as Medical Device (SaMD) framework and its adaptation of the International Medical Device Regulators Forum (IMDRF) SaMD taxonomy, provides the regulatory route for novel DTx without a predicate device. The pathway requires a Pre-Submission meeting with CDRH, a randomized controlled trial demonstrating clinical benefit, manufacturing controls appropriate for software (SaMD QMS under IEC 62304), and a risk classification analysis under the FDA-adapted IMDRF risk framework.

The commercial story is more complicated. Pear Therapeutics, which brought three FDA-authorized PDTs to market including reSET for substance use disorder and reSET-O for opioid use disorder, filed for bankruptcy in 2023 — a signal of the profound market access challenges facing the PDT category. Authorization by FDA does not translate to coverage by payers, and without payer coverage, commercial viability is severely compromised. The reimbursement question remains the most consequential unsolved problem in the PDT field.

Patient Engagement Platforms and Support Programs

Beyond clinical trials and regulated therapeutics, pharmaceutical companies have invested heavily in patient engagement platforms designed to support medication adherence, connect patients with disease education resources, facilitate communication with care teams, and — frankly — gather real-world data on drug use patterns and patient-reported outcomes in the post-market setting. These programs sit at the intersection of commercial strategy, medical affairs, and market access, and they raise important questions about the appropriate boundaries between patient support and commercial influence.

Medication Adherence: The $530 Billion Problem

Non-adherence to prescribed medications is estimated to cost the US healthcare system over $530 billion annually in avoidable hospitalizations, emergency department visits, disease progression, and productivity loss. For pharmaceutical companies, non-adherence has the more immediate effect of reducing treatment effectiveness and patient outcomes in ways that undermine real-world evidence of drug performance. A drug with 85% efficacy in a randomized trial taken with near-perfect adherence may look far less effective in post-market RWE studies where adherence to complex regimens drops to 50% within twelve months.

The most effective digital adherence interventions combine multiple mechanisms: electronic pill bottles (MEMS caps) that record every opening event; companion apps that convert adherence data into gamified feedback and social comparison; intelligent reminder systems that adapt to individual behavior patterns; and care coordinator access for patients whose adherence signals suggest they are struggling. AstraZeneca's AZ&ME patient assistance program, Novartis's Patients and Programs (P&P) platform, and Gilead's patient support infrastructure for HIV and hepatitis C treatments represent mature implementations that have demonstrated measurable adherence improvements in post-market settings.

PatientsLikeMe, Inspire, and the Social Health Movement

Patient communities — structured online networks where patients with shared diagnoses can exchange experience, compare treatment outcomes, and collectively advocate for research priorities — have become consequential actors in pharmaceutical development. PatientsLikeMe, founded in 2004 and initially focused on ALS, pioneered the structured sharing of patient-reported outcome data among community members in ways that generated research-quality datasets from patient-contributed data. The platform's 2019 acquisition by UnitedHealth Group raised legitimate questions about data use and community trust that the patient advocacy community has not fully resolved.

Inspire operates a broader network of disease-specific communities with over 3 million members across hundreds of conditions. Its research services arm connects pharmaceutical sponsors with patient communities for co-design of research protocols, identification of unmet needs, and validation of patient-centered endpoints. The rare disease community has been particularly innovative in using these platforms — a theme we develop further in Chapter 20 — because the fragmentation of small patient populations across geographic areas makes digital community the only viable modality for meaningful patient-to-patient connection.

Real World Data from Digital Sources

The FDA's 2018 Real World Evidence Framework and subsequent Program Framework for RWE established the conceptual and regulatory scaffolding for using data generated outside conventional clinical trials — from EHRs, claims databases, disease registries, and increasingly from digital and wearable sources — to support regulatory decisions. This was a significant evolution in regulatory thinking, and it has profound implications for how pharmaceutical companies design their post-approval evidence strategies.

EHR Data Partnerships

The value of EHR data for pharmaceutical research is theoretically enormous and practically constrained by a set of well-documented problems. EHR data is not collected for research purposes. It contains systematic missingness patterns — laboratory values, vital signs, and clinical notes that exist only when clinicians ordered tests or made entries, not according to any research protocol. It conflates disease severity, comorbidity burden, and socioeconomic factors in ways that confound treatment effect estimation. And it reflects the particular characteristics of the care systems and patient populations that contributed the data, which are rarely representative of the broader population in ways that matter for regulatory inference.

Despite these limitations, EHR partnerships with health systems like Flatiron Health (acquired by Roche), TriNetX, and Veradigm have become standard components of pharmaceutical companies' RWE strategies. The most valuable applications are not those attempting to replicate randomized trial results, but those addressing questions that randomized trials cannot: comparative effectiveness in populations excluded from trials; safety signal characterization in rare populations; disease natural history characterization; treatment pattern analysis to inform trial design. The FDA's acceptance of RWE from EHR sources in several regulatory contexts — including expanded indications for Ibrance in male breast cancer, supported in part by real-world data — has validated the approach while making clear that the evidentiary standards are high and context-specific.

The regulatory privacy framework governing digital health data is a patchwork that reflects the historical accident of when different data types and collection modalities came into existence, rather than any coherent theory of how health data should be protected. This creates compliance complexity and genuine patient protection gaps that pharmaceutical companies operating in digital health must understand and actively manage.

The Consumer Wearable Privacy Gap

One of the most consequential and underappreciated gaps in the US digital health privacy framework is that consumer health apps and wearables are generally NOT covered by HIPAA. A person's Fitbit data, Apple Health records, Oura Ring sleep scores, and the contents of most wellness apps fall outside HIPAA's jurisdiction because the companies producing these devices are not covered entities or business associates under the statute. This means the default legal protection for a substantial fraction of the health data that patients generate and share is the FTC Act's prohibition on deceptive practices — a far weaker protection than patients typically assume. When pharmaceutical companies partner with consumer device companies for research purposes, the privacy legal framework governing the resulting data requires careful legal analysis and explicit contractual protections that supplement the limited statutory floor.

FDA's Digital Health Framework: SaMD and the Digital Health Center of Excellence

The FDA's regulatory approach to digital health has evolved considerably over the past decade, from an early period of regulatory ambiguity that permitted the proliferation of unsubstantiated health app claims, through the 2017 Medical Device Amendments that clarified which software functions constitute devices subject to FDA oversight, to the establishment of the Digital Health Center of Excellence (DHCoE) within CDRH in 2020.

The Software as Medical Device (SaMD) framework, adopted from the IMDRF international working group, classifies software functions based on the significance of the information provided and the state of the healthcare situation or condition. The resulting four-class risk matrix determines the level of clinical evidence and regulatory oversight required. Class I (low risk, non-serious condition) may qualify for enforcement discretion. Class IV (high risk, critical/serious condition, driving clinical management) requires the most rigorous evidence and regulatory review. This framework has brought welcome clarity to a previously chaotic landscape, though edge cases proliferate and companies continue to seek informal feedback from CDRH through Pre-Submission meetings before committing to development programs.

The Pre-Certification Pilot Program (Pre-Cert) — FDA's ambitious attempt to regulate digital health companies rather than individual products, based on the quality of their development culture and real-world performance monitoring systems — ultimately stalled without formal implementation, leaving the product-by-product review paradigm intact. The lessons from Pre-Cert's difficulties are worth understanding: the existing regulatory system, designed for physical devices with fixed characteristics, strains to accommodate software products that are updated continuously and whose safety profiles may change with every software release.

Payer Coverage and Reimbursement for Digital Health

The payer landscape for digital health is in a state of productive flux, with several coherent coverage models emerging even as the majority of digital health innovations remain outside the reimbursement system. Understanding the reimbursement pathways — and their limitations — is essential for any pharmaceutical company evaluating a digital health strategy.

Current Reimbursement Pathways

Remote Patient Monitoring (RPM) CPT codes (99453, 99454, 99457, 99458) established in the 2019 Medicare Physician Fee Schedule have created a reimbursable pathway for connected devices that transmit physiological data to clinicians. These codes cover device setup, equipment supply, and physician time spent reviewing and acting on remotely monitored data. The RPM pathway has been used successfully for blood pressure monitors, CGM, weight scales, and pulse oximeters in chronic disease management programs. For pharmaceutical companies, RPM creates an ecosystem in which connected medication delivery devices or companion monitors can potentially be reimbursed as part of an integrated treatment package.

Prescription Digital Therapeutics face a more challenging reimbursement environment. Without a clear CPT code pathway equivalent to RPM, PDTs have pursued payer-specific coverage arrangements, employer health plan contracts, and in some cases, managed Medicaid agreements. Several states have moved faster than CMS: New York and Michigan have established Medicaid coverage for specific digital health interventions. Germany's Digital Health Applications (DiGA) framework, which established a fast-track pathway for digital health reimbursement in the statutory health insurance system (GKV), is the world's most advanced national PDT reimbursement system and is being watched closely by other health technology assessment bodies as a model for systematic evaluation and coverage decision-making.

Pharma's Digital Health Strategy: Build, Buy, or Partner

Every major pharmaceutical company has now articulated some form of digital health strategy, and most have established dedicated digital medicine teams, innovation labs, or venture investment vehicles to pursue it. The strategic question that most commonly occupies senior leadership is the classic build-buy-partner calculus, applied to a domain where the technology is evolving rapidly, the regulatory requirements are becoming clearer but remain complex, and the commercial models are still being established.

My observation across the companies where I have worked or served as an advisor is that the most successful digital health strategies share four characteristics. First, they are rooted in a specific unmet clinical need — a clear problem that digital technology is positioned to solve better than existing approaches — rather than a general enthusiasm for digital innovation. Second, they apply the same evidence standards to digital interventions as to pharmacological ones: if you would not approve a drug without randomized controlled trial evidence, you should not deploy a digital therapeutic without it. Third, they invest seriously in the regulatory and market access strategy from the beginning, not as an afterthought after the technology is built. Fourth, they build or acquire the data science and software engineering capabilities needed to execute, rather than attempting to outsource core technical functions entirely to partners whose incentives diverge from the sponsor's.

Case Study: AstraZeneca's Digital Patient Engagement

AstraZeneca has pursued one of the pharmaceutical industry's most articulated digital health strategies, with investments spanning respiratory disease management (Propeller Health partnership for connected inhaler adherence tracking in asthma and COPD), oncology patient support (MyTherapy companion app integrations), cardiovascular digital endpoints, and a substantial investment in AI-driven drug discovery through a partnership with BenevolentAI. The respiratory inhaler work is particularly instructive: by partnering with Propeller Health (later acquired by ResMed), AstraZeneca was able to offer pulmonologists and respiratory nurses real-time adherence data for patients on Symbicort and other products — data that in clinical studies demonstrated a meaningful improvement in medication adherence and, in real-world observational studies, an association with reduced hospitalizations.

Livongo and Omada: Digital Health as Therapeutic Alternative

Livongo Health (acquired by Teladoc in 2020) and Omada Health represent a model where digital health platforms are positioned not as companions to pharmaceutical therapy but as alternatives or adjuncts to it, particularly in metabolic disease. Livongo's diabetes management platform — combining cellular-connected glucometers, AI-driven coaching, and real-time data transmission to care teams — demonstrated in published studies a statistically significant reduction in HbA1c, comparable in magnitude to some pharmaceutical interventions. Omada's behavior change program for diabetes prevention, based on the CDC-recognized National Diabetes Prevention Program protocol, has demonstrated sustained weight loss and reduced diabetes incidence risk in published peer-reviewed trials.

For pharmaceutical companies, these platforms represent both a threat and an opportunity. As a threat: a robust digital behavior change program may reduce the pool of patients who need pharmacological intervention or reduce the intensity of pharmacological treatment needed. As an opportunity: a pharmaceutical company that can integrate a validated digital behavior change program with its drug therapy may offer a combination that produces better outcomes than either alone — a value proposition that commands premium pricing and creates barriers to competitive substitution.

Health Equity and the Digital Divide

I have saved what I consider the most important challenge in digital health for near the end of this chapter, because it is the consideration most frequently mentioned in conference presentations and most consistently underinvested in practice. The digital divide — the gap between populations with robust access to digital technology, broadband internet, and the literacy to use health technology effectively, and those without — creates a real risk that digital health will amplify existing health inequities rather than reduce them.

The evidence on differential digital health access is unambiguous. Broadband internet access in rural America remains substantially lower than in urban and suburban areas, with the FCC's 2023 Broadband Data Collection showing that millions of households in rural counties lack access to 25/3 Mbps service. Smartphone ownership rates, while approaching universality among younger adults, decline substantially among Americans over 65 — precisely the population with the highest burden of the chronic diseases that digital health most commonly targets. The intersection of age, income, and geography creates pockets of digital exclusion that perfectly overlap with health disparity populations.

In clinical trials, this creates a diversity problem with direct regulatory consequences. FDA's 2023 action plan to increase diversity in clinical trials explicitly called out digital recruitment and DCT modalities as potential tools for improving access — but only if they are designed with accessibility in mind. A recruitment strategy relying exclusively on social media advertising will not find patients in communities with limited digital access or media literacy. A fully app-based ePRO system will exclude patients who cannot reliably access a smartphone or have difficulty with touchscreen interfaces. A direct-to-patient drug shipment protocol that requires patients to arrange home delivery and manage temperature-sensitive storage will burden patients without stable housing or refrigeration — the patients most likely to be undertreated and most likely to benefit from new therapies.

Designing for Accessibility: Practical Principles

Digital health designs that genuinely serve diverse populations share common principles: offering multiple access modalities (app AND telephone AND paper backup); proactively assessing and addressing device and connectivity gaps for trial participants; partnering with community health organizations in underserved areas for hybrid digital/in-person support; developing ePRO interfaces with large text, simple navigation, and audio support for low-literacy users; and measuring digital access disparities as a protocol-specified metric rather than discovering them post-hoc in subgroup analyses. These are not technically challenging requirements. They are choices about whose outcomes we consider worth the operational investment to obtain.

The Future: Continuous Monitoring, Closed-Loop Systems, and Drug-Device Convergence

The trajectory of digital health technology points toward a future that was science fiction when I was a medical student and is now, in its early forms, clinical reality. The artificial pancreas — a closed-loop system combining a continuous glucose monitor, an insulin pump, and a control algorithm that makes autonomous dosing decisions without patient input — is approved and available. Medtronic's MiniMed 780G, Tandem's Control-IQ, and Insulet's Omnipod 5 are all closed-loop systems in commercial use, with published clinical data demonstrating superior time-in-range compared to sensor-augmented pump therapy. For patients with type 1 diabetes, this represents a transformation in quality of life that I find genuinely moving: the cognitive burden of continuous carbohydrate counting and manual dose adjustment, sustained over decades, is substantially reduced.

The artificial pancreas is the template for a broader category of closed-loop therapeutic systems that will emerge over the next decade. Closed-loop deep brain stimulation systems that adjust stimulation parameters in real time based on neural signal biomarkers are in development for Parkinson's disease and treatment-resistant depression. Closed-loop cardiac resynchronization therapy that adapts pacing algorithms based on continuous hemodynamic monitoring is in clinical evaluation. Closed-loop chemotherapy dosing systems that use pharmacokinetic models and real-time biomarker measurements to individualize dose and schedule are in early research.

Continuous drug monitoring — measuring drug concentrations in biological fluids continuously rather than at discrete sampling timepoints — represents another frontier with profound therapeutic optimization potential. Sweat-based biosensors capable of detecting drug concentrations in interstitial fluid are in development for antibiotics, immunosuppressants, and certain chemotherapy agents. If validated, these technologies would enable therapeutic drug monitoring that is currently done weekly or monthly (at great patient burden and cost) to be done continuously and passively — enabling precision dosing adjustments that would improve efficacy and reduce toxicity across the population of patients whose pharmacokinetics deviate substantially from the population average.

I am not a digital utopian. I have seen too many technologies that were going to transform medicine fail to deliver on their promise because they were technically impressive but clinically unvalidated, commercially sound but inequitably distributed, or scientifically rigorous but impossible to integrate into the fragmented reality of healthcare delivery. Digital health will not be different from every preceding technology wave in this respect. But I do believe the underlying scientific opportunity is real, the regulatory framework is maturing in ways that will distinguish validated interventions from noise, and the patient advocacy and engagement movements that have emerged from the digital health era represent a genuine and permanent shift in the relationship between patients and the institutions that develop medicines for them. That relationship — built on trust, transparency, and demonstrated commitment to patient-defined outcomes — is the most important digital health development of the past twenty years. The technology is a tool. The relationship is the point.

Summary: Key Takeaways for Pharma Leadership

  • Digital biomarkers require three-stage validation — analytical, clinical, and utility — before use in regulatory-grade trial endpoints. Most industry programs stall at stage one.
  • DCTs are the dominant model for modern trial design, but hybrid approaches outperform fully site-less designs in most therapeutic contexts. The goal is optimal patient access and data quality, not maximum decentralization.
  • Prescription Digital Therapeutics face a reimbursement crisis that requires active engagement with payers from the earliest stages of program development. FDA authorization without payer coverage is commercially non-viable, as Pear Therapeutics demonstrated.
  • Consumer health app data is not HIPAA-protected in the US — a gap that creates patient privacy risks and compliance obligations when pharma incorporates this data into research or commercial programs.
  • Digital health amplifies health disparities unless explicitly designed to address digital access inequities. Diversity in digital recruitment requires deliberate, protocol-specified investment, not assumptions about universal smartphone access.
  • The build-buy-partner decision in digital health must be grounded in strategic differentiation analysis: build where digital capability is core to the therapeutic proposition; partner where technology is enabling but not differentiating; acquire where speed to market justifies the premium.
  • Closed-loop therapeutic systems represent the next frontier in drug-device convergence. Pharmaceutical companies that do not develop fluency in algorithmic control systems, sensor integration, and real-time biomarker interpretation will be unprepared for this convergence.
  • Patient centricity is not a marketing slogan. Genuine engagement — through patient advisory boards, PRO instrument development, participatory trial design, and transparent outcome sharing — produces better science and stronger regulatory submissions.
