I have spent four decades in this industry. I have watched the PCR machine go from a curiosity to the backbone of diagnostics. I have watched monoclonal antibodies go from laboratory impossibility to the largest drug class on earth. I have watched biologics go from feared regulatory novelty to routine. Each time, the people inside the industry underestimated the change, and the people outside overestimated the timeline. Both errors are expensive. My attempt in this final chapter is to commit neither.

This is the most important moment in pharmaceutical history since the discovery of penicillin. I say that not for dramatic effect — I am constitutionally allergic to hyperbole — but because I believe it is demonstrably true. The convergence happening right now, in 2025 and 2026, between artificial intelligence, genomics, protein structure prediction, RNA delivery, gene editing, and an unprecedented volume of real-world biological data, represents a phase transition in what is biologically possible. The question is not whether pharma will be transformed. It is whether the transformation will be led from inside the industry or imposed from outside it.

I founded my current company at an age when many of my peers are writing memoirs. I did it because standing on the sidelines of this moment felt like a moral failure. And because, after forty years of watching institutional inertia slow things down by a decade here and a patent cliff there, I wanted to build something that moves at the speed of the science. This chapter is my attempt to articulate what that future looks like — and what must change to get there.

Where We Are Now: An Inflection Point

The pharmaceutical industry in 2025 sits at an awkward juncture. On one hand, the scientific capabilities available to researchers today are extraordinary: AlphaFold3 has essentially solved protein structure prediction for small molecules and protein-protein interactions; CRISPR base editing can rewrite single nucleotides in vivo with therapeutic precision; GLP-1 agonists are reshaping the entire metabolic disease landscape; and AI models are generating IND-ready candidate molecules in weeks, not years. On the other hand, the business model that has sustained pharmaceutical innovation for sixty years — the blockbuster, patent-protected, fee-for-service pricing model — is under unprecedented political, payer, and competitive pressure.

The IRA (Inflation Reduction Act) drug pricing provisions, however imperfect their implementation, represent the first serious structural challenge to price-setting power in the United States since Medicaid was established. The biosimilar wave about to hit is real — some estimates put $100 billion or more of branded biologic revenue at risk by 2030. China has gone from being a contract manufacturing destination to a full-spectrum pharmaceutical power. And the talent base that pharma historically relied upon — medicinal chemists, clinical pharmacologists, regulatory scientists — is now competing for AI engineers and data scientists who have many other options.

I have been inside enough boardrooms to know that the instinctive response to all of this is defensive. Cut R&D. License in late-stage assets. Buy back stock. Grow through M&A. These strategies have worked, for a while. They will not work for the next decade. What will work is embracing the inflection point with genuine urgency.

"The companies that will dominate pharma in 2035 are being founded right now, or are already in existence and do not yet look like pharma companies. The incumbents that survive will be the ones that cannibalize themselves before someone else does." — Author's note, from a keynote delivered at JPM Healthcare Conference, January 2025

Megatrend 1 AI-Driven Drug Discovery: The Compressed Timeline

When DeepMind's AlphaFold published the 3D structures of essentially every known protein in 2021, I remember calling several colleagues who had spent careers on structural biology. The response was not panic; it was a kind of stunned recalibration. Those structures took lifetimes to determine, and now they were free, downloadable, and essentially complete. That was the opening shot of a transformation still accelerating.

AI in drug discovery today is no longer a research project — it is a competitive necessity. Isomorphic Labs, spun out of DeepMind, is generating drug candidates with a speed that classical medicinal chemistry cannot match. Insilico Medicine received the first AI-designed IND candidate approval in China and is in Phase II. Recursion's operating system approach — running millions of cellular perturbation experiments and training foundation models on the resulting phenotypic data — is a genuine platform, not a proof of concept.

What I find most significant is not the speed of candidate generation but the potential for autonomous lab platforms: systems where AI models design experiments, robotic labs execute them overnight, and ML systems analyze results to propose the next iteration. Emerald Cloud Lab, Strateos, and the Ginkgo Bioworks model suggest what is coming. By 2030, I expect the majority of hit-to-lead optimization in large pharma to be AI-autonomous. Human scientists will define the problem and interpret clinical results; machines will handle the molecular iteration in between.

The Translation Problem Remains Unsolved

The honest caveat: AI excels at navigating chemical space and predicting binding affinities. It is substantially less reliable at predicting efficacy in complex disease biology, off-target toxicity in humans, and pharmacokinetic behavior across patient populations. The attrition rate from AI-designed candidates will improve, but it will not collapse to near zero. The bottleneck is shifting from discovery to the biology of disease itself — which AI cannot yet model with the fidelity needed to predict Phase II outcomes. Anyone who tells you AI will "solve" drug attrition by 2030 is selling something.

Megatrend 2 Precision Medicine at Scale: Polygenic Scores, Multi-Omics, N-of-1

The promise of precision medicine has been dangled before us for twenty years. The Human Genome Project was supposed to usher it in. It did not, because translating a reference genome into clinical decision-making required tools that did not yet exist: affordable whole-genome sequencing, the computational capacity to analyze multi-omics data at scale, and enough patients with linked genetic and phenotypic data to build useful prediction models.

Those conditions now exist. The UK Biobank, the All of Us Research Program, and dozens of commercial biobanks have created the training data that polygenic risk score (PRS) models require. Companies like Genomics plc, Ambry Genetics, and Color Genomics are translating PRS into clinical workflows. I expect that by 2028, polygenic risk scores for cardiovascular disease, type 2 diabetes, and certain cancers will be standard components of primary care visits in health systems that want to remain competitive.

The more radical version of precision medicine — N-of-1 trials, where a single patient is their own control, treated with a therapy designed specifically for their molecular profile — is already happening in rare genetic disease. The collaborations between Broad Institute, Children's Hospital Boston, and companies like Encoded Therapeutics are generating patient-specific ASO therapeutics in months. As the cost of custom therapy manufacturing falls and regulatory frameworks catch up, N-of-1 medicine will move from rare disease into oncology and then, eventually, into common disease.

Megatrend 3 Gene and Cell Therapy Maturation: CRISPR In Vivo, Off-the-Shelf CAR-T

2023 was the year gene therapy moved from hope to reality. The FDA approval of Casgevy (exagamglogene autotemcel, Vertex/CRISPR Therapeutics) for sickle cell disease and beta-thalassemia was a landmark — the first CRISPR-based therapy approved anywhere in the world. For those of us who watched the Jesse Gelsinger tragedy set back gene therapy by a decade in 1999, the approval of Casgevy felt like redemption at scale.

But the real prize — and the harder problem — is in vivo gene editing: delivering CRISPR machinery directly into the body and editing target cells without removal and re-infusion. Intellia Therapeutics demonstrated in vivo CRISPR editing in liver cells for transthyretin amyloidosis with durable results. David Liu's base editors and prime editors are now in clinical development at Beam Therapeutics and Prime Medicine. These technologies can achieve the precision of traditional CRISPR without the double-strand DNA breaks that create off-target risk.

CAR-T cell therapy, meanwhile, is constrained by manufacturing: each patient's therapy is made from their own T-cells, a process that takes weeks, costs upward of $400,000, and is technically demanding. The allogeneic or "off-the-shelf" CAR-T approach — using donor T-cells engineered to avoid rejection — has had setbacks (Allogene's clinical holds, early efficacy questions) but the engineering solutions are advancing. By 2030, I expect at least two or three approved allogeneic CAR-T products, which will begin to normalize the manufacturing economics of cell therapy.

Megatrend 4 Longevity Science: Senolytics, NAD+ Biology, Anti-Aging as Medicine

I approach longevity science with a mixture of genuine scientific excitement and practiced skepticism accumulated over decades of watching promising biology fail in humans. The scientific rationale for senolytics — drugs that clear senescent cells, which accumulate with aging and drive chronic inflammation — is solid. Dasatinib plus quercetin, navitoclax, and newer, more selective ABT-199 derivatives have shown compelling data in rodent models of aging, fibrosis, and frailty. Unity Biotechnology's clinical programs have had mixed results, but the target biology is not dead.

The deeper challenge for longevity science is regulatory. The FDA does not currently recognize "aging" as a disease indication. The TAME trial (Targeting Aging with Metformin), led by the American Federation for Aging Research, is attempting to establish a regulatory precedent: demonstrating that metformin delays a composite of aging-related disease endpoints, which would create an approval pathway for aging drugs. If TAME succeeds, the regulatory floodgates open.

George Church's Altos Labs, Calico (funded by Alphabet), and the growing cohort of longevity-focused biotechs backed by billionaire investors represent a serious institutional commitment to this field. I am not yet confident that any current longevity drug candidate will achieve broad FDA approval by 2030. But I am confident that by 2035, "healthspan extension" will be a recognized medical category with approved drugs. The science is real. The timeline is uncertain. That combination is exactly where the boldest bets should be made.

Megatrend 5 GLP-1 and the Metabolic Medicine Revolution

Nothing in my career has moved as fast as the GLP-1 story. Semaglutide — Ozempic, Wegovy — went from a diabetes drug to the most talked-about pharmaceutical in history in roughly eighteen months. The SELECT cardiovascular outcomes trial, showing 20% reduction in major cardiovascular events in people with obesity but without diabetes, transformed the market framing from "weight loss drug" to "metabolic disease platform." The FLOW trial showing kidney protection. The SURMOUNT trials. The emerging data on Alzheimer's risk reduction. This drug appears to be doing things that no one fully predicted when the GLP-1 receptor agonist class was first developed.

The market projection numbers are staggering. Goldman Sachs projected the GLP-1 market could reach $100 billion by 2030. Others go higher. If oral GLP-1 formulations — Novo's semaglutide pill, Eli Lilly's orforglipron — achieve comparable efficacy to injectable forms with better tolerability, the addressable population expands by an order of magnitude. Consider: roughly 42% of US adults meet criteria for obesity. If oral GLP-1 becomes as easy to take as a statin, the 50+ million user scenario is not fantasy.

The Access Problem Cannot Be Ignored

At $1,000 per month or more, GLP-1 drugs are inaccessible to the majority of people who need them. Compounding pharmacies have created a grey market. The political pressure on Novo Nordisk and Eli Lilly pricing is not going away. If the industry handles this as a pricing standoff rather than a genuine access challenge, Congress will intervene, and the intervention will be blunt. The industry's long-term interest requires making these drugs dramatically cheaper at scale — which manufacturing investment and patent expiration will eventually enable, but probably not fast enough to avoid a political crisis first.

Megatrend 6 Multispecific Antibodies: Bispecifics, Trispecifics, ADCs

The antibody platform that produced blockbusters like Humira, Herceptin, and Keytruda is far from exhausted — it is being re-engineered at a molecular level. Bispecific antibodies, which can simultaneously bind two different targets, have moved from scientific curiosity to commercial reality. Blinatumomab (Blincyto, Amgen) — a CD3xCD19 bispecific T-cell engager — was the first approved bispecific and has transformed certain leukemia treatment. Teclistamab, elranatamab, and talquetamab are redefining multiple myeloma treatment. By 2030 I expect bispecifics to be the largest sub-segment of the antibody market.

Antibody-drug conjugates (ADCs) represent the most exciting near-term opportunity in oncology. The concept — attaching a potent cytotoxic payload to an antibody that delivers it specifically to tumor cells — is old, but the execution had been poor for decades. The approval of trastuzumab deruxtecan (Enhertu, AstraZeneca/Daiichi Sankyo) demonstrated that with the right linker, payload, and antibody, ADCs can achieve response rates that chemotherapy cannot touch. The HER2-low breast cancer indication was a particularly revelatory finding: a target that was previously considered clinically irrelevant became therapeutically significant. The ADC pipeline now numbers in the hundreds.

Trispecific antibodies — binding three targets simultaneously — are in early development at companies like Harpoon Therapeutics, Merus, and several large pharma R&D labs. The engineering complexity is substantial, but the potential to recruit T-cells while simultaneously blocking two tumor escape mechanisms is compelling. This is where antibody engineering is headed.

Megatrend 7 RNA Medicine: mRNA, siRNA, LNP Delivery, Prime Editing

Katalin Karikó spent decades in academic obscurity, unable to attract major grant funding, insisting that modified mRNA could be used as a medicine without triggering catastrophic immune responses. The COVID-19 pandemic made her work famous; the Nobel Prize in 2023 made it historic. mRNA's clinical proof-of-concept, delivered at unprecedented scale through 5 billion COVID-19 vaccine doses, has unlocked a wave of investment and clinical programs that will define the next decade of drug development.

The siRNA (small interfering RNA) class, led by Alnylam Pharmaceuticals, has quietly achieved regulatory success in rare liver diseases — patisiran, inclisiran, vutrisiran — through lipid nanoparticle (LNP) delivery to hepatocytes. These approvals have validated the RNA delivery platform and demonstrated that knockdown of disease-causing genes can achieve durable efficacy. Inclisiran, with its twice-yearly dosing for LDL reduction, is a genuine innovation in adherence for cardiovascular medicine.

The next frontier is delivery beyond the liver. The liver is easy — it takes up LNPs naturally. The hard targets are the lung, brain, muscle, and tumor microenvironment. Multiple companies (Arctus Bio, Precision BioSciences, Orna Therapeutics) are engineering next-generation delivery vehicles: organ-selective LNPs, circular RNA, engineered extracellular vesicles. In vivo prime editing — David Liu's most sophisticated editing technology — is now entering clinical trials at Prime Medicine and Beam Therapeutics. By 2030, I expect the first in vivo prime editing approval.

Megatrend 8 Microbiome Therapeutics: Gut-Brain Axis, Live Biotherapeutics

The microbiome field spent much of the 2010s in a hype cycle that damaged its credibility. The first wave of clinical failures — fecal microbiota transplant (FMT)-like products for Crohn's disease, early failures in microbiome modulation for obesity — gave legitimate scientists reason for caution. Then Seres Therapeutics received FDA approval for Vowst (fecal microbiota spores) for prevention of recurrent C. difficile infection in 2023, and the field quietly reset on more solid footing.

The gut-brain axis is the most scientifically intriguing area. Multiple lines of evidence now suggest that the microbiome influences neurological function, mood, and neurodegenerative disease risk through vagal nerve signaling, short-chain fatty acid production, and systemic immune modulation. This is early biology — I would not bet significant capital on a microbiome-CNS drug in Phase I today. But by 2030, if the mechanistic work in the lab translates even partially into clinical data, we may be looking at a new category of neurological therapeutics that no one saw coming.

The Changing Regulatory Environment: FDA in the AI Age

The FDA is, by institutional temperament, a cautious organization. It is designed to be. Its core mandate — ensure that medicines are safe and effective before reaching patients — demands caution in a way that commerce does not. But the agency is also, to its credit, capable of adaptation. Accelerated Approval, Breakthrough Therapy Designation, the Real-Time Oncology Review program — these are not the behaviors of a rigid bureaucracy. They are evidence of an institution that has learned to calibrate risk against unmet need.

The AI era is creating challenges for regulatory science that existing frameworks were not designed to handle. When an AI generates a drug candidate and an autonomous lab validates it, where is the audit trail? When a digital biomarker from a wearable device is used as a primary endpoint, how is it qualified? When an adaptive clinical trial uses an AI model to re-assign patients to arms in real time, what constitutes the pre-specified analysis plan?

The FDA's Center for Drug Evaluation and Research (CDER) has begun publishing guidance on AI/ML in drug development, and the Digital Health Center of Excellence is working on biomarker qualification frameworks. But the honest assessment is that regulatory science is 3-5 years behind the technology. This gap creates both risk (therapies delayed because the regulatory pathway is unclear) and opportunity (companies that proactively engage the FDA in defining new frameworks gain first-mover regulatory advantage).

Digital Biomarker Qualification: The Next Frontier

FDA's Digital Health Center of Excellence is working to qualify wearable-derived endpoints as primary clinical trial measures. Accelerometer-based gait metrics in Parkinson's. Continuous glucose monitor readings as surrogate endpoints in metabolic disease. EEG-derived sleep architecture in neurology. When these biomarkers are qualified, they will compress trial timelines by enabling remote, continuous monitoring and catching efficacy signals earlier. Pharma companies that have invested in the underlying data science infrastructure will have a decisive advantage.

The Pricing and Access Crisis: IRA, Sustainability, and Political Reality

The Inflation Reduction Act of 2022 authorized CMS to negotiate prices for a defined set of high-cost drugs — initially 10 drugs, expanding over time. The first negotiated prices, announced in 2024, ranged from 38% to 79% below existing list prices. For drugs like Eliquis and Jardiance, the impact on manufacturer revenue is real, and the message to the industry is unambiguous: the era of unchallenged price-setting in the US is over.

I have watched the pricing debate play out for thirty years, and what strikes me about the current moment is that the political consensus has shifted in a way that feels structural rather than cyclical. The public's tolerance for paying $150,000 per year for a drug that costs $1,500 to manufacture has effectively expired. The question is not whether pricing power will be constrained; it is whether the constraints will be managed intelligently (in ways that preserve innovation incentives) or bluntly (in ways that destroy them).

The industry's own behavior has not helped. List prices that bear no relationship to manufacturing cost. Rebate-driven formulary placement that distorts economics. Patient assistance programs designed to generate positive headlines while doing little for uninsured patients at the pharmacy counter. These practices have created justified cynicism that makes reasonable policy very hard to design.

My view: the innovation model is sustainable, but only if the industry accepts a grand bargain. Lower list prices (or genuine value-based pricing) in exchange for volume guarantees and faster regulatory timelines. The alternative — continued price escalation followed by congressional intervention — ends badly for everyone, patients included.

The Biosimilar Tsunami: $100 Billion at Risk by 2030

The patent cliff that defined pharma economics in the 2010s was about small molecules — Lipitor, Plavix, Seroquel losing exclusivity and facing generic competition within months. The biosimilar wave approaching in the late 2020s is about the most commercially successful biologics in history: Keytruda, Dupixent, Stelara, Eylea, Humira's successor products, and several others. The economics are different from small molecule generics (biosimilars never achieve 90%+ market share the way generics do), but the revenue displacement will be substantial.

The AbbVie Humira transition is the case study every pharma strategy team is studying. Humira was a $20 billion drug. Biosimilars launched. And AbbVie executed a remarkably disciplined transition to Skyrizi and Rinvoq, which collectively now generate more revenue than Humira at its peak. The lesson: biosimilar exposure is survivable if the pipeline is managed proactively. It is fatal if you are caught without successors.

China as a Pharmaceutical Power: From Contract Manufacturing to Originator Innovation

Twenty years ago, China was primarily a source of low-cost active pharmaceutical ingredients (APIs) and clinical research outsourcing. Today, Chinese biotechs are generating novel first-in-class molecules and licensing them to global pharma at prices that would have seemed implausible five years ago. The BeiGene, Zymeworks, LegoChem, and Zenas BioPharma deals of 2023-2025 are not anomalies; they are the beginning of a structural shift.

Several forces are driving Chinese pharma's ascent. The National Medical Products Administration (NMPA) has implemented regulatory reforms that align increasingly with ICH guidelines, making Chinese clinical data more internationally acceptable. China's biotech talent pool has been turbocharged by returnees from US graduate programs and pharma companies. And the domestic NRDL (National Reimbursement Drug List) negotiation process — brutal on pricing but large in volume — has forced Chinese biotechs to compete on clinical differentiation rather than pricing power alone.

The geopolitical dimension is real and cannot be ignored. The BIOSECURE Act discussions in the US Congress reflect legitimate concerns about API supply chain concentration in China and data security at Chinese CROs and CDMOs like WuXi AppTec and BGI. The tension between scientific collaboration (which advances medicine) and supply chain security (which advances national resilience) is not resolvable in the near term. Pharma companies will need to maintain bifurcated supply chains: China-engaged for cost and scale, non-China alternatives for regulatory and political resilience.

The Talent War: AI Engineers in Pharma

The single most significant organizational challenge facing pharmaceutical companies today is not regulatory, competitive, or scientific — it is human. The talent war for AI engineers, machine learning scientists, and data architects is being fought simultaneously by pharma, tech companies, financial services, autonomous vehicles, defense, and a hundred other industries. Pharma does not win compensation battles against Google or Citadel. What it can offer is mission: the chance to apply world-class technical skills to problems that matter in a way that few other industries can match.

The companies winning the talent war are those that have figured out how to structure the work in ways that attract top technical talent. Recursion built a genuine tech culture inside a drug discovery company. Isomorphic Labs hired DeepMind scientists by giving them scientific problems of genuine complexity. Genentech's computational biology group has become a destination for ML researchers who want biological impact. The formula is: real data at massive scale, hard problems, publication culture, and competitive compensation that may not match the frontier tech companies but is close enough that mission fills the gap.

The career pipeline is also shifting. Pharma companies have historically recruited from pharmacy schools, medical schools, and chemistry departments. In 2025, the most consequential hires are coming from computer science, biostatistics, bioinformatics, and systems biology programs. Medical schools and chemistry departments produce people who understand the target. CS and data science programs produce people who can find patterns in a dataset of 10 million molecules. The industry needs both, and it needs them to speak each other's language.

New Business Models: Platform Companies, Asset-Centric Models, AI-First Biotechs

The traditional vertically-integrated pharmaceutical company — discovering, developing, manufacturing, and commercializing drugs across multiple therapeutic areas — is still the dominant form. But it is no longer the only form, and I believe its dominance will erode over the next decade as more specialized models prove their efficiency.

Three models are most relevant. First, the technology platform company: Recursion, Relay Therapeutics, and Schrödinger are building infrastructure — AI models, automated labs, molecular simulation capabilities — that they use for internal drug discovery while also licensing to partners. The bet is that the platform value exceeds the drug-by-drug value, creating durable revenue streams. The challenge is that pharma has historically been skeptical of paying large technology licensing fees, and platform companies have struggled to monetize without proving clinical outcomes.

Second, asset-centric structures: Royalty Pharma and similar organizations have demonstrated that pharma royalties are a viable asset class. The model separates the financial risk of drug development from the operational risk. As drug development costs rise and capital markets become more risk-averse to binary biotech outcomes, asset-centric structuring will become more common. Expect to see more spinouts of late-stage pipeline assets into dedicated SPACs or royalty-backed vehicles.

Third, the AI-first biotech: companies that use AI as their primary discovery engine, not as a tool added to a classical process. Insilico Medicine, Exscientia, Generation Bio, and dozens of others are built this way from the ground up. Their culture, organizational structure, and incentive systems are designed for computational speed rather than bench science iteration. They will have different failure modes than classical biotechs — but also, potentially, different timelines to clinic.

The Patient of 2030: Empowered, Data-Rich, Expecting Cures

The patient who walks into a clinic in 2030 will look nothing like the patient of 2005. They will have continuous biometric data from wearables: heart rate variability, blood glucose, sleep staging, activity patterns. They may have a polygenic risk score on file that quantifies their lifetime risk for ten major diseases. They will have used AI health assistants to research their symptoms before the appointment. They will have connected communities of other patients with the same diagnosis, sharing real-world outcome data that no clinical trial has captured.

This patient will not accept "manage your symptoms" as a treatment goal if a cure is biologically achievable. They will not accept a therapy that works for 40% of people without demanding a biomarker test to know if they are in the 40%. They will not accept a black box about side effects when patient forums are full of granular experience data. The relationship between pharmaceutical companies and patients will become more direct, more data-mediated, and more demanding.

Companies that treat this as a compliance problem — managing what patients know and expect — will lose the trust of the very population they need to recruit for trials and ultimately sell to. Companies that treat empowered patients as partners — in trial design, in real-world evidence generation, in access advocacy — will build durability that no competitor can easily replicate.

Geopolitical Risks: Supply Chains, Re-Shoring, Pandemic Preparedness

COVID-19 exposed the pharmaceutical supply chain's hidden fragility with brutal efficiency. The concentration of API manufacturing in China and India, the single-source reliance for critical raw materials, the absence of surge manufacturing capacity for emergency needs — all of this became visible in 2020 and 2021 in ways that were embarrassing and dangerous simultaneously.

The political response has been sustained. The BARDA Domestic Manufacturing Initiative, the EU's Strategic Technologies for Europe Platform (STEP), and Japan's pharma supply chain security legislation all reflect a genuine political consensus that API re-shoring is a national security priority. This is creating investment opportunities in US and European manufacturing, but the cost differential with Asian manufacturing remains substantial — 40-60% higher in many cases — which creates a tax on domestic manufacturing that ultimately lands somewhere in the pricing system.

Pandemic preparedness is the geopolitical risk most likely to trigger the next major pharmaceutical industry transformation. The COVID-19 response demonstrated that mRNA vaccines can be designed, tested, manufactured, and deployed in twelve months from sequence to injection. That is genuinely remarkable. It also demonstrated that the global supply chain for vials, lipids, and cold-chain logistics collapses under simultaneous worldwide demand. The next pandemic may be worse. Preparing the manufacturing and regulatory infrastructure now — while there is no crisis demanding attention — requires institutional will that has historically not survived the first peacetime budget cycle.

A Personal Vision: What Pharma Must Do to Remain Essential

I have spent enough time in this industry to know its virtues and its pathologies. The virtues are real: a culture of scientific rigor that takes two decades to build, clinical development infrastructure that no other industry has replicated, regulatory expertise that keeps dangerous products off the market even when commercial pressure argues otherwise. These are genuine achievements, not PR narratives.

The pathologies are also real. A risk aversion so deep in large organizations that genuinely novel science gets licensed in rather than invented. An incentive system that rewards short-term share price performance over long-term scientific bet-making. A pricing and access model that has become so detached from social legitimacy that it invites the blunt political interventions it fears most. A diversity problem in leadership that results in homogeneous decision-making on problems that require diverse cognitive approaches.

If I could issue a strategic mandate to the industry for the next decade, it would be this: act as if your social license to operate is finite and must be earned every year. Not because it is the right thing to do (though it is), but because it is the strategically correct thing to do. The moment the public ceases to believe that pharmaceutical companies serve human welfare ahead of shareholder returns — even imperfectly, even with commercial motives — the political support for the innovation model collapses. We are closer to that moment than most pharma executives are comfortable acknowledging.

"Pharma is not just an industry. It is the only enterprise in human history organized around the systematic extension of healthy human life. Nothing in civilization is more important. The question is whether the people inside it believe that enough to act accordingly." — Author's reflection, written while founding his current company, 2024

A Challenge to the Next Generation: Ten Things You Must Do Differently

I have mentored hundreds of people over forty years. The best ones have one thing in common: they reject the premise that how we have done things is the best we can do. Here is what I want the next generation of pharmaceutical leaders to do differently than we did:

  1. Start with the patient, not the molecule. Define the therapeutic goal in terms of what a patient gains — time, function, quality of life, cure — before you design the molecule or the trial. Too much drug development is target-driven rather than outcome-driven. The targets that matter are the ones that, when engaged, give patients something they did not have before.
  2. Use AI as a first-principles tool, not a garnish. Do not add an AI team to a classical drug discovery organization and call it transformation. Rebuild the discovery process around computational first principles, with wet lab validation as the confirmation step rather than the starting point. This requires organizational courage because it threatens existing expertise hierarchies.
  3. Price for value, not for what the market will bear. Value-based pricing is not a concession to populist politics; it is the most durable commercial model for an industry whose products are scrutinized by payers, politicians, and the public simultaneously. Develop the health economics infrastructure to know, before launch, what a year of life gained in your indication costs in your drug. Price close to that number. It will require giving up some margin in some cases. It will purchase social legitimacy that sustains your ability to fund the next drug.
  4. Make clinical trials representative of the population that will use the drug. The persistent failure to enroll clinical trials with demographic diversity that matches the disease population is a scientific failure as much as an equity failure. A drug tested in a predominantly white, male, 55-70 year-old population may behave differently in Black women or elderly Asian patients. Expand enrollment deliberately. Build decentralized trial infrastructure that removes the geographic and logistical barriers that create homogeneous study populations.
  5. Engage the FDA as a scientific partner, not a gatekeeper to be managed. The agency is staffed with scientists who want to see good drugs approved. The adversarial relationship that develops between sponsors and reviewers during contentious reviews benefits no one. Companies that invest in pre-submission engagement, share data generously, and treat the review process as collaborative rather than adversarial get better outcomes on average. The culture of treating regulators as obstacles is self-defeating.
  6. Solve manufacturing before you need it at scale. The most consistent failure in pharmaceutical scale-up is discovering manufacturing problems at the point of commercial launch rather than Phase II. Gene therapy, cell therapy, mRNA therapeutics, and complex antibody formats all have manufacturing challenges that are knowable before the pivotal trial. Invest in process development proportionally to the clinical investment, not as an afterthought after regulatory approval creates urgency.
  7. Build data and AI infrastructure as a core asset, not an IT function. The pharmaceutical companies that will win the next decade are accumulating proprietary biological data — multi-omics, real-world outcomes, biomarker repositories — and training AI models on that data to generate insights no competitor can replicate. This requires treating data science as a primary business function, not a support function. It means paying data scientists at market rates, publishing data science results to attract talent, and building data governance infrastructure that makes the data scientifically usable.
  8. Invest in pandemic preparedness infrastructure even when there is no pandemic. The next pathogen will not announce itself in advance. The mRNA platform has demonstrated that the design-to-dose timeline can be compressed to weeks if the manufacturing and regulatory infrastructure is pre-positioned. Pharma companies, governments, and multilateral institutions (CEPI, BARDA, GAVI) need to maintain warm manufacturing capacity for pandemic-relevant platforms. This is a cost with uncertain return on an unknown timeline, which is exactly the kind of investment that gets cut in every budget cycle. Resist that impulse.
  9. Take longevity science seriously while maintaining scientific standards. The aging biology is real. The potential market is extraordinary. The hype around longevity is also dangerous — it attracts capital to speculative claims and enables charlatans who sell supplements and off-label rapamycin without the evidence base to justify it. The next generation of pharma leaders should engage longevity science with the same rigor applied to any other area, which means demanding solid mechanistic biology, robust preclinical models, and clinical endpoints that regulators can evaluate. The field deserves rigorous investment, not evangelical enthusiasm.
  10. Lead with the mission when recruiting, and mean it. The best scientists and technologists who join pharma do so because they believe the mission justifies the complexity and frustration of the industry. That belief is not naive; it is accurate. But it requires that the institution actually behave in accordance with the mission — that it makes pricing decisions, R&D portfolio decisions, and organizational decisions as if human welfare is the north star. If you say the mission matters and then optimize exclusively for earnings-per-share, you will lose the people who came for the mission. They will go to academia, or to the biotech that believes what it says.

Conclusion: The Most Important Industry on Earth

I began this chapter by asserting that this is the most important moment in pharmaceutical history since penicillin. Let me end by asserting something else: the outcome is not predetermined. The convergence of AI, genomics, gene editing, RNA medicine, and an extraordinary volume of biological data creates the potential for a transformation in human health that would have seemed miraculous to the scientists I trained under. It does not guarantee it.

The potential will be realized only if the people inside this industry — scientists, executives, regulators, investors, patient advocates — make a series of decisions over the next decade that prioritize long-term scientific ambition over short-term commercial safety. That means funding programs with five-year time horizons when the incentive system rewards quarterly guidance. It means building manufacturing capacity before it is needed. It means pricing medicines in ways that create social legitimacy rather than extracting maximum value from temporary monopoly. It means including every population in clinical evidence, even when logistical difficulty makes homogeneous enrollment easier.

None of this is easy. I know because I have tried, with mixed success, across a career that has now spanned the era of recombinant proteins, the monoclonal antibody revolution, the genomics revolution, and now the AI era. Each time, the science proved more transformative than the pessimists predicted and the timelines proved longer than the optimists hoped. I expect the same will be true of the decade ahead.

What I am certain of is this: forty years from now, the people who look back at 2025-2035 will either say it was the decade when the pharmaceutical industry rose to the moment — when it channeled the most remarkable scientific capabilities in human history into the most durable improvement in human health ever achieved — or they will say it was the decade when the industry fumbled it, when institutional inertia, pricing politics, and short-term thinking squandered a once-in-a-century opportunity.

I am founding a new company at this inflection point precisely because I believe the former outcome is achievable. The science is there. The tools are there. The talent is there. What remains is the institutional will to use them with the urgency the moment demands.

That is the challenge I leave to the next generation. I have no doubt they are capable of meeting it.
