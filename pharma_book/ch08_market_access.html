8.1 Market Access as a Strategic Function

Market access is the discipline of ensuring that approved medicines reach patients through mechanisms of payer coverage, reimbursement, and favorable formulary positioning. Conceptually it bridges three domains: health economics (demonstrating value), pricing (capturing that value), and policy (navigating the regulatory and institutional frameworks that govern payment). In practice, it is one of the most resource-intensive, intellectually demanding, and chronically underinvested functions in pharma.

For most of my career, market access was viewed as a late-stage commercial support function—teams mobilized after Phase III readout to "get the payers on board." This framing is catastrophically wrong. Drugs that fail on access almost always reveal design decisions made years earlier: endpoints that didn't align with payer decision frameworks, comparators that didn't match clinical practice, health-related quality of life instruments that were never validated, cost-effectiveness models that couldn't be built because the right data were never collected. The organizational implication is clear: market access strategy must begin at IND, not NDA.

8.1.1 Organizational Placement and Scope

The market access function sits differently across companies. At GSK and AbbVie, it reports directly into the Chief Commercial Officer with strong dotted lines to Medical Affairs and Regulatory. At Roche and Novartis, HEOR (Health Economics and Outcomes Research) sits within a combined Global Medical & Access division, reflecting the belief that economic evidence generation is scientific work, not commercial work. Both models have merit; what matters is that access has a seat at the table during portfolio decisions, clinical design, and regulatory strategy.

Core market access functions include:

  • Global Pricing Strategy: setting list prices, net price targets, rebate structures, and international reference price management
  • HTA Submission & Dossier Development: building clinical and economic evidence packages for payer bodies in each market
  • Health Economics & Outcomes Research (HEOR): economic model development, real-world evidence (RWE) generation, PRO instrument selection
  • Payer Engagement: managed care contracting in the US; government payer negotiation in Europe, Canada, Australia
  • Patient Access Programs: co-pay assistance, patient support programs, specialty pharmacy networks
  • Policy & Government Affairs: shaping legislation and regulation affecting reimbursement pathways
"The drug that cures a disease but sits on Tier 4 with a $300 co-pay is not a success. It's a tragedy with good Phase III data." — Former VP, Global Access, Novartis, addressing internal strategy review, Basel 2019

8.2 Health Technology Assessment: The Global Gatekeeper Landscape

Health Technology Assessment (HTA) is the systematic evaluation of the clinical, economic, social, and ethical properties of a health technology. Every major pharmaceutical market now has an HTA process that determines whether a drug will be reimbursed, at what price, and with what restrictions. Understanding these bodies—their methodological preferences, evidentiary standards, and political contexts—is the single most important market access competency for any global pharma organization.

The AMNOG Model: Germany as the Global Pricing Bellwether

The 2011 AMNOG (Arzneimittelmarktreformgesetz) law fundamentally restructured European pharmaceutical pricing. Prior to AMNOG, Germany had free pricing—manufacturers set launch prices freely and received statutory reimbursement. AMNOG changed this: from day 91 post-launch, a negotiated price applies retroactively, determined by the benefit rating the G-BA assigns. A "no added benefit" finding means the drug receives no premium above the reference price of the appropriate comparator. A "considerable benefit" rating unlocks meaningful price headroom. Because Germany is a founding member of many international reference pricing systems, a low AMNOG-negotiated price cascades to Austria, Belgium, the Czech Republic, and others. The strategic implication: G-BA strategy is not a German problem. It is a global pricing problem.

8.2.2 HTA Submission Requirements

Every HTA submission, regardless of jurisdiction, requires a carefully constructed evidence package. The core components are:

  • Clinical Evidence Dossier: Systematic review of all evidence for the drug and comparators; indirect treatment comparisons (network meta-analyses) where head-to-head data are absent; subgroup analyses relevant to the approved indication; long-term extrapolation of trial data
  • Economic Model: A formal health economic model (Markov, decision tree, partitioned survival, or hybrid) demonstrating cost-effectiveness vs. the nominated comparator in the payer's healthcare system
  • Budget Impact Analysis: A 3-5 year projection of the aggregate budget impact on the payer system; NICE, CADTH, and most European bodies require this separately from the ICER analysis
  • Patient-Reported Outcomes: Evidence on health-related quality of life (HRQoL) using validated instruments; the EQ-5D is near-universal for QALY-based systems
  • Real-World Evidence: Increasingly required to supplement RCT data, especially for rare diseases or accelerated approvals where trial populations are narrow

8.3 Health Economics: The Quantitative Language of Value

Health economics provides the analytical framework through which payers evaluate whether a drug delivers sufficient benefit to justify its cost. Mastering this discipline is not optional for anyone in market access, medical affairs, or executive leadership. The concepts that follow are the vocabulary of every formulary decision made in the developed world.

8.3.1 Cost-Effectiveness Analysis and Cost-Utility Analysis

Cost-effectiveness analysis (CEA) compares two or more interventions on a common natural unit outcome—for example, life-years gained, hospitalizations avoided, or mmHg reduction in blood pressure. CEA is useful for comparisons within a disease area but cannot compare value across therapeutic areas.

Cost-utility analysis (CUA) addresses this limitation by expressing outcomes in Quality-Adjusted Life Years (QALYs)—a generic measure that weights each year of life by the quality of health experienced (1.0 = perfect health; 0 = death; values below 0 are possible for health states worse than death). CUA enables cross-disease comparison and is the foundation of NICE, CADTH, PBAC, and most European HTA systems.

8.3.2 QALYs and ICERs

The Incremental Cost-Effectiveness Ratio (ICER) is the central metric of health economic evaluation:

ICER = (Costnew − Costcomparator) / (QALYnew − QALYcomparator)

The ICER is then compared to a willingness-to-pay (WTP) threshold—the maximum a payer is willing to spend per additional QALY gained. Drugs with ICERs below the threshold are considered cost-effective; those above are not, unless other factors (severity, unmet need, innovation) justify an exception.

8.3.3 Economic Modeling Approaches

The economic model is the analytical engine of any HTA submission. Model choice depends on disease natural history, available data, and the HTA body's methodological preferences.

Markov Models

The workhorse of chronic disease modeling. Patients occupy mutually exclusive health states (e.g., stable disease, progressed, dead); transition probabilities govern movement between states over fixed cycles (usually monthly or quarterly). Each state has an associated cost and utility weight. Markov models are well-suited for oncology, cardiovascular disease, diabetes, and any condition where patients transition between well-defined stages. Limitation: memoryless (Markovian assumption)—future transitions cannot depend on how long a patient has been in a state unless tunnel states are used.

Partitioned Survival Models

Dominant in oncology HTA submissions. Three health states—progression-free, progressed, and dead—are defined by extrapolated survival curves (PFS and OS) directly from trial data. The proportion of patients in each state at any time is derived from the area under the survival curves. NICE oncology submissions almost universally use this structure. Key technical challenge: survival extrapolation beyond trial follow-up, where choice of parametric function (Weibull, log-normal, Gompertz, generalized gamma) can dramatically alter lifetime cost and QALY estimates.

Decision Trees

Used for acute conditions, diagnostic pathways, or short time horizons where Markov cycling is unnecessary. Each branch represents a possible clinical event or decision, with associated probabilities, costs, and outcomes. Common in infectious disease (e.g., treatment of acute bacterial infection), diagnostic value of care models, and surgical decision frameworks.

Discrete Event Simulation (DES)

The most flexible modeling approach: patients are simulated individually, events occur in continuous time, and history-dependency is fully accommodated. DES is increasingly used for rare diseases, gene therapies, and complex multi-line treatment pathways. Computationally intensive; requires substantial individual patient data.

Survival Extrapolation: Where Models Can Fail

In the early days of immuno-oncology HTA submissions (2015–2018), manufacturers used long-tail survival extrapolations based on 18-month trial follow-up data, claiming 20-30% cure fractions. NICE and IQWiG pushed back hard. The methodological debate about whether plateau effects in Kaplan-Meier curves reflect true cure or censoring artifacts resulted in NICE issuing explicit guidance on extrapolation uncertainty (DSU Technical Support Document 21). Today, any survival extrapolation claiming a cure fraction without external validation data will face intense HTA scrutiny. Model credibility is non-negotiable.

8.4.2 The Three PBMs and Formulary Power

In the United States, three PBMs—Express Scripts (now Evernorth, owned by Cigna), CVS Caremark, and OptumRx (a UnitedHealth subsidiary)—collectively manage pharmaceutical benefits for roughly 270 million Americans. Their formulary decisions, powered by rebate negotiations, determine whether a drug is Tier 1 (preferred generic), Tier 2 (preferred brand), Tier 3 (non-preferred brand), Tier 4 (specialty), or excluded entirely.

A drug excluded from a national PBM formulary faces patient access barriers that no amount of physician sampling overcomes. Exclusion means patients who request the drug from their physician receive either a prior authorization denial or are automatically substituted to a competitor. The rebate a manufacturer pays to earn preferred positioning can range from 20% to 70% of WAC (Wholesale Acquisition Cost), depending on class competitiveness. Cardiovascular, diabetes, and immunology classes are particularly rebate-intensive; drugs in less competitive specialty areas retain more list-to-net integrity.

8.5 US Pricing Dynamics: The Architecture of List and Net

American drug pricing is not a single price—it is a cascade of discounts, rebates, and fees that transforms a published list price into a realized net price that can be dramatically lower. Understanding this architecture is essential to building a viable US launch strategy.

8.5.1 The Pricing Waterfall

WAC (Wholesale Acquisition Cost): The manufacturer's published list price to wholesalers. This is the price appearing in public databases (Medi-Span, Red Book). It is the anchor for all other price calculations.

ASP (Average Selling Price): For Part B drugs (physician-administered), Medicare reimburses at ASP + 6%. ASP is the volume-weighted average of all non-exempt manufacturer sales, reported quarterly. Rebates and discounts reduce ASP, which reduces Part B reimbursement.

Best Price: The lowest price a manufacturer sells a drug to any commercial customer, exclusive of certain statutory programs. Best Price determines Medicaid base rebate calculations (AMP + 23.1% or Best Price minus AMP, whichever is greater). Any pricing concession to any commercial customer—rebates, free goods, co-pay programs—can trigger Best Price recalculation with costly Medicaid implications.

340B Price: Under the 340B Drug Pricing Program, covered entities (safety-net hospitals, FQHCs, certain clinics) access drugs at deeply discounted prices—often below Medicaid levels. The 340B program has expanded dramatically; major academic medical centers with 340B eligibility now represent a significant share of specialty drug volume.

Net Price: WAC minus all rebates, discounts, and fees paid to PBMs, health plans, government programs, and specialty distributors. The spread between WAC and net price has grown dramatically since 2010; as of 2024, industry-wide net-to-WAC ratios average approximately 55% (meaning 45% of list price is returned as discounts and rebates). In highly competitive classes like insulin, GLP-1 agonists, and TNF inhibitors, net prices can be 70-80% below list.

8.5.2 The Inflation Reduction Act: A Structural Shift

The Inflation Reduction Act of 2022 (IRA) represents the most significant US drug pricing legislation since the Hatch-Waxman Act of 1984. Its key provisions affecting market access:

  • Medicare Drug Price Negotiation: For the first time, CMS is authorized to directly negotiate prices with manufacturers for high-expenditure drugs lacking generic/biosimilar competition. Year 1 (2026): 10 Part D drugs, including Eliquis, Jardiance, Farxiga, Entresto, Enbrel. Year 2 (2027): 15 more Part D drugs. Year 3 (2028): 15 Part D + 15 Part B drugs. Eventually, up to 20 drugs per year will be negotiated. The maximum fair price (MFP) negotiations use a ceiling based on years of market exclusivity and a non-small molecule / small molecule distinction.
  • Inflation Rebates: Manufacturers must pay rebates to CMS if drug prices increase faster than CPI-U. This effectively caps real price growth at CPI for Medicare-paid drugs. Companies that previously relied on annual WAC increases of 6-9% to offset growing rebate pressures must now reassess pricing strategy fundamentally.
  • Part D Redesign: Eliminates the donut hole; introduces a $2,000 annual out-of-pocket cap for Part D beneficiaries; shifts more manufacturer liability into the catastrophic phase via the Manufacturer Discount Program. This dramatically changes the incentive structure of Part D contracting and will reduce manufacturer revenues in certain therapeutic areas.
  • Short Small Molecule vs. Biologic Cliff: IRA defines small molecules as eligible for negotiation after 9 years; biologics after 13 years. This has already shifted R&D investment incentives toward biologics and complex modalities, and away from small molecule NCEs that would face negotiation before full commercial lifecycle realization.
"The IRA changed the economic equation for small molecule drug development more fundamentally than most people realize. When you model the NPV of a drug that will face price negotiation at year 9, versus a biologic that gets until year 13, you're talking about hundreds of millions in portfolio value. Every pipeline prioritization decision made after 2022 will carry the IRA's fingerprints." — Former Chief Strategy Officer, Pfizer, speaking at the ISPOR Annual Meeting, 2023

8.6 European Pricing Systems

8.6.1 Reference Pricing and International Reference Pricing (IRP)

International Reference Pricing (IRP) is the practice by which national payers set maximum reimbursable prices by reference to prices in a basket of other countries. IRP is one of the most powerful—and most commercially disruptive—mechanisms in global pharmaceutical markets. A low price achieved in one country can cascade through reference pricing to reduce prices in 20-30 other markets.

Germany, Austria, Belgium, the Czech Republic, Greece, Portugal, Spain, Hungary, and many others formally incorporate IRP into their pricing regulations. The specific basket of reference countries, the averaging methodology (minimum, arithmetic mean, weighted by GDP or PPP), and the frequency of price updates vary widely. The strategic implication: launch sequence must be managed with IRP cascades explicitly modeled.

8.6.2 Tender Markets

In many European countries—Italy, Spain at the regional level, the Nordics, Poland, Romania, and increasingly in hospital-based oncology across Europe—drugs are procured through tender processes. Tenders involve competitive bidding for exclusive or near-exclusive supply contracts for defined patient populations. The winner takes most or all of the market; the loser receives zero volume, regardless of clinical differentiation. Tenders are particularly dominant in biosimilars (where the original biologic often cannot compete on price) and generic markets. Original brands in tender-dominated markets must decide whether to participate (with significant price concessions) or withdraw, accepting market share loss but protecting reference prices elsewhere.

8.6.3 Managed Entry Agreements

Managed entry agreements (MEAs), also called risk-sharing arrangements, outcomes-based contracts, or performance-based agreements, are negotiated arrangements between manufacturers and payers that link reimbursement to real-world outcomes or budget constraints. They are increasingly common as HTA bodies face uncertainty about long-term drug effectiveness and payers seek protection against paying for drugs that underperform their trial results.

MEAs fall into two broad categories:

  • Financial-based agreements: Price-volume agreements (cap total expenditure; if volumes exceed threshold, price drops); simple discounts; rebates conditional on budget impact
  • Outcomes-based agreements: Conditional reimbursement (full reimbursement contingent on achieving real-world clinical outcomes); payment by results (manufacturer pays rebate or refund if outcomes targets not met); coverage with evidence development (reimbursement conditional on collection of additional real-world evidence within defined timeframe)

Italy's AIFA (Agenzia Italiana del Farmaco) registry system is the most sophisticated national MEA infrastructure globally, tracking patient-level outcomes for hundreds of drugs and triggering automatic rebates when outcomes criteria are missed. The UK's NHS managed access agreements and the NICE Outcome-Based Agreement framework serve similar purposes. For manufacturers, MEAs require substantial investment in data infrastructure, registry design, and outcome adjudication capabilities.

8.7 Global Pricing Strategy: Corridors, Sequencing, and Launch Order

Every global pharmaceutical company faces the same fundamental tension: drugs are worth more in wealthy markets (US, Germany, Switzerland, Japan) than in lower-income markets (Eastern Europe, LATAM, Southeast Asia), but international reference pricing creates pressure toward the lowest common denominator. Resolving this tension requires a disciplined global pricing architecture.

8.7.1 Price Corridors and Launch Sequencing

A price corridor is the range of net prices a manufacturer targets across its global portfolio, calibrated to (1) reflect differential purchasing power and willingness to pay by market tier, (2) maintain internal consistency to avoid reference pricing disasters, and (3) protect the US price anchor, which typically drives 60-80% of global branded drug revenues for large-cap pharma.

Standard launch sequencing for a major global brand:

  1. US first: Launch at WAC reflecting full commercial value; no government mandated price; negotiate with PBMs to establish net price and formulary positioning
  2. Germany simultaneously or immediately after: Germany's free pricing during the first 12 months (AMNOG provision) means no price restriction at launch; negotiate G-BA benefit assessment aggressively to achieve premium AMNOG price before the retroactive negotiated price applies
  3. Major EU5 (UK, France, Italy, Spain) next: Each market sequenced based on IRP basket membership; UK (NICE) can be slow due to appraisal timeline but does not typically reference into major EU markets; France and Italy reference each other and much of Eastern Europe
  4. Canada, Japan, Australia: Mid-tier markets; CADTH and PBAC often follow after EU HTA data are available
  5. Low-income / emerging markets last: Tiered pricing, differential pricing, or voluntary licensing programs; these prices must be structurally separated from developed market prices through program design to avoid IRP contamination

8.8 HEOR Strategy: Building the Evidence Architecture

HEOR—Health Economics and Outcomes Research—is the discipline responsible for generating, analyzing, and communicating the economic and quality-of-life evidence needed to support market access decisions. It is not a submission-writing function. It is a strategic research function that must plan, fund, and execute a multi-year evidence generation program aligned with the HTA requirements of every target market.

8.8.1 Early HEOR Planning: Starting at IND

The fundamental HEOR activities that must occur during Phase II and early Phase III include:

  • HTA landscape analysis: Identify the relevant HTA body requirements for each target market; identify the appropriate comparator(s) the HTA body will use; assess whether planned trial comparators match HTA expectations (misalignment here is fatal)
  • PRO instrument selection: Select validated HRQoL instruments (EQ-5D-5L for most QALY-based systems; condition-specific measures where required) and embed them in Phase II/III protocols before first patient in; retrospective PRO data are rarely accepted by NICE or CADTH
  • Early economic model development: Prototype models before Phase III readout to identify evidence gaps; the model structure defines what data the trial must collect (e.g., which resource use data, which utility data, which subgroup analyses)
  • Indirect treatment comparison planning: Where head-to-head trial data vs. HTA comparator will not exist, design the NMA (Network Meta-Analysis) strategy; identify the evidence network; plan systematic reviews
  • RWE strategy: Design real-world data collection plans (claims, EMR, registries) that will generate population-level evidence to supplement trial data post-approval

8.8.2 The Value Dossier

The global value dossier (GVD) is the master evidence compendium from which all country-specific HTA submissions are adapted. It is not a single document—it is a structured library containing:

  • Disease burden section: epidemiology, natural history, unmet need, patient-reported burden
  • Clinical evidence section: systematic review, direct evidence summary, NMA, subgroup analyses, safety summary
  • Economic evidence section: cost-effectiveness model structure and results, budget impact model, sensitivity analyses, scenario analyses
  • Patient and societal value section: caregiver burden, productivity losses, societal costs, equity considerations

Country teams adapt GVD content to local HTA templates, local epidemiology, local cost inputs, and local clinical practice patterns. The GVD is typically finalized 12-18 months before first regulatory submission; adaptation for major markets (NICE, G-BA, CADTH) begins concurrently with regulatory review.

8.8.3 Real-World Evidence in HTA

RWE has evolved from a supplementary curiosity to a critical component of HTA submissions, particularly in oncology, rare disease, and drugs seeking accelerated or conditional approval. NICE's Real-World Evidence Framework (2022), CADTH's RWE guidelines, and EMA's RWE platform (DARWIN EU) all reflect this shift.

RWE is used in HTA contexts to:

  • Characterize the real-world patient population eligible for the drug (often broader and sicker than trial populations)
  • Validate trial efficacy assumptions in post-approval outcomes research
  • Support outcomes-based MEAs with objective outcome tracking data
  • Demonstrate effectiveness in subpopulations underrepresented in trials (elderly, multi-comorbid, pediatric)
  • Fulfill coverage-with-evidence-development requirements

8.9 Formulary Management: Tier Placement, Step Therapy, and Prior Authorization

Even with a positive HTA recommendation and a negotiated reimbursement price, a drug may still be inaccessible to patients due to formulary management tools that payers use to steer utilization toward lower-cost alternatives. Understanding these tools and how to negotiate around them is operational market access.

8.9.1 Tier Placement

Most commercial formularies have 3-5 tiers, with Tier 1 (lowest co-pay, generics) through Tier 4-5 (highest co-pay, specialty drugs). A brand drug placed on Tier 3 vs. Tier 2 may see 30-50% lower prescription volume due to patient cost sensitivity. The incremental rebate required to achieve preferred Tier 2 placement over non-preferred Tier 3 can be substantial but is often worth the net revenue impact for high-volume drugs.

8.9.2 Step Therapy

Step therapy ("fail-first" policies) requires patients to try and fail one or more less expensive drugs before the payer will cover the target drug. Step therapy is common in therapeutic classes with multiple effective options (TNF inhibitors, antidepressants, anticonvulsants, diabetes agents). For specialty drugs treating serious conditions, step therapy can delay appropriate treatment for months, causing clinical harm. Many states have enacted step therapy reform laws requiring override provisions for certain conditions (oncology, rare disease); these laws vary significantly in scope and enforceability.

8.9.3 Prior Authorization

Prior authorization (PA) requires prescribers to seek payer approval before a drug is covered. PA criteria typically include: confirmed diagnosis meeting coverage criteria; documented failure of required prior therapies; prescriber specialty requirements; lab or biomarker results; compliance with risk evaluation and mitigation strategies (REMS). PA processes create administrative burden, delay treatment initiation, and in some analyses lead to patient drop-off rates of 20-40% between prescription and first fill. Market access strategy must include a PA minimization negotiation track as part of payer contracting, particularly for innovative specialty drugs where PA burden falls disproportionately on patient populations with limited ability to navigate complex appeals processes.

8.10 Patient Access Programs and Specialty Pharmacy

Even with favorable formulary positioning, patients may face cost-sharing burdens that prevent adherence or initial fill. Patient access programs bridge this gap through several mechanisms.

8.10.1 Co-Pay Cards and Patient Assistance Programs

Co-pay cards (also called co-pay coupons or co-pay assistance programs) are manufacturer-funded programs that cover some or all of the patient's out-of-pocket cost-sharing obligation for commercially insured patients. They are extremely effective at reducing patient abandonment at the pharmacy counter; studies show co-pay programs reduce prescription abandonment rates by 60-70% for high-cost specialty drugs.

Critical limitation: CMS prohibits co-pay assistance for Medicare and Medicaid patients. This creates a significant access gap for the elderly and low-income populations who may not qualify for PAPs but face substantial Part D cost-sharing. The IRA's $2,000 Part D out-of-pocket cap partially addresses this for Medicare patients starting 2025.

Patient Assistance Programs (PAPs) provide free drug to uninsured or underinsured patients who cannot afford their medication. Nearly all major manufacturers operate PAPs; eligibility typically requires income below 200-400% of the federal poverty level and absence of qualifying insurance coverage. PAPs are administered through specialty pharmacies, foundations, or directly by the manufacturer. They serve an important access function but do not address the broader systemic issue of affordability for middle-income patients who exceed income thresholds yet cannot afford specialty drug co-pays.

8.10.2 Specialty Pharmacy Networks

Specialty pharmacy is the distribution channel for high-cost, complex-administration drugs requiring special handling, patient education, or clinical monitoring. Specialty pharmacies provide services that retail pharmacies cannot: nursing support, adherence programs, cold-chain management, REMS program administration, and specialty payer navigation. Major specialty pharmacies include Accredo (Evernorth/Express Scripts), Caremark Specialty (CVS), BriovaRx (OptumRx), and Walgreens Specialty.

Manufacturers negotiate limited distribution networks (LDNs) for drugs requiring tight distribution control, REMS compliance, or specialty patient support. LDNs channel all dispensing through 1-5 specialty pharmacies, enabling data collection on dispensing patterns, patient support program enrollment, and outcome tracking. The decision to operate an LDN vs. open distribution involves tradeoffs between access breadth, data capture, and supply chain control.

8.11 Rare Disease Reimbursement: Orphan Pathways and Special Considerations

Rare diseases present a unique market access challenge: small patient populations mean trial data are limited, natural history is often poorly characterized, health utilities are rarely measured with validated instruments, and the comparator is frequently best supportive care or no treatment at all. Yet the unmet need is often profound and the cost of development high, creating a compelling moral and economic case for premium pricing.

8.11.1 Orphan Drug Frameworks and HTA

Most HTA systems have developed special pathways for orphan drugs that apply modified evidentiary standards and different WTP thresholds:

  • NICE Highly Specialised Technologies (HST): Applies to ultra-rare diseases (prevalence <1 in 50,000 in England); WTP threshold of £100,000-300,000/QALY; requires robust data collection post-approval; approximately 25-30 products assessed under this pathway to date
  • NICE Innovative Medicines Fund (IMF): Successor to the Cancer Drugs Fund; provides managed access for drugs with promising evidence while longer-term data mature; payments contingent on hitting clinical outcome triggers
  • Germany — Orphan exception: Drugs with EMA orphan designation receive automatic "non-quantifiable" benefit rating if annual sales are below €30M; this exempts them from the most restrictive G-BA assessments; however, crossing the €30M sales threshold triggers a full AMNOG assessment retrospectively—a cliff that has created complex commercial planning challenges
  • France — ASMR V / ATU / AAP: France's early access authorization (Autorisation d'Accès Précoce, formerly ATU) provides compassionate use pricing before formal HAS evaluation; manufacturers receive free pricing during AAP; post-HAS price is negotiated and AAP payments reconciled
"When I led the global access strategy for a spinal muscular atrophy therapy at $2.1 million per dose, I didn't sleep well the night before the NICE appraisal committee meeting. Not because of the clinical data—that was strong. But because the economic model relied on survival extrapolations 30 years beyond our trial data and utilities estimated in a disease so rare that EQ-5D normative values barely existed. We got approved, but it took three rounds of re-analysis and a patient access scheme with outcomes monitoring that required us to build a data infrastructure we'd never built before. Rare disease market access is the hardest thing in this business." — Former VP, Global Rare Disease Access, with experience at BMS and Novartis

8.11.2 Gene Therapy Pricing Models

Gene therapies present the most extreme case of the tension between clinical value and reimbursement reality. A one-time curative treatment that prevents lifetime disease burden can be economically justified at prices of $1-3M—but no payer is structured to absorb a single $2M drug cost, even if it saves $5M in future healthcare costs. The key emerging solutions:

  • Annuity payment models: Multi-year installment payments contingent on sustained efficacy (Novartis Zolgensma's outcomes-based payment program); requires payer commitment to multi-year contracts
  • Reinsurance and risk pooling: Federal mechanisms to pool catastrophic drug cost risk across payers (discussed but not yet implemented at scale in the US)
  • NHS England gene therapy framework: Commercial framework that provides one-time payment but with clawback provisions if outcomes fail to materialize; pioneered for Zolgensma, Hemgenix, and Casgevy

8.12 Value-Based Contracts and Outcomes-Based Agreements

Value-based contracts (VBCs) and outcomes-based agreements (OBAs) represent the frontier of market access strategy—arrangements in which the manufacturer's payment is explicitly linked to the drug's real-world performance rather than volume alone. They are simultaneously the most intellectually satisfying and operationally difficult market access tool.

Successful OBA programs share several characteristics: a clearly defined, measurable, and administratively feasible outcome measure; a mutually agreed-upon threshold for rebate triggering; robust data infrastructure to capture outcomes without onerous burden on prescribers or patients; and a contract term long enough to observe clinically meaningful outcomes.

Notable examples include Novartis Entresto's heart failure hospitalization OBA with Harvard Pilgrim (later expanded to multiple plans), Cigna's outcomes-based Humira agreement, and the ICER-facilitated OBA framework for PCSK9 inhibitors that linked real-world LDL-lowering and CV event prevention to rebate structures. All these agreements face the same structural challenges: claims data capture events but not all outcomes; attribution of outcome to drug vs. other factors is methodologically complex; payer administrative infrastructure to operationalize OBAs is immature in most US health plans.

8.13 How to Build a Compelling Value Dossier

A compelling value dossier is not a compendium of trial data. It is a persuasive, evidence-supported argument that the drug delivers sufficient benefit, to the right patients, at an acceptable cost to the health system, compared to the realistic alternative. Building one requires disciplined thinking about the audience, the argument, and the evidence.

8.13.1 Structure and Content of an Effective Submission

Executive summary that leads with the conclusion: The HTA committee chair should be able to read two pages and understand precisely what you are claiming, why you are claiming it, and what the economic and clinical bottom line is. Most submissions bury the lead in methodology sections no committee member reads thoroughly.

Disease burden that makes unmet need vivid: Quantify not just incidence and prevalence but the lived experience of the disease: quality of life scores vs. general population norms, productivity losses, caregiver burden, patient testimony integrated with evidence. The burden section sets the WTP context for the committee before they open the economic model.

Comparator selection that reflects clinical reality: The single biggest source of HTA conflict is comparator misalignment. If you choose a comparator that makes your drug look good but doesn't match what the HTA body considers standard of care, you will face a recommendation that reanalyzes against their preferred comparator—and the result is rarely favorable. Engage HTA bodies in pre-submission scientific advice to lock comparator alignment before the submission.

Economic model transparency and modularity: NICE's Evidence Review Group (ERG) and CADTH's Economic Guidance Panel will rebuild your model from scratch. Design your model to be transparent, well-documented, and modular so that parameter substitution is easy. An impenetrable black-box model that produces favorable results under all scenarios is a red flag that reviewers are trained to identify and challenge.

Sensitivity analyses that honestly explore uncertainty: Present one-way sensitivity analyses, probabilistic sensitivity analysis (PSA) with credible intervals, scenario analyses for key model drivers. If there are scenarios under which the drug is not cost-effective, acknowledge them and address them directly. HTA bodies are experienced in finding the scenarios you didn't present; surfacing them yourself builds credibility.

Budget impact that matches payer financial reality: The budget impact model should use realistic uptake projections based on clinical indication prevalence and prescribing behavior, not optimistic commercial forecasts. A budget impact model that projects 80% market penetration in Year 1 will be dismissed immediately; realistic market dynamics (including competitor persistence, physician inertia, formulary restrictions) build analytical credibility.

The Pre-Submission Scientific Advice Investment

NICE, G-BA/IQWiG, and CADTH all offer formal pre-submission scientific advice programs. These are among the highest-ROI activities in market access. A single NICE scientific advice meeting, attended by the right HEOR, clinical, and regulatory team members, can identify comparator conflicts, methodology concerns, and data gaps early enough to course-correct Phase III design or plan bridging studies. The cost—a few hundred thousand dollars in team time—is trivial compared to the cost of an unexpected HTA rejection or a negative G-BA benefit rating that forces a price below the cost of goods. Every drug targeting the EU5 or Canada should invest in formal scientific advice during Phase II. No exceptions.

8.14 Integrating Market Access Across the Drug Development Lifecycle

The most important organizational change any pharmaceutical company can make to improve market access outcomes is integrating access thinking into the earliest stages of drug development. This requires structural changes, not just exhortations to "think about payers earlier."

At AbbVie, the organizational model that produced some of the most successful access outcomes in the industry embedded a HEOR scientist and a market access strategy lead on every Phase II asset team. Their role was not to do submission prep work. It was to challenge clinical design decisions in real time: "If we power this trial for a composite endpoint but the HTA body only values the hard outcome, we have a problem." At Roche, the Personalized Healthcare division developed a framework for integrating HTA requirements into the Target Product Profile (TPP) as a companion to regulatory requirements—creating a payer TPP alongside the regulatory TPP from the moment of candidate selection.

The emerging standard of excellence in market access integration requires:

  • Payer advisory boards convened during Phase II to stress-test value hypotheses before first patient treated
  • HTA scientific advice sought before Phase III protocol finalization
  • Economic model prototyping completed before Phase III database lock
  • Real-world data strategy and registry design initiated at product launch
  • Outcomes-based contract templates negotiated with anchor payers within 6 months of approval
  • Global pricing corridor analysis updated with every major clinical data cut
"I have seen drugs that should have changed medicine fail in the market because the people who discovered them were never in the same room as the people who would pay for them. I have seen modestly effective compounds achieve blockbuster access because the team behind them understood precisely what a payer needed to say yes. Market access is not a tax on innovation. It is the final scientific discipline in the chain that runs from target identification to patient benefit. Get it right, and your drug reaches the people it was designed to help. Get it wrong, and no one will ever know what it could have done." — The Author, reflecting on 40 years in pharmaceutical development and commercialization

Chapter 8: Key Takeaways

  • Market access is a strategic function that must be embedded from IND, not activated post-approval. Clinical trial design, endpoint selection, comparator choice, and PRO instrument inclusion are all market access decisions.
  • HTA bodies differ substantially in methodology, comparator expectations, and evidence standards. A one-size-fits-all submission is never optimal; build a global value dossier and adapt rigorously for each market.
  • The ICER (cost per QALY) is the central metric of health economic evaluation. Know the WTP threshold in every target market and design the evidence strategy to deliver an ICER below it, or build the qualitative case for an exception.
  • Germany's AMNOG system creates a global pricing trap: a low AMNOG-negotiated price cascades via IRP to dozens of other markets. G-BA strategy is global pricing strategy.
  • The US pricing architecture—WAC, net price, rebates, best price, WAC/ASP/340B pricing—is a system of extraordinary complexity where net prices average 45% below list. The IRA has permanently altered the incentive structure for small molecule development and Medicare price-setting.
  • PBMs control formulary access for 270 million Americans. Preferred formulary placement is not optional for commercial success; it must be negotiated through rebate structures, outcomes-based agreements, or clinical differentiation evidence compelling enough to require unrestricted access.
  • Rare disease access requires special pathways (NICE HST, Germany orphan exception, France AAP), modified WTP thresholds, and innovative payment models (annuity, outcomes-based) for ultra-high-cost therapies.
  • HEOR is the quantitative language of access. Invest in it early, design economic models for HTA transparency, and never mistake a positive clinical trial for a guaranteed access story.
