Science, Evidence, and the Bridge to Practice — How pharmaceutical companies translate clinical data into real-world medical understanding

Expert Committee — Medical Affairs Leadership Perspectives

Contributors and perspectives drawn from: Former Chief Medical Officers and Medical Affairs VPs at AbbVie, Bristol Myers Squibb, and Pfizer, as well as senior medical directors at Roche, Novo Nordisk, and AstraZeneca. Their collective experience spans oncology, immunology, cardiometabolic disease, rare diseases, and CNS — representing over 150 years of combined medical affairs practice across both US and global organizations.

"Medical Affairs is not the conscience of the commercial organization — it is the scientific identity of the company expressed in the medical community. Get that wrong, and you lose credibility that takes a decade to rebuild." — Former Chief Medical Officer, AbbVie

"The best MSL I ever managed could walk into a tumor board at a top academic center and add value to the clinical conversation without ever mentioning a brand name. That is the standard." — VP Medical Affairs, Bristol Myers Squibb (Oncology)

"In the post-COVID era, the question is no longer whether digital engagement works for MSLs — it is how you blend it with in-person scientific exchange to deepen rather than replace the relationship." — VP Global Medical Affairs, Pfizer

Medical Affairs occupies a singular position in the pharmaceutical value chain: it is the only function that is simultaneously scientific and commercial, internally facing and externally facing, pre-approval and post-approval, regulated and judgment-driven. It is the function that a regulator expects to be rigorously independent, that a payer expects to be evidence-rich, that a physician expects to be scientifically credible, and that a CFO expects to demonstrate return on investment. Navigating those tensions is not a design flaw — it is the entire point.

After four decades working inside and advising some of the world's largest pharmaceutical companies, I have watched Medical Affairs evolve from a small department of physicians answering physician letters into a sophisticated, data-generating, evidence-orchestrating function that can determine the commercial fate of a product as decisively as any sales force. The companies that understand this invest accordingly. The ones that treat Medical Affairs as a compliance firewall between sales and physicians eventually pay a steep price — in lost access, damaged KOL relationships, and clinical adoption curves that never achieve their potential.

The Medical Affairs Function: Mission, Mandate, and Organizational Design

The formal mission of Medical Affairs, as articulated in most company charters, is some variant of: "to ensure that scientific data and clinical evidence are communicated accurately, completely, and compliantly to the medical and scientific community, and that external medical insights are integrated into the company's scientific strategy." That mission statement is accurate but insufficient. The real mission is to build and sustain scientific credibility that no commercial activity can manufacture.

Medical Affairs functions typically encompass four broad capability clusters: field medical (MSLs and field-based medical directors), scientific communications (publications, congresses, CME), evidence generation (IITs, RWE studies, registries), and medical information (response services, label interpretation). Depending on company size and therapeutic area, HEOR and health outcomes teams may sit within Medical Affairs or adjacent to it in a Market Access structure.

Reporting Lines: The CMO vs. Commercial Debate

Few organizational questions generate more internal debate than where Medical Affairs should report. The three dominant models are: reporting to the Chief Medical Officer (CMO), reporting to the Chief Commercial Officer (CCO), or operating as a standalone Medical Affairs organization with direct CEO reporting. Each has defenders and each has its failure modes.

The CMO model, prevalent at companies like Roche and Novartis, preserves scientific independence and tends to produce stronger physician credibility. Its weakness is that medical teams can become disconnected from commercial timelines, producing beautiful evidence that arrives two years after the formulary decisions were made. The CCO model, more common at companies with strong US commercial cultures like AbbVie in certain business units, produces tighter commercial alignment but creates real compliance risk when the boundary between promotion and medical exchange becomes ambiguous. The hybrid model — a Chief Medical Officer with matrix accountability to commercial for certain activities — is increasingly the preferred architecture at large, diversified companies like Pfizer and J&J, precisely because it allows context-dependent governance.

My strong view, earned through working inside all three models: Medical Affairs must have ultimate scientific governance independence. If an MSL cannot decline a request from a sales director without fear of career consequence, the entire system is compromised. That independence has to be structural, not just policy.

Medical Science Liaisons: The Field Face of Medical Affairs

The Medical Science Liaison (MSL) role is the single most discussed, most debated, and most misunderstood position in pharmaceutical medicine. Created at Eli Lilly in 1967 as a field-based PhD scientist who could engage academic physicians at a level beyond what sales representatives could, the MSL has evolved into a sophisticated scientific communicator, evidence gatherer, clinical educator, and relationship manager — all rolled into one person who works largely autonomously, often from a home office, covering thousands of square miles of territory.

The best MSLs I have worked with shared a common profile: deep therapeutic area expertise (typically a PharmD, MD, or PhD), genuine intellectual curiosity about the science, comfort with ambiguity, and the interpersonal intelligence to know when to talk and when to listen. That last quality is underrated. An MSL who spends a physician encounter presenting slide decks is a sales representative with better credentials. An MSL who spends the same encounter asking insightful questions and genuinely learning about the physician's clinical challenges is building the kind of relationship that compounds over years into meaningful scientific partnership.

Territory Management and Call Planning

MSL territory design balances scientific priority (concentration of relevant KOLs and high-volume prescribers) with geographic practicability. In oncology, where high-volume academic cancer centers drive a disproportionate share of prescribing behavior, territories may be account-based rather than geographically drawn — an MSL may cover five major cancer centers across three states. In primary care or broad-indication immunology, territories are more classically geographic, typically covering 150–300 target healthcare providers.

Call planning for MSLs is fundamentally different from sales call planning. MSLs are reactive to physician-initiated contact as a primary priority (responding to scientific inquiries), and proactive in the context of scientific exchange rather than promotional messaging. Frequency targets should be set not as floors but as appropriate engagement cadences informed by scientific activity — a KOL who is running a company-sponsored clinical trial may warrant monthly contact; a community rheumatologist with scientific interest in a disease area may warrant quarterly contact; a physician who has never expressed interest in engagement should not be contacted at all.

Key Opinion Leader Strategy: Identification, Tiering, and Engagement

The term "Key Opinion Leader" has acquired something of a pejorative connotation in public discourse — the image of a pharmaceutical company paying physicians to promote products under the guise of scientific exchange. That abuse is real, documented in government settlements with multiple companies, and every serious medical affairs professional should understand it as a cautionary failure mode. But the legitimate concept — that certain physicians shape clinical practice through their research, teaching, publications, and clinical reputation — is not only real but essential to how medical science propagates through a healthcare system.

The identification of KOLs has become increasingly systematic and data-driven. Publication analysis (h-index, citation counts, disease-specific publication volume), conference speaking frequency (ASCO, ASH, ACC, ACR, ADA, etc.), clinical trial principal investigator activity, guideline committee membership, and medical society leadership positions all contribute to an objective KOL profile. Several commercial data providers — Veeva Link, Definitive Healthcare, and similar — aggregate these signals into vendor-managed KOL databases that MSL teams use as a starting point. But those databases are a floor, not a ceiling. Regional KOLs who are highly influential within a specific health system or geographic community may have modest publication records but outsized influence on local prescribing patterns — and the MSL's local intelligence is irreplaceable in identifying them.

KOL Tiering

Most companies use a three- or four-tier KOL classification system. Tier 1 (Global or National KOLs) are typically fewer than 50 per therapeutic area globally — the individuals who publish landmark trials, chair guideline committees, and speak at the plenary sessions of major international congresses. Tier 2 (Regional KOLs) number in the hundreds — academic and community physicians who present at regional conferences, train fellows, and whose opinions carry significant weight within a health system or metropolitan area. Tier 3 (Emerging KOLs) are early-career investigators or community leaders with growing influence. Tier 4 (Community Champions) are high-volume prescribers in community practice who are respected locally but not on the academic circuit.

Engagement plans differ by tier. Tier 1 KOLs typically have direct relationships with medical directors and VP-level medical affairs leaders, serve on advisory boards and steering committees for clinical trials, and are engaged at the most senior scientific level. Tier 2 KOLs are primary MSL territory — the heart of the field medical relationship model. Tier 3 KOLs are development investments: the company identifies them early, supports their research (often through IITs or scientific grants), and cultivates a long-term relationship as their influence grows.

"A KOL relationship built solely on speaker bureau activity is not a relationship — it is a transaction. The physician knows it, the company knows it, and the medical community knows it. Genuine KOL engagement is built on two-way scientific exchange where the physician's insights genuinely influence the company's scientific strategy. That requires humility on the company's part that is not always easy to find." — Senior Medical Director, Immunology, AbbVie (retired)

Advisory Boards: Design, Governance, and Compliance

Pharmaceutical advisory boards — both Medical Advisory Boards (MABs) and Scientific Advisory Boards (SABs) — serve as structured forums for gathering expert external opinion on scientific, clinical, and strategic questions. When designed and governed well, they produce genuine insights that improve clinical development strategies, evidence generation plans, and medical education programs. When designed poorly, they are paid promotional events wearing scientific clothing, and they expose the company to significant regulatory and reputational risk.

Medical Advisory Boards (MABs)

MABs typically focus on clinical and therapeutic area questions directly relevant to current marketed products or late-stage pipeline assets. Common MAB topics include clinical practice patterns in a disease area, identification of unmet needs in the label population, feedback on case study-based clinical scenarios, or review of real-world evidence findings. MABs are typically convened twice a year per product and range in size from 8 to 20 participants. They require a clear scientific agenda published to participants in advance, an independent chairperson where possible, minutes or summary reports, and a documented record of how the advisory board input influenced company decision-making.

Scientific Advisory Boards (SABs)

SABs operate at a higher level of scientific abstraction, typically reviewing data packages, clinical development strategies, or translational science questions. They tend to be smaller (6–12 participants), more tightly focused, and often involve pre-clinical or early-clinical data that requires confidentiality protections. SABs for oncology and rare disease programs frequently include basic scientists and geneticists alongside clinical physicians. Compensation for SAB participation must reflect fair market value for the expertise and time provided — typically determined by an independent third-party compensation analysis — and all agreements must comply with the PhRMA Code, OIG guidance, and applicable Anti-Kickback Statute requirements.

Advisory Board Governance Requirements

Every advisory board, regardless of type, should be governed by a standard operating procedure that addresses: documented scientific rationale for convening (the "need statement"), venue selection criteria (appropriate, not extravagant), participant selection justification (expertise-based, not prescriber volume-based), independent legal review of all materials, fair market value compensation, compliance pre-approval, and a post-meeting report documenting insights received and intended uses. The compliance pre-approval step is non-negotiable — it is the single most effective structural safeguard against advisory boards drifting into promotional activity.

Scientific Publications Strategy

The publications strategy is, in my view, one of the most consequential and most underinvested capabilities in pharmaceutical medical affairs. A well-executed publications plan converts clinical trial data — which the company has spent hundreds of millions of dollars generating — into the permanent scientific record that shapes clinical practice, guideline decisions, payer evaluations, and physician education for a decade or more. A poorly executed publications plan allows that data to be published late, fragmented, buried in lower-tier journals, or undermined by competitor narrative. The difference in product adoption curves can be measured in billions of dollars.

The Publications Plan

A publications plan is a strategic document — typically developed 18–24 months before an anticipated regulatory submission — that maps all existing and anticipated data assets to planned publications, target journals, author assignments, and publication timelines. It distinguishes primary publications (landmark trial results, including the primary endpoint publication in a high-impact journal), secondary publications (subgroup analyses, secondary endpoints, long-term follow-up), and ancillary publications (patient-reported outcomes, pharmacoeconomic analyses, real-world evidence). The plan should be developed collaboratively with clinical investigators, who have academic independence and reputational interests, and governed by a publications steering committee with representation from medical affairs, clinical development, and the principal investigators.

Author Order, Embargo, and the Ghost-Writing Problem

Ghost-writing — where company employees or contracted medical writers draft manuscripts attributed solely to physician authors — is the most damaging practice in pharmaceutical publishing, and it is not extinct. The ICMJE authorship criteria require that each listed author has made substantial contributions to conception or design, data acquisition or analysis, manuscript drafting or critical revision, AND has approved the final version AND is accountable for the work. Company medical writers who contribute substantially to drafting should be listed as contributors. Acknowledgment of editorial assistance from commercial medical communications agencies is required when it occurs.

Embargo management is a genuine operational challenge. Clinical trial results are typically presented as late-breaking trials at major congresses before formal journal publication. The embargo — the period between congress presentation and journal publication — must be carefully managed to ensure that congress presentation data aligns precisely with publication data, that no promotional activity references unpublished embargo-period data, and that no material scientific information is selectively communicated to specific audiences during the embargo window.

Medical Education: CME, Symposia, and Congress Strategy

Continuing Medical Education (CME) represents a regulated intersection of medical affairs, medical education, and compliance. The Accreditation Council for Continuing Medical Education (ACCME) in the United States maintains strict standards for commercial support of CME that prohibit pharmaceutical companies from controlling CME content or selecting faculty for commercially supported educational programs. These standards exist for good reason: commercially controlled education is promotion, not education, and the medical community has historically been slow to distinguish between them.

Pharmaceutical companies may provide independent educational grants (IEGs) to accredited CME providers — medical schools, specialty societies, professional education companies — to support the development and delivery of certified CME programs. The critical compliance requirements are that the grant is unrestricted (the company cannot control topic selection, faculty, or content), that it is supported by a documented educational need assessment, and that all commercial support is disclosed to participants.

Medical Congress Strategy

The major disease-area medical congresses — ASCO, ASH, ESMO, ADA, AHA, ACC, EULAR, EASD, ECTRIMS, and their equivalents — are the primary venues for scientific data dissemination and medical affairs engagement. An effective congress strategy orchestrates three distinct streams of activity: data presentation (abstract submission, late-breaking trial applications, poster presentations), education (symposia, meet-the-expert sessions, CME programming), and field medical engagement (pre-planned KOL meetings, advisory board activities, booth scientific exchange).

Congress planning begins 18–24 months in advance for major congresses. The medical affairs congress team typically owns the scientific program, advisory board logistics, and HCP engagement activities, while brand teams own the exhibit hall presence. The most critical failure mode in congress planning is allowing commercial priorities to override scientific integrity in session design — a symposium agenda shaped by what the brand team wants to say rather than what the scientific community needs to hear will be recognized as promotional and attended poorly by serious physicians.

Investigator-Initiated Trials: Science, Budget, and Governance

Investigator-Initiated Trials (IITs) — also called Independent Medical Research (IMR) programs or Independent Investigator-Sponsored Studies (ISS) — are clinical or translational studies proposed and conducted by external investigators using a company's product, funded in whole or in part by the company but under the investigator's IND. They are among the most strategically valuable and compliance-sensitive activities in the medical affairs portfolio.

The strategic value of IITs is considerable. A well-selected IIT portfolio can generate evidence in disease areas or patient populations not addressed in the company's own clinical program, explore combination regimens or novel administration strategies that the company cannot pursue itself, establish the company's product in the clinical research culture of a key institution, and develop the next generation of clinical investigators who will design future pivotal trials. At their best, IITs are genuine scientific partnerships between a company and an independent investigator with aligned scientific interests.

The IIT Review and Governance Process

Most mid-to-large pharmaceutical companies manage IIT programs through a formal review committee — often called the Independent Research Committee (IRC) or similar. The committee typically includes medical affairs, clinical operations, legal, and regulatory representatives, and evaluates proposals against criteria including scientific merit, feasibility, safety profile, regulatory complexity, and strategic alignment. Importantly, commercial rationale — whether the IIT addresses a topic favorable to product promotion — should not be a primary selection criterion; that is the difference between a legitimate scientific program and a disguised promotional activity.

Medical Information: Responding to the Scientific Community

Medical Information (MI) — the function responsible for responding to unsolicited medical inquiries from healthcare professionals, patients, and caregivers — is one of the oldest and most regulated activities in pharmaceutical medical affairs. The core legal and ethical principle governing MI is the distinction between unsolicited and solicited communication. When a physician calls a company's medical information line and asks a specific question about a drug — including questions about off-label uses — the company may respond with truthful, accurate, balanced information. When a company proactively communicates off-label information without a specific inquiry, that is promotion, and it is illegal under FDA regulations.

The unsolicited inquiry standard sounds simple, but its implementation is genuinely complex. What happens when an MSL is in a physician's office for a different scientific discussion and the physician asks an off-label question? The answer depends on whether the inquiry is genuinely spontaneous and unsolicited, whether the MSL is trained to respond appropriately (and most are not trained to provide on-the-spot responses to complex off-label inquiries — that should be routed to the MI center), and whether the response is documented and compliant. Most companies train MSLs to acknowledge the question, offer to have the MI center provide a complete formal response, and document the inquiry appropriately.

The Medical Information Response Library

Large pharmaceutical companies maintain response libraries — pre-approved medical information letters (MILs) or standard response documents (SRDs) — that cover the most commonly asked questions about their products, including both labeled uses and frequently asked off-label topics. These documents are reviewed by medical affairs, regulatory, and legal, and typically expire on a 12- or 24-month review cycle to ensure they reflect current data. The development and maintenance of the MI response library is a significant operational undertaking that is often underestimated in resource planning.

Integrated Evidence Planning: Aligning All Evidence Streams

Integrated Evidence Planning (IEP) is the strategic discipline of aligning clinical trial evidence, health economics and outcomes research (HEOR), real-world evidence (RWE), and medical affairs-generated evidence into a coherent, non-duplicative, mutually reinforcing evidence package that addresses the needs of all key stakeholders — regulators, payers, physicians, patients, and health technology assessment bodies.

The concept is simple; the execution is hard. It requires that clinical development, HEOR, medical affairs, market access, and commercial functions agree — very early, typically at Phase II — on the priority questions that each stakeholder audience needs answered, the evidence type best suited to answer each question, and the timeline implications of building that evidence. In most organizations, these functions have historically operated in relative isolation, producing evidence for their own purposes without sufficient cross-functional coordination. The result is predictable: a regulatory package that satisfies the FDA but leaves payers with unanswered cost-effectiveness questions, a medical education strategy built on Phase III data that does not address the real-world patient population where the drug is being used, and HEOR models built without access to the clinical data assumptions they need.

Real-World Evidence in Medical Affairs: Beyond the Clinical Trial

The clinical randomized controlled trial is the gold standard for establishing efficacy and safety, but it answers a narrow question: does the drug work better than control in a carefully selected, protocol-specified patient population under idealized conditions? The question that physicians, payers, and health systems actually face is different: does the drug work in my patients — the ones with comorbidities, the ones who take it inconsistently, the ones in the 80th percentile of age excluded from most pivotal trials, the ones whose insurance formulary places a competing agent at lower cost-share?

Real-world evidence, generated from claims databases, electronic health record data, disease registries, and other observational sources, is the evidence type that speaks to that question. For medical affairs, RWE serves multiple strategic purposes: demonstrating effectiveness in broader patient populations than clinical trial eligibility criteria allowed, characterizing patterns of care and identifying unmet needs in the treated population, supporting payer negotiations with real-world cost-effectiveness evidence, and generating comparative effectiveness data in the absence of head-to-head trials.

The major data sources available to medical affairs RWE programs in the United States include: administrative claims databases (IBM MarketScan, IQVIA PharMetrics Plus, Optum Clinformatics — representing 50–150 million covered lives), EHR networks (Flatiron Health for oncology, TriNetX, PCORnet), and disease-specific registries (SEER for cancer, Surveillance Epidemiology and End Results; OCEANS, FORWARD, and similar condition-specific registries). Globally, the Nordic countries' population-linked registry systems (Denmark's CPR-linked registries, Sweden's National Patient Register) offer some of the richest RWE environments in the world due to universal healthcare and population-level data linkage.

RWE Methodological Standards in Medical Affairs

Not all real-world evidence is created equal, and the medical community has become increasingly sophisticated in evaluating RWE quality. Medical affairs teams commissioning RWE studies should adhere to STROBE (STrengthening the Reporting of OBservational Studies in Epidemiology) guidelines for publication, RECORD (REporting of studies Conducted using Observational Routinely-collected health Data) standards for claims-based studies, and ISPOR's good practices for real-world data and real-world evidence. Methodological transparency — pre-specification of research questions, analytical methods, and outcomes before data extraction — is the single most important marker of credible RWE and should be standard practice.

Medical Affairs in Rare Diseases vs. Common Diseases: Different Worlds

The rare disease medical affairs model is fundamentally different from the common disease model, and companies that try to apply common disease playbooks to rare disease products pay a steep operational and credibility price. Understanding these differences is essential for anyone designing or evaluating a medical affairs organization.

In rare diseases — defined in the United States as conditions affecting fewer than 200,000 individuals — the physician universe is tiny, often consisting of fewer than 300–500 specialists globally who treat a given condition. The MSL team may number 10–15 people globally rather than hundreds. Every KOL relationship is deeply personal and long-term. The advisory board is often the same group of 12–15 investigators who have run all the natural history studies, run the pivotal trial, and will write the treatment guidelines. The medical information needs are highly complex, often involving genetic counseling, newborn screening interpretation, and biosynthesis pathway questions that require PhD-level expertise. Publications matter enormously in rare disease — a single case report in the Journal of Inherited Metabolic Disease can change practice if it identifies a novel phenotype or treatment response.

In common diseases — cardiovascular disease, type 2 diabetes, major depression, COPD, osteoarthritis — the physician universe may be tens of thousands of prescribers. The MSL team is correspondingly large. KOL relationships are more formal and more bounded. The publications portfolio is vast, competing for attention in high-traffic disease areas. The medical information center handles thousands of calls monthly. Advisory boards are carefully tiered to manage a large community. The evidence generation challenge is not scarcity of data but standing out in a crowded evidentiary environment where ten other products have already generated substantial evidence bases.

Expanded Access and Compassionate Use Programs

Expanded access — the provision of investigational drugs outside the context of a clinical trial to patients with serious or life-threatening conditions who have no satisfactory alternative — is one of the most ethically complex and operationally demanding activities in medical affairs. The FDA's expanded access regulations (21 CFR 312, Subpart I) distinguish between individual patient access (including emergency use), intermediate-size patient populations, and widespread treatment IND (used for large-scale pre-approval programs). The European framework uses the term "compassionate use" and is governed at the member-state level, creating significant variation in process and timelines across the EU.

Named patient programs (NPPs) — country-specific mechanisms that allow physicians to request investigational or unapproved products for individual identified patients — represent the most common global expanded access pathway. Managing a named patient program for a product in late Phase III development requires a dedicated operational infrastructure: a medical affairs or medical information team to evaluate requests, a clinical operations group to manage supply logistics, a regulatory team to navigate country-specific approvals, a safety team to collect adverse event data, and a legal team to manage liability exposure. Companies that underestimate this operational burden when designing pre-launch expanded access strategies routinely find themselves overwhelmed — and denying access to patients who have legitimate clinical needs — because the infrastructure was not in place.

Global Medical Affairs: Spanning Borders and Regulatory Jurisdictions

Global Medical Affairs introduces a layer of complexity that purely domestic medical affairs organizations never encounter: the simultaneous management of different regulatory timelines, different prescribing landscapes, different KOL ecosystems, and different compliance environments across dozens of countries. A product that has received FDA approval may still be under EMA review in Europe, unapproved but accessed via named patient programs in Japan, and subject to entirely different reimbursement evidence requirements in Germany (where AMNOG early benefit assessment drives pricing), France (where the HAS ASMR rating governs reimbursement level), and the UK (where NICE technology appraisals determine NHS access).

International Medical Information Bureau (IIIB) and Country Medical Director Model

The International Information Bureau (IIB), sometimes called the International Medical Information Bureau or Global Medical Affairs Operations center, is the hub-and-spoke infrastructure that most large pharmaceutical companies use to manage global medical information consistency while accommodating country-specific regulatory requirements. The IIB maintains the global standard response documents and data packages; country medical affairs teams adapt these to local regulatory requirements and languages, and are responsible for local compliance review before dissemination.

The Country Medical Director (CMD) model places a senior physician or PharmD in each major market as the accountable medical affairs leader for that country. The CMD typically manages local MSLs, oversees local advisory boards and medical education, serves as the primary interface between the global medical affairs organization and the local affiliate, and is responsible for ensuring that all medical affairs activities in the country comply with local regulations and the PhRMA Code equivalent (EFPIA Code in Europe, JPMA Code in Japan, etc.). The CMD role requires an unusual combination of scientific credibility, organizational leadership, business acumen, and regulatory literacy — and good CMDs are among the most valuable and difficult-to-replace assets in a pharmaceutical company.

Compliance in Medical Affairs: PhRMA Code, OIG Guidance, and the Boundary of Promotion

Medical Affairs operates closer to the promotional boundary than any other non-commercial function, which is precisely why compliance infrastructure in medical affairs must be more sophisticated, not less, than in purely commercial functions. The regulatory and legal framework governing medical affairs activities in the United States includes: the FDA's regulations on promotion of prescription drugs (21 CFR Parts 201, 202), the Anti-Kickback Statute (42 USC 1320a-7b(b)) and its safe harbors, the False Claims Act, the PhRMA Code on Interactions with Healthcare Professionals (updated 2022), and the Office of Inspector General's Compliance Program Guidance for Pharmaceutical Manufacturers (2003) and subsequent advisory opinions.

The core compliance principles that govern medical affairs activities are: scientific exchange must be non-promotional; financial relationships with HCPs must reflect fair market value for legitimate services; company-funded educational activities must be genuinely educational; and off-label information may only be shared in response to unsolicited inquiries. These principles are well-established, but their application to specific situations requires ongoing judgment and compliance counsel. The proliferation of digital engagement channels — scientific exchange via Zoom, Twitter/X scientific discussions, LinkedIn posts by MSLs — has created a new set of interpretation questions that compliance teams are still working through.

Medical Affairs Metrics: Demonstrating Value Through Data

The historical resistance of Medical Affairs to metrics and measurement — grounded in a legitimate concern that reducing scientific relationships to counts would corrupt the relationships — has given way to a more mature understanding: without demonstrating value through data, Medical Affairs budgets are the first cut in difficult fiscal years. The challenge is designing a KPI framework that captures genuine scientific and business value without incentivizing gaming the metrics.

The Metrics Trap: Activity vs. Impact

The most common medical affairs metrics failure is measuring activity rather than impact. An MSL who conducts 12 interactions per week but delivers no scientific value in any of them is worse than an MSL who conducts 4 deeply impactful scientific exchanges. The KPI framework above attempts to balance activity metrics (which are leadings indicators of coverage) with quality and outcome metrics (which are lagging indicators of genuine value). The judgment call of when an interaction has "scientific value" remains partially subjective — which is why qualitative HCP feedback mechanisms, such as periodic structured interviews with KOLs about the quality of scientific exchange with the company's medical team, are as important as any quantitative KPI.

The Medical Affairs Organization of the Future

The medical affairs organization of the next decade will look recognizably similar to today's in its core mission and ethical foundations — scientific credibility, independent evidence generation, compliant HCP engagement — but dramatically different in its tools, talent requirements, and engagement models.

Digital and Hybrid MSL Engagement

COVID-19 forced an accelerated, involuntary experiment in digital MSL engagement. The results were more nuanced than either enthusiasts or skeptics predicted. Virtual scientific exchange worked well for certain interaction types — sharing published data, discussing ongoing clinical trial enrollment, providing a formal medical information response, facilitating a virtual advisory board. It worked poorly for others — building initial relationships, conducting clinical case discussions that benefit from shared patient chart review, participating in tumor board discussions. The resulting model — hybrid engagement, with digital used where it adds convenience without sacrificing depth, and in-person preserved for high-value relationship-building moments — has become the emerging standard at companies including Novartis, Pfizer, and Roche.

Remote-engagement MSL models — where an MSL handles multiple virtual territories from a centralized location — have emerged as a legitimate staffing complement for lower-priority segments or geographic areas where in-person MSL deployment is not cost-effective. These roles require different competencies (virtual presentation skills, digital relationship management) and different territory management approaches, but they represent a genuine capability expansion rather than a compromise.

AI-Assisted Medical Information

Artificial intelligence applications in medical information are the near-term frontier of medical affairs technology. The use cases range from conversational AI for initial inquiry triage (routing inquiries to appropriate pre-approved standard responses, or to a human specialist for complex inquiries) to AI-assisted literature review for response document development, to real-time identification of medical information trends that may signal emerging safety questions or off-label use patterns.

The compliance implications of AI in medical information are still being worked out by FDA, FTC, and the industry itself. The core question — when an AI system provides a response to a physician's inquiry, who is responsible for the accuracy and completeness of that response, and does the response constitute promotion if it is not in response to a genuinely unsolicited inquiry — does not yet have settled regulatory answers. Companies deploying AI in medical information should do so with robust human oversight, comprehensive validation testing, and proactive engagement with regulatory counsel about applicable requirements.

MSL Talent and the Scientific Generalist vs. Specialist Debate

The perennial debate about whether MSLs should be deep disease-area specialists (who bring irreplaceable expertise but are expensive and territorially inflexible) or broad scientific generalists (who can cover multiple indications and adapt to portfolio changes but lack the depth to engage top-tier academic KOLs) is unlikely to be resolved by a single model. The emerging consensus — visible in the talent architectures at companies like AbbVie, BMS, and Genentech/Roche — is a tiered model: deeply specialized senior MSLs (often with PhDs or MDs and 5–10 years of disease-area research experience) managing Tier 1 and Tier 2 KOL relationships in high-priority accounts, complemented by scientifically capable generalists (typically PharmDs with strong clinical training) managing the broader HCP universe in the same territory.

"The medical affairs function that will be most valued in 2030 is not the one with the most MSLs or the biggest IIT budget. It is the one that has established genuine scientific credibility with the medical community — the kind of credibility where a physician reaches out to the MSL because they trust that person's scientific judgment, not because they need a speaker honorarium. That takes years to build and can be destroyed in a single promotional incident." — Former Global Medical Affairs VP, Novartis Oncology

Chapter Summary: The Medical Affairs Imperative

Medical Affairs is not a support function. At its best, it is the mechanism by which a pharmaceutical company earns and sustains the scientific trust of the medical community — and that trust, once established, is among the most durable competitive advantages a company can possess. A physician who trusts that the company's MSL will give them accurate, balanced, scientifically grounded information regardless of whether that information favors the company's product is a physician who will call that MSL when they have a complex patient case, when they are thinking about participating in a clinical trial, when they are writing a guideline, and when they are deciding how to talk about the product with their colleagues.

Building that trust requires: organizational independence from commercial pressure that is structural, not aspirational; scientific rigor in every publication, advisory board, and CME activity; genuine respect for the physician's time and intelligence that manifests in concise, accurate, balanced scientific exchange; and compliance standards enforced not as bureaucratic obligation but as an expression of the company's scientific values. When these elements are in place, Medical Affairs is not a bridge between science and practice — it is a living demonstration that the bridge is safe to cross.

The chapters that follow examine how the evidence and relationships built by Medical Affairs translate into commercial adoption through the Commercial Excellence function, and how the overall business performance of pharmaceutical products is managed across the product lifecycle. But the foundation for sustainable commercial success — the scientific credibility of the company's evidence and the trust of the physician community in the company's medical engagement — is built here, in Medical Affairs.
